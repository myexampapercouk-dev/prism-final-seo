Website Builder vs Custom Website: How UK Businesses Can Choose

Squarespace, Wix, Shopify, WordPress or a custom build? An honest comparison of control, cost of ownership, flexibility, SEO and growth for UK businesses.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Hosted builders trade flexibility for simplicity; self-hosted and custom sites trade simplicity for control.
  • The right choice depends on what the site must do in two or three years, not just at launch.
  • Whatever you choose, make sure you can export your content and keep your domain.

One of the first decisions in any website project is what to build it on. Hosted website builders promise a site in an afternoon; developers talk about flexibility and control. Both are right, for different businesses. The mistake is choosing on launch-day convenience alone, then discovering a year later that the platform cannot do what the business now needs.

Prism Works Studio is a UK web design, ecommerce and digital marketing agency working remotely with businesses across the country. This comparison is general and our view; platforms change their features often, so check current details before deciding.

The main options

OptionWhat it isTypically suits
Hosted builders such as Squarespace and WixAll-in-one platforms where hosting, design tools and updates are handled for youSimple brochure sites, portfolios, early-stage businesses
ShopifyA hosted platform built specifically for selling onlineProduct businesses of most sizes
WordPress (self-hosted)Open-source software you install on hosting you chooseContent-heavy sites, service businesses wanting flexibility
Custom buildA site developed specifically for your requirementsUnusual functionality, complex integrations, specific performance needs

Where hosted builders shine

  • Speed to launch. Templates and drag-and-drop editors make it possible to publish quickly.
  • Low maintenance. Security, hosting and software updates are the platform's job.
  • Predictable running costs. A subscription covers most of what you need.
  • Easy editing. Non-technical owners can usually change text and images themselves.

Where builders can hold you back

  • Design and functionality are limited to what the platform allows
  • Moving to another platform later can mean rebuilding rather than exporting cleanly
  • Some technical SEO settings and performance tweaks may be restricted
  • Adding many third-party apps can raise costs and slow pages
  • You are dependent on the platform's pricing and policy decisions

If you are already feeling these limits, our Wix to WordPress migration guide covers what moving involves.

Shopify: a builder with a specialism

Shopify sits between the two worlds. It is hosted and handles the technical basics, but it is built around selling, with mature checkout, inventory and app support. For product businesses it is often the sensible default. Content-heavy sites with only a few products, or service businesses with no shop at all, may find it less natural. Our ecommerce sector page has more on how we approach online shops.

WordPress: flexibility with responsibility

Self-hosted WordPress gives you a huge choice of themes and plugins, full control over content and structure, and the ability to move hosts. The trade-off is responsibility: someone needs to keep the core software, theme and plugins updated, manage backups and choose decent hosting. Neglected WordPress sites become slow and vulnerable. Our guides to WordPress for small businesses and WordPress hosting explain what good looks like.

Custom builds

A custom site makes sense when off-the-shelf tools genuinely cannot do the job: booking systems with unusual rules, client portals, deep integrations with internal systems or very specific performance requirements. It offers the most freedom but usually needs ongoing developer involvement for changes. Be wary of choosing custom just for the sake of it.

Think about total cost of ownership

The cheapest option at launch is not always the cheapest over three years. When comparing, consider:

  • Subscription or hosting fees
  • Paid themes, apps, plugins and their renewals
  • Transaction or payment fees for online shops
  • Time spent on maintenance, or the cost of paying someone
  • The cost of rebuilding if you outgrow the platform

For budgeting, see our WordPress website cost guide, Shopify website cost guide and pricing page.

What about SEO?

All of the major platforms can support sites that rank well. Content quality, structure, speed and links usually matter more than the platform itself. That said, platforms differ in how much control they give over URLs, redirects, structured data and performance, which matters more as a site grows. Our technical SEO audit checklist shows the kinds of things worth being able to control.

Questions to help you decide

  1. Will you sell products online, now or soon?
  2. Who will update the site, and how technical are they?
  3. How much content do you plan to publish?
  4. Do you need integrations with booking, CRM or stock systems?
  5. Who will handle updates, security and backups?
  6. What happens if you want to move platform in a few years?

Three example scenarios

These are illustrations rather than real clients, to show how the questions above play out.

A sole trader electrician who needs a few pages explaining services, the areas covered and a contact form, and who wants to update it occasionally. A hosted builder or a lean WordPress site would both be reasonable. The deciding factor is often who will maintain it.

A homeware brand selling a growing range of products, with plans for wholesale customers and email marketing. Shopify is usually the natural fit here, because the shop is the business and the platform is built for it.

A consultancy publishing regular articles, case studies and team profiles, with enquiries routed into a CRM. Self-hosted WordPress often suits this kind of content-led site, provided someone looks after updates and hosting.

A business with unusual requirements, such as a booking system with complex availability rules tied into internal software, might be the one where a custom build genuinely earns its cost.

Editing experience and day-to-day use

The person who will use the site most after launch is often not the person who chose the platform. Before committing, ask for a look at the editing screens. Can your team add a blog post, change an opening time or swap a photo without help? Hosted builders tend to make this simple but restrict what can be changed. WordPress can be set up to be very straightforward, but a cluttered installation with dozens of plugins can be confusing. Shopify's admin is built around products and orders, which suits shop staff but can feel less natural for content pages. A custom site's editing experience depends entirely on how it was built, so ask to see it.

Growth and integrations

Think about the systems your website may need to talk to as the business grows: email marketing, booking tools, accounting software, stock management, a CRM or a marketplace feed. Popular platforms usually offer ready-made connections for common tools, while unusual systems may need custom work. If a particular integration is essential, check it works on the platform you are considering before you build, not after.

Security and responsibility

On hosted platforms, the provider handles the underlying security and updates, although you remain responsible for strong passwords, staff access and any apps you add. On self-hosted WordPress or a custom build, responsibility for updates, backups and monitoring sits with you or whoever you pay to look after the site. Neither approach is automatically safer; what matters is that someone is clearly responsible. Our website maintenance guide explains what ongoing care involves.

Red flags when getting advice

Be cautious if a supplier recommends one platform for every client regardless of their needs, cannot explain the ongoing costs, or proposes a setup where they control your domain and you cannot access the admin area. Equally, be wary of anyone who dismisses all builders out of hand. Good advice starts with what your business needs and explains the trade-offs honestly.

Ownership, whatever you pick

Register your domain separately in the business's name, keep admin logins in your control and check how you would export content if you ever left. These steps protect you regardless of platform.

FAQs

Is a website builder good enough for a small business?

For many simple sites, yes. If you need a few pages, a contact form and easy editing, a hosted builder can be a sensible choice. Think about whether your needs are likely to grow.

Can I move from a website builder to WordPress or Shopify later?

Usually, but it often means rebuilding the design and moving content manually, plus setting up redirects to protect search visibility.

Which platform is best for SEO?

No single platform guarantees rankings. Content, structure and speed matter most, though some platforms give more technical control than others.

Is WordPress free?

The software is free, but you pay for hosting, and often for premium themes, plugins and maintenance.

When is a custom website worth it?

When your requirements genuinely cannot be met by existing platforms and plugins, and you are prepared for ongoing development support.
