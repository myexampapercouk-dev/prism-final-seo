Accountant Website Design: Services, Pricing and Client Trust

Website design for UK accountants and bookkeepers: service pages by client type, pricing or quotes, onboarding, credentials, client portals and secure contact.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Prospective clients usually want to know quickly whether an accountant works with businesses like theirs, what it might cost and how to get started.
  • Organising services by client type, such as sole traders, limited companies or landlords, often makes a practice site easier to navigate than a list of technical services.
  • Professional body membership, regulatory status and data handling need to be described accurately, so check the rules that apply to your practice.

For many small businesses and individuals, choosing an accountant is a decision they put off until a deadline or a growing business forces it. When they finally search, they want to find someone who understands their situation, explains things plainly and doesn't feel intimidating. An accountancy website that does those things well can turn a reluctant search into an enquiry.

This guide covers website design for UK accountants, bookkeepers and tax advisers, from sole practitioners to multi-partner firms. It focuses on the website itself: structure, content and practical features. For wider promotion, such as local search, referrals and advertising, see our guide to accountant marketing. Firms offering advisory work may also find our guide to consultant website design useful.

What prospective clients want to know

Most visitors to an accountancy site are asking:

  • Do you work with people or businesses like me?
  • What exactly will you do for me?
  • Roughly how much will it cost?
  • Are you qualified and trustworthy?
  • How easy are you to work with, and how do we get started?

Design the homepage around these questions. A visitor should be able to tell within seconds who you help and how to take the next step. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise content.

Organise services by who you help

Accountants often list services in their own terms: compliance, tax returns, payroll, VAT, management accounts. Clients tend to think in terms of their own situation. Structuring the site around client types can make it far easier to navigate:

  • Sole traders and freelancers.
  • Limited companies and directors.
  • Landlords and property investors.
  • Contractors.
  • Charities or specific sectors you know well.
  • Individuals needing Self Assessment help.

Each page can then explain which services that type of client typically needs, the common questions they have and how you help. You can still keep separate pages for individual services, such as payroll or VAT returns, and link between them. Only list sectors and services you actually cover. See our guide to website navigation.

Plain language, not jargon

Accountancy is full of terms that mean little to many clients. Good website copy explains things clearly without talking down. For example, rather than just listing "year-end accounts and CT600", explain that you prepare the annual accounts and Corporation Tax return a limited company must file. Where you explain tax rules or deadlines, keep them general, date your content, and link to official HMRC or Companies House guidance, because rates and rules change. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing for clarity.

Pricing: show it or explain it

Price is one of the first things many small business clients want to know. Options include:

  • Published fixed monthly packages for common client types.
  • "From" prices with a clear explanation of what affects the final fee.
  • An online quote form that asks a few key questions, such as turnover, number of transactions and whether payroll is needed.

If you don't publish prices, explain how you set them and how quickly you will provide a quote. Whatever you show, be clear about what is included and avoid hidden extras appearing later. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout options.

Credentials and regulatory information

Trust is central to choosing an accountant. Your site should state accurately:

  • Professional body memberships held by the firm or its people, using the correct designations.
  • Whether the firm is registered or regulated for particular activities, such as audit, where applicable.
  • Anti-money laundering supervision, if you wish to show it.
  • Professional indemnity insurance details, where your professional body requires disclosure.

Professional bodies have their own rules on how membership and logos can be used and on what information must be disclosed, for example on your website or engagement letters. Check your body's current requirements. Note that "accountant" is not a protected title in the UK, while certain designations are, so be precise about qualifications. Only display memberships and accreditations you actually hold, and don't imply endorsement by any software provider unless you hold a status you can verify.

Introducing the people

Clients are choosing who they will share their finances with. An about or team page with real photos, qualifications and a little personality helps. For small practices, a personal introduction from the founder on the homepage or about page can make the firm feel approachable. Avoid stock photos of handshakes and calculators. See our guide to website trust signals.

Explaining how working together works

Many people are unsure what switching accountant or engaging one for the first time involves. A short "How it works" section or page can help, explaining in general terms:

  • How the first conversation works.
  • What information you will need from them.
  • Identity checks you need to carry out under anti-money laundering rules.
  • How you communicate and how documents are shared.
  • What happens if they are moving from another accountant.

Describe your real process, not an idealised one. Clarity here reduces anxiety and can reduce back-and-forth once someone becomes a client.

Client portals and secure document sharing

Many practices use a client portal or secure file sharing tool. The website should link to it clearly, typically from a "Client login" item in the header. Make sure clients can find it easily without it competing with calls to action for new visitors.

Avoid encouraging people to send sensitive financial documents through a basic contact form or ordinary email. State clearly how documents should be shared securely. The website should have an up-to-date privacy notice, and forms should collect only what is needed, in line with UK GDPR. See our guide to website contact forms.

Useful content and resources

Helpful articles, deadline reminders and guides can show expertise and give clients reasons to return. Topics might include key tax dates, explaining Making Tax Digital in general terms, or what records a new sole trader should keep. A few principles:

  • Date every article and review it regularly, because tax rules change.
  • Add a note that content is general information, not personal advice.
  • Link to official HMRC and Companies House sources for detail.
  • Write for your actual clients, not for other accountants.

For a firm with a defined local area, our guide to local SEO covers how location pages and a Google Business Profile can help, and our guide to professional services SEO covers the wider picture.

Booking a first call

Many prospective clients would rather book a short introductory call than fill in a long form and wait. If that suits how your practice works, an online booking tool embedded on the contact page lets people choose a time that suits them, which can be helpful for busy business owners who search in the evening. Keep the booking form brief, say how long the call lasts and whether it is free, and explain what you will cover. If you prefer to qualify enquiries first, a short form with a promise of a reply within a stated time works too. Our guide to online booking systems covers the options.

Whichever route you choose, make it visible from every page and test it on a phone. A contact page that only offers a generic email address can feel slow and impersonal compared with a clear, simple next step.

Choosing a platform

Most accountancy sites suit a content management system such as WordPress, or a hosted builder like Squarespace or Wix for very small practices. WordPress tends to offer more flexibility for structured service pages, resource libraries and integrations. Whatever you choose, make sure you can update deadlines and content easily. See our guides to website builders vs custom websites and website maintenance.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A homepage that lists technical services without saying who you help.
  • Out-of-date tax rates or deadlines left on the site.
  • Vague or misleading descriptions of qualifications.
  • No indication of pricing or how quotes work.
  • Encouraging clients to email sensitive documents insecurely.

FAQs

Should accountants show prices on their website?

Many clients appreciate it. Fixed packages, "from" prices or a quick quote form can all work, as long as it is clear what is included.

How should an accountancy website organise its services?

Grouping services by client type, such as sole traders, limited companies and landlords, often helps visitors find what applies to them.

What credentials should an accountant show on their website?

Accurate professional body memberships and any required regulatory disclosures. Check your professional body's rules on logos and wording.

Can clients upload documents through an accountant's website?

It is usually better to link to a secure client portal or file sharing system than to accept sensitive documents through a basic form.

Should an accountancy website publish tax guides?

Helpful guides can show expertise, but date them, review them regularly and make clear they are general information, not personal advice.
