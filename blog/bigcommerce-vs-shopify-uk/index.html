BigCommerce vs Shopify for UK Online Stores

BigCommerce vs Shopify for UK retailers: built-in features, apps, B2B selling, catalogue size, SEO, checkout, costs and how to decide between them.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify and BigCommerce are both hosted ecommerce platforms, so the choice is about fit rather than one being simply better.
  • BigCommerce leans towards more built-in features; Shopify leans towards a very large app and theme ecosystem.
  • Catalogue complexity, B2B needs, integrations and who will run the store day to day are usually the deciding factors.

Shopify and BigCommerce are two of the best-known hosted ecommerce platforms. Both handle hosting, security and checkout for you, both can support small shops and large retailers, and both have enterprise tiers. For UK businesses choosing between them, the decision usually comes down to how each platform approaches features, how complex the catalogue is, whether you sell to trade customers as well as consumers, and which systems the store needs to connect to.

This guide compares the two for UK retailers. Platform features and pricing change often, so treat it as a framework for your own evaluation and check current details with each platform.

What they have in common

Both are software-as-a-service platforms. You pay a monthly subscription, the platform hosts the store and keeps the software updated, and you manage products, orders and content through an admin dashboard. Both offer themes, app marketplaces, multi-currency selling, APIs for integrations and headless options for brands that want a custom front end. Either can support a professional UK store.

Philosophy: built-in features vs apps

The clearest difference is approach.

  • BigCommerce has historically included more functionality in the core platform, such as advanced product options, faceted search and some B2B features, so fewer third-party apps are needed for common requirements.
  • Shopify keeps its core focused and relies on a very large app ecosystem to extend it. Almost any feature is available somewhere, but stores can accumulate apps that each carry a cost and add code to the site.

Neither approach is wrong. Built-in features can mean lower ongoing app costs and fewer moving parts. A large ecosystem means more choice and more specialist developers familiar with the platform.

Ease of use

Shopify is widely regarded as one of the easiest platforms for store owners to learn. Its admin is clean and the everyday tasks of adding products, processing orders and editing pages are straightforward.

BigCommerce's admin offers more settings in the core platform, which can make it feel more involved at first but means more can be configured without apps. Teams with complex catalogues sometimes value that.

Catalogue and product options

For stores with complex products, such as many options, configurable items, custom fields or large SKU counts, check carefully how each platform models them. BigCommerce has long been noted for flexible product options. Shopify has expanded its variant limits and uses metafields and apps for more complex needs. Map your real catalogue onto each platform before deciding, including your most awkward products. Our guide to product variants and bundles explains the common structures.

B2B and trade selling

Many UK businesses sell to both consumers and trade customers. Both platforms offer B2B functionality, though what is included can depend on plan, particularly at the enterprise level. Features to check include customer-specific price lists, quotes, purchase orders, payment terms, account hierarchies and bulk ordering. If trade selling is central to your business, list the exact workflows you need and confirm how each platform handles them. Our B2B sector page explains how we work with trade-focused businesses.

Themes and design

Shopify has a large theme store and a big community of designers and developers, so there is a wide choice of starting points and expertise. BigCommerce also offers free and paid themes, with a smaller selection. On both, a well-chosen theme can be customised substantially, and a bespoke build is possible. Our guide to Shopify theme customisation explains what that involves on Shopify.

Checkout and payments

Shopify's checkout is familiar to many shoppers, and Shopify Payments is available in the UK. Using a third-party payment provider instead may carry additional transaction fees, so check current terms. BigCommerce works with a range of payment providers and has historically not charged its own transaction fees on top of provider fees. Payment costs at scale can make a real difference, so model them against your expected order volumes.

Checkout customisation depends on plan on both platforms, with more control at the enterprise tiers.

SEO

Both platforms cover the essentials: editable titles and descriptions, custom URLs, redirects, sitemaps and structured data via themes. Some differences exist in how URLs are structured and how much can be changed. Shopify, for example, uses fixed path prefixes such as /products/ and /collections/. In practice, content, site structure, speed and internal linking tend to matter more than the platform. Our Shopify SEO guide covers the essentials on Shopify.

Selling across channels

Both connect to Google's free listings and Shopping ads, social platforms and major marketplaces, with varying depth depending on the channel and any connectors used. If multichannel selling is important, check exactly which channels you need and how stock and orders sync. Our guide to Google Shopping ads explains the product feed side.

Integrations

Most growing retailers connect their store to other systems: accounting, inventory or ERP, warehouse and fulfilment, email marketing and customer service tools. Both platforms have APIs and integration marketplaces. Before choosing, list the systems you use or plan to use and confirm whether a reliable integration exists for each.

International selling

Many UK retailers sell into Europe, the US and beyond. Both platforms support multiple currencies and international storefronts, though the way they handle localised pricing, languages, domains and duties differs and can depend on plan. Shopify Markets brings country-specific pricing, currencies and domains together in one place. BigCommerce supports multi-storefront and multi-currency setups, with some features tied to higher plans. Selling abroad also involves customs, VAT treatment and delivery arrangements that sit outside the platform, and the rules can be complex, so take professional advice on tax. Our guide to selling internationally covers the main points.

Support and the partner ecosystem

Both platforms offer their own support channels, with more dedicated help at the enterprise level. Beyond that, consider the wider ecosystem. Shopify has a very large pool of agencies, freelancers and app developers, which can make it easier to find help and to replace a supplier if needed. BigCommerce has a smaller but established partner community. Whichever you choose, check that you can get the kind of support you need, at the times you need it, before you commit.

Costs

Comparing costs means looking beyond the headline subscription. Include:

  • The plan that actually covers your features and order volumes.
  • Payment processing and any platform transaction fees.
  • Apps or add-ons needed to match your requirements.
  • Theme costs and design or development work.
  • Ongoing support and maintenance.

Plan tiers and limits differ between the platforms and change over time, so check current pricing directly. For a sense of project costs on Shopify, see our Shopify website cost guide and our pricing page.

How to decide

Shopify may suit you if:

  • Ease of use for a small team is a priority.
  • You value a very wide choice of themes, apps and developers.
  • Your catalogue is reasonably straightforward, or can be modelled with variants and metafields.
  • You want a platform many partners and tools already support.

BigCommerce may suit you if:

  • You want more functionality built in and fewer apps to manage.
  • Your catalogue has complex options or many custom fields.
  • Trade or B2B selling is a significant part of your business.
  • Payment flexibility without platform transaction fees matters to your margins.

A practical approach is to take your five most complex requirements and test each one on both platforms, using free trials where available. If you are also considering open-source options, our Shopify vs WooCommerce comparison may help.

Switching later

Products, customers and orders can be migrated between platforms, but themes, apps and customisations do not transfer, and URL changes need careful redirects to protect search visibility. Choosing well at the start avoids an expensive move later. If you are already planning a move to Shopify's enterprise tier, our Shopify Plus migration guide covers the process.

FAQs

Is BigCommerce better than Shopify?

Neither is better for everyone. BigCommerce includes more features as standard; Shopify offers a larger ecosystem and is often easier to learn. The right choice depends on your requirements.

Is BigCommerce available in the UK?

Yes, BigCommerce supports UK merchants, with pounds sterling and UK payment providers. Check current availability of specific features.

Which is better for B2B?

Both offer B2B features, often depending on plan. List your exact trade workflows and test them on each platform.

Does Shopify charge transaction fees?

Shopify may charge additional fees if you use a third-party payment provider instead of Shopify Payments. Check current terms for your plan.

Can I move from BigCommerce to Shopify?

Yes. Products, customers and orders can be migrated, but design and customisations need rebuilding and URLs need redirecting.
