How to Choose a Payment Gateway for a UK Online Shop

How to choose a payment gateway for a UK online shop: how gateways work, what to compare, Shopify and WooCommerce options, security and payouts.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A payment gateway is the part of your shop that securely takes card and wallet payments and passes the money on to you.
  • Compare gateways on total cost, payout timing, supported payment methods, checkout experience and how disputes are handled, not on headline rates alone.
  • Using a hosted or embedded gateway from a major provider keeps most card data off your own website, which reduces your security burden.

Every online shop needs a way to take payments. The payment gateway is the part of your setup that does this: it securely collects card or wallet details at checkout, checks them, and arranges for the money to reach your bank account. Choose well and it is invisible to customers. Choose badly and you can face slow payouts, declined payments, clunky checkouts or unexpected costs.

This guide explains how payment gateways work and how to choose one for a UK online shop. It does not quote fees, because they vary by provider, plan, card type and business and change over time. Always check current pricing directly with each provider.

What a payment gateway actually does

When a customer pays online, several things happen in a few seconds:

  1. The customer enters card details or chooses a digital wallet at checkout.
  2. The gateway encrypts those details and sends them for authorisation.
  3. The customer's bank checks the payment, sometimes asking the customer to confirm it through their banking app or a code.
  4. The payment is approved or declined, and your shop is told the result.
  5. Approved funds are settled and paid out to your bank account on the provider's schedule.

You will see terms such as gateway, payment processor and merchant account. In the past these were often separate services from different companies. Today, most small UK shops use an all-in-one provider that handles all three, which is much simpler to set up.

The main options for UK shops

Platform payment systems

Shopify has its own built-in payment system, Shopify Payments, available to UK merchants. It is tightly integrated with the platform and avoids setting up a separate provider. Shopify may charge extra transaction fees if you use a third-party gateway instead, so check the current terms on your plan.

WooCommerce also offers its own payments option, built in partnership with a payment provider, alongside extensions for other gateways.

Major standalone providers

Stripe and PayPal are widely used by UK shops and work with Shopify, WooCommerce, Squarespace, Wix and most other platforms. Stripe is commonly used for card payments embedded into checkout. PayPal is recognised by many shoppers, and some customers prefer paying with a PayPal account rather than entering card details.

Bank and specialist providers

Some UK banks and specialist payment companies offer online payment services. These can suit businesses with existing banking relationships, high volumes or particular needs, but setup can be more involved. If you go this route, check your platform supports the provider well.

Many shops use more than one option, for example a card gateway plus PayPal, to give customers a choice.

What to compare

Total cost, not headline rates

Providers price in different ways. Some charge a percentage plus a fixed amount per transaction. Others have monthly fees, different rates for different card types, or extra charges for international cards, currency conversion, refunds or chargebacks. Your platform may add its own charges depending on which gateway you use.

Work out the likely total cost using your own typical order values and volumes. A gateway that looks cheaper on one line can cost more overall once everything is included. For a wider look at running costs, our guide to ecommerce website costs may help, and you can see how we price work on our pricing page.

Payout timing

How long does it take for money to reach your bank account? Payout schedules vary between providers and can be longer for new accounts. For a small business, cash flow matters, so check the payout schedule and whether funds can be held back in some situations.

Payment methods supported

Customers expect to pay the way they prefer. Check which of these each gateway supports:

  • Debit and credit cards, including the major card networks.
  • Digital wallets on phones and browsers.
  • PayPal.
  • Buy now, pay later options, if you choose to offer them.
  • Payments in other currencies, if you sell abroad.

Buy now, pay later has its own considerations, including how it is presented to customers and regulatory changes in the UK. If you plan to offer it, check current guidance from the Financial Conduct Authority and your provider's own requirements.

Checkout experience

Some gateways keep customers on your checkout page with an embedded payment form. Others send them to a page hosted by the provider and then back to your site. Embedded checkouts tend to feel smoother, though hosted pages from well-known providers can reassure some customers.

Test the checkout on a phone, since most shoppers browse on mobile. Our guide to mobile website design explains why small frictions matter. Wallet payments can be particularly quick on mobile because customers do not need to type card details.

Strong Customer Authentication

UK rules require many online card payments to go through an extra verification step, known as Strong Customer Authentication. Customers might confirm a payment in their banking app or with a code. Major gateways handle this, but how smoothly they do it affects how many payments complete. Ask how each provider handles authentication and exemptions.

Disputes, refunds and chargebacks

Sometimes customers dispute payments with their bank. Check how each provider handles chargebacks, what evidence you can submit and whether there are charges. Also check how refunds work, including whether partial refunds are easy to issue from your shop's admin.

Fraud tools

Most major providers include some fraud screening. Ask what is included, whether you can set your own rules, and how false declines (genuine customers being blocked) are handled.

Platform integration

How well does the gateway work with your platform? Good integrations sync payments, refunds and order statuses automatically. Poor ones can mean manual work, mismatched records or broken checkouts after updates. On WooCommerce, a gateway relies on an extension, so it should be well maintained and kept up to date. Our guide to WordPress security covers why this matters.

Security and PCI DSS

Any business that accepts card payments needs to meet the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, known as PCI DSS. The good news for small shops is that using a reputable hosted or embedded gateway means card details are handled by the provider rather than passing through or being stored on your server. This significantly reduces your own compliance burden, though it does not remove it entirely.

Practical steps include:

  • Never storing card numbers yourself, in spreadsheets, emails or your website database.
  • Using HTTPS across your entire site.
  • Keeping your platform, theme and extensions updated.
  • Using strong passwords and two-factor authentication for your shop admin and gateway accounts.
  • Limiting who has access to payment dashboards.

Your provider will usually ask you to complete a short self-assessment. Take it seriously and ask your provider if anything is unclear. Card payment rules and data protection law are both areas where specialist advice may be worthwhile.

Setting up a gateway

The setup process is broadly similar whichever provider you choose:

  1. Create an account with the provider, or activate the platform's built-in payments.
  2. Verify your business. Expect to provide business details, identity documents for directors or owners, and bank account details. Have these ready.
  3. Describe what you sell. Providers restrict certain product categories, so be accurate. Some categories may need extra approval or may not be accepted at all.
  4. Connect the gateway to your shop through the platform's payment settings or an official extension.
  5. Configure options such as which payment methods to show, the statement descriptor customers see on bank statements and email notifications.
  6. Test in test mode using the provider's test card numbers before taking real payments.
  7. Go live and place a small real order to check everything works end to end, then refund it.

If you are setting up a new shop, our Shopify store setup guide covers payments alongside other launch tasks.

Signs your current gateway is not working well

  • Customers report failed payments or being asked to verify repeatedly.
  • A high number of baskets are abandoned at the payment step.
  • Payouts are slow or unpredictable.
  • Refunds and order statuses do not sync with your shop.
  • Popular payment methods are missing.
  • Total costs have crept up as your volumes or customer mix changed.

Checkout analytics can help you see where customers drop off. Our guide to website analytics for small businesses explains how to track this, and our guide to ecommerce CRO looks at improving checkout more broadly.

Switching gateways

Changing provider is usually straightforward for one-off purchases, but plan carefully if you have subscriptions or saved cards. Stored payment details cannot always be moved between providers, and moving subscriptions may require cooperation from both providers or asking customers to re-enter details. If you sell subscriptions, see our guide to ecommerce subscriptions.

Run the new gateway alongside the old one briefly if possible, test thoroughly, and keep the old account open until refunds and disputes on earlier orders are settled.

FAQs

What is the difference between a payment gateway and a payment processor?

The gateway securely captures and sends payment details; the processor moves the transaction through the card networks. Most small shops use one provider that does both.

Do I need a merchant account?

Not usually. All-in-one providers such as Stripe, PayPal and Shopify Payments let you accept cards without arranging a separate merchant account.

Should I offer PayPal as well as card payments?

Many shops do, because some customers prefer it. Offering a few popular options can help, but avoid cluttering checkout with too many.

Is it safe to take card payments on my website?

Using a reputable hosted or embedded gateway keeps card details off your server and handles much of the security. You still need to keep your site secure and meet PCI DSS requirements.

How much do payment gateways cost?

It varies by provider, plan and transaction type, and pricing changes. Compare current pricing from each provider using your own order data.
