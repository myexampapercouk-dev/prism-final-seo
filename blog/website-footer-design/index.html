Website Footer Design: What to Include and How to Make It Useful

A UK guide to website footer design: essential links, contact details, legal pages, newsletter sign-ups, ecommerce footers, mobile layout and accessibility.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • The footer is where visitors look when they cannot find something elsewhere, so it should act as a reliable safety net.
  • Group footer links into a few clear sections rather than listing every page on the site.
  • Contact details, legal information and key policies belong in the footer, presented clearly on every screen size.

The footer is easy to overlook. It sits at the bottom of every page, often designed last and updated rarely. Yet visitors use it more than you might expect. When someone cannot find contact details, delivery information or a privacy policy, the footer is where many of them scroll to look. A clear, well-organised footer quietly supports navigation, trust and practical tasks across the whole site.

This guide explains what a website footer is for, what to include, how to organise it and how to make it work well on mobile and for every visitor.

What the footer is for

A footer has a few practical jobs:

  • Safety net navigation. It gives visitors a second chance to find pages they could not locate in the main menu.
  • Practical information. It holds details people look for at the end of a page, such as contact information, opening hours or delivery and returns links.
  • Legal and policy links. It provides a consistent home for privacy, cookie and terms pages.
  • Reassurance. Business details and clear policies help visitors feel they are dealing with a real, accountable business.
  • A final next step. For visitors who reach the end of a page, it can offer a route to contact, subscribe or explore further.

The footer supports the main navigation rather than replacing it. Our website navigation guide covers planning the main menu and site structure.

What to include in a footer

The right contents depend on your business and site, but most footers draw from the following.

Contact details

Include the main ways customers can reach you: an email address, phone number if you take calls, and a link to your contact page. If you have a physical location that customers visit, include the address and opening hours. If you work remotely, there is no need to invent an address; a clear contact route is what matters.

Business information

Many businesses show their legal business name and other identifying details in the footer. Depending on your business type, there may be information you are expected or required to show on your website, such as company registration details for limited companies or regulatory information for regulated firms. Requirements vary, so it is worth checking what applies to your business rather than relying on a general list.

Legal and policy pages

Common links include your privacy policy, cookie policy and website terms. Shops usually add terms of sale, delivery information and returns. Accessibility statements are also often linked from the footer. Keep these links consistent across every page so they are always easy to find.

Key navigation links

Choose the pages visitors most often need, such as main services or product categories, about, blog or resources, and help or FAQ pages. You do not need to link to everything. A footer listing every page on the site becomes a wall of links that is hard to scan.

Social media links

If you maintain active social media profiles, small icons linking to them are common. Only include profiles you actually keep up to date; a link to an abandoned account can do more harm than good.

Newsletter sign-up

A short email sign-up form works well in many footers, particularly for shops and content-led sites. Keep it simple: an email field, a clear description of what subscribers will receive and a link to your privacy policy. Make sure your sign-up process follows the rules that apply to email marketing consent; if you are unsure, check current guidance.

Copyright line

A copyright line with the business name and year is conventional. If you include a year, keep it current, as an old date can make a site look neglected.

Organising footer content

A good footer is easy to scan. Some principles:

  • Group links into a few columns or sections, each with a short, clear heading such as "Services", "Help" or "Company".
  • Keep labels short and familiar. Use the same names as your main navigation so visitors recognise them.
  • Prioritise. Put the most-used links in the most prominent positions.
  • Separate legal links into a smaller row at the very bottom, so they are available without competing with main links.
  • Use enough spacing and contrast so the footer is readable, not cramped.

Footers are often designed in a darker or contrasting colour to separate them from the main content. That is fine, but make sure text still meets contrast guidelines.

Ecommerce footers

Online shops tend to have busier footers because customers look there for practical help. Useful elements include:

  • Delivery information and delivery areas.
  • Returns and refunds information.
  • Order tracking or account links.
  • Customer service contact details and hours.
  • Size guides or care guides where relevant.
  • Accepted payment methods, shown simply.
  • Main product categories.

Shoppers often check delivery and returns before buying, so making these easy to find from the footer can help reassure them. Our ecommerce website design guide covers shop design more broadly, and our website trust signals guide explains other ways to build confidence.

Service business footers

For service businesses, the footer can reinforce how to get in touch and what you offer. Consider including a short summary of your services, links to each main service page, contact details and a simple call to action such as a link to book a call or request a quote. Keep it calm and useful rather than repeating a sales pitch.

Footer design on mobile

On mobile, a footer with four columns of links can turn into a very long scroll. Common approaches include:

  • Stacking sections vertically with clear headings.
  • Using collapsible sections so visitors can open only the group they need.
  • Keeping contact details and key actions visible rather than collapsed.
  • Making links large enough to tap comfortably, with enough spacing between them.

Test the footer on real devices. Our mobile website design guide has more on designing for smaller screens.

Accessibility

Footers should be as accessible as the rest of the site. Good practice includes:

  • Using a proper footer landmark in the page code, so assistive technology can identify it.
  • Giving link groups clear headings.
  • Ensuring sufficient colour contrast, particularly on dark backgrounds.
  • Making social icons understandable to screen readers with text labels.
  • Ensuring collapsible sections work with a keyboard and announce their state.

Our website accessibility guide explains the principles in more depth.

Footers and SEO

Footer links help visitors and search engines discover important pages, but they are not a shortcut to rankings. Stuffing the footer with long lists of keyword-heavy links, such as dozens of location pages, can look spammy and is unlikely to help. Link to pages that are genuinely useful to visitors, and rely on good main navigation and contextual internal links within content for structure.

Footer mistakes to avoid

  • Listing every page on the site.
  • Out-of-date copyright years, contact details or social links.
  • Missing or hard-to-find privacy and policy pages.
  • Low-contrast text that is hard to read.
  • Very long footers on mobile with no structure.
  • Keyword-stuffed link lists aimed at search engines.
  • Broken links to pages that have moved.

A simple footer checklist

  • Clear contact details and a link to the contact page.
  • Business information appropriate to your business type.
  • Privacy, cookie and terms links, plus shop policies if relevant.
  • A small set of key navigation links, grouped under headings.
  • Optional newsletter sign-up and active social links.
  • Readable contrast and spacing.
  • A tidy mobile layout with tappable links.
  • Accessible markup and keyboard-friendly interactions.
  • A regular check for broken or outdated links.

FAQs

What should every website footer include?

Most footers benefit from contact details, key navigation links and links to privacy and other policy pages. The rest depends on your business.

Do footer links help SEO?

They help search engines discover important pages, but they are not a ranking shortcut. Keep footer links useful to visitors rather than stuffed with keywords.

Should my address be in the footer?

If customers visit you, yes. If you work remotely, a clear contact route is enough. Check whether any business information is required for your business type.

Is a newsletter sign-up in the footer worthwhile?

It can be, particularly for shops and content sites. Keep it simple and make clear what subscribers will receive.

How often should I review my footer?

Whenever your services, policies or contact details change, and as part of a regular site check for broken or outdated links.
