Multi-Location SEO Audit: How UK Businesses Can Review Every Branch

How to audit multi-location SEO across UK branches: business profiles, location pages, duplicate content, links, reviews, tracking and what to fix first.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A multi-location SEO audit checks each branch and the system around it, comparing profiles, location pages, business details, links, reviews and reporting.
  • Use one simple spreadsheet with a row for every branch, because it reveals patterns that are easy to miss when you review locations one at a time.
  • Tackle problems that affect many branches or could send customers to the wrong place first, such as wrong details, duplicate profiles or weak page templates.

A multi-location SEO audit is a structured review of how every branch of your business appears in search, and whether your website and profiles support each one. It checks ownership and accuracy of business profiles, the quality of location pages, duplicate content, consistent business details, internal links, reviews and reporting by branch. The result should be a prioritised list of fixes, with the problems affecting customers or many locations dealt with first.

This guide is for UK businesses with several branches, offices, clinics, showrooms or service areas. It sets out how to review what you already have. For the principles of building a strategy from scratch, read our guide to multi-location SEO in the UK, and for the fundamentals, see our local SEO guide.

Why audit branches together?

Looking at one branch at a time can hide the real cause of a problem. If one location page performs poorly, that may be down to local competition. If a whole set of them performs poorly, the issue may be the template, the internal linking or the way the pages were made. Multi-location businesses also tend to accumulate inconsistency as sites are added, branches move and different staff take over the marketing.

An audit gives you a shared view of those issues. It also shows which branches are in good shape, so you can learn from what they do differently while allowing for differences in their local markets.

Set up your audit sheet

Before you start, create a spreadsheet with one row per location. Treat it as the single source of truth for the audit. Useful columns include:

  • Branch name and official address.
  • Phone number, opening hours and page URL.
  • Business profile link and who holds access to it.
  • Primary and secondary categories.
  • Review count, recent review activity and whether reviews are answered.
  • Page status, including whether it is indexed and has unique content.
  • Issues found, priority and owner.

Confirm the correct details with someone in each branch, rather than copying them from the website. Your existing data may be the very thing that is wrong.

Audit your business profiles

For most physical locations, your Google Business Profile listing is a central part of local visibility. Our Google Business Profile guide explains set-up in detail. In the audit, check each one for the following:

  • Ownership and access: is it held by a company account, or by a former employee or supplier? Can you manage all branches in one organised way?
  • Duplicates: search for each branch and look for duplicated or out-of-date listings. Follow Google's current process for dealing with them.
  • Closed or moved branches: make sure these are marked correctly, so customers are not sent to an empty building.
  • Eligibility: check that each listing meets Google's guidelines, particularly for service-area businesses and shared premises.
  • Accuracy: compare names, addresses, phone numbers, hours, holiday hours and website links with your sheet.
  • Categories and services: make sure they reflect what each branch actually offers, which may not be identical everywhere.
  • Link destination: each listing should normally link to the relevant location page, rather than always to the homepage.

Business names should reflect your real-world name. Adding keywords or town names that are not part of it may breach the guidelines, so check current guidance before making changes.

Audit your location pages

Each location page should help someone who needs that particular branch. Look at them side by side and be honest about whether they are useful.

Uniqueness and usefulness

Copy the main text of several pages into one document. If only the town name changes, you have a template problem. Useful local detail could include who works at the branch, services specific to it, directions, parking, nearby landmarks, local accessibility information, photographs of the actual location and relevant case studies or reviews.

Basic local information

Each page should show the correct name, address, phone number and opening hours, a map or directions and a clear next step. On a phone, it should be easy to tap to call or get directions. Our mobile website design guide covers those details.

Titles, headings and structured data

Check that each page has its own title, description and main heading. Where appropriate, local business schema markup can help describe the location to search engines, and it must match what is visible on the page. Our schema markup guide explains how this works.

Missing and unnecessary pages

Compare your page list with your actual branches. Each real branch should have a page if it serves customers. You should also look out for pages created only to target towns where you have no presence. Pages made purely to catch searches, with little real value, can be treated as doorway pages, which is against Google's spam policies. Service-area content is fine if it is honest and genuinely helpful, but review it carefully.

Audit your site structure and technical health

Good pages will struggle if search engines and people cannot find them easily. Check the following:

  • Indexing: use Google Search Console to see whether each location page is indexed. Look for accidental noindex tags or canonical tags pointing elsewhere.
  • Canonicals: templates can sometimes point every location page to one master version, which is a serious error.
  • Internal links: make sure a branch finder or locations hub links to every branch in plain crawlable links, and that relevant service pages link to related locations.
  • Branch finders: if your locator relies on a search box or script, search engines and some users may not reach individual pages. Provide simple links too.
  • URLs: check that the pattern is consistent and that old branch URLs redirect sensibly.
  • Speed: maps and embedded widgets can slow a page down, so test the template on a mobile connection.

When a branch moves or closes, think before you delete the page. Our guide to website redirects explains the principles, and our technical SEO audit checklist covers the wider site.

Audit your business details across the web

Search for each branch's old and current addresses and phone numbers. Check industry directories, trade bodies, social profiles and any local listings that matter in your sector. You do not need to appear everywhere, but where a listing exists, it should be correct. Tracking phone numbers need care too, so use them in a way that does not cause confusion about your real number.

Audit reviews and reputation

Compare branches rather than only looking at the overall company score. Look for:

  • Branches with few or very old reviews.
  • Reviews going unanswered, including negative ones.
  • Recurring complaints that point to a real operational problem.
  • Whether staff know how, and when, to ask for reviews.
  • Any practices that may breach platform rules or UK consumer law, such as selectively asking only happy customers or offering rewards for positive reviews. Check the current rules before setting up any review scheme.

Decide who responds. A central team can maintain standards, while branch staff often know the context. Whichever you choose, write it down, and be careful not to reveal personal details in a public reply.

Audit local links and content

Look at which sites link to your branches. Links from local organisations, suppliers, events and the local press can be valuable, as long as they are earned through real activity. Our guide to digital PR and link building explains the approach.

Branch staff can be an underused source of local material. Ask what each branch does in its community and what customers there ask about, and use that where it helps. Avoid publishing local news simply for the sake of naming a town.

Audit reporting and ownership

A blended company total can hide a struggling branch. Check whether you can see, by location:

  • Search visibility and clicks to the location page, using Google Search Console.
  • Profile actions such as calls, website visits and direction requests.
  • Website enquiries, tracked in analytics by branch.
  • Calls, bookings or sales, where your internal systems allow it.

Remember that results are shaped by each town's competition and demand, so a direct league table can mislead. Our guide to SEO reporting offers more on useful measures. Also agree who updates what when something changes: a new branch manager, a change of hours or a closure should trigger a defined process.

How to prioritise what you find

PriorityExamples
Fix nowWrong phone numbers or addresses, closed branches shown as open, lost profile access, location pages blocked from search
Fix nextDuplicate listings, template errors, weak branch finder links, missing tracking
Improve over timeUnique page content, photographs, local links, review processes
Consider carefullyRemoving or merging thin pages, changing URL structure, restructuring branches

Fixes to a template can help many pages at once, so they can be efficient, though large changes should be tested first and rolled out carefully.

FAQs

How often should we audit multi-location SEO?

It depends on how often your business changes. A regular light check on details and profiles, plus a deeper review when you add, move or close branches, is a sensible approach for many businesses.

Can we use the same content on every location page?

Some shared information is reasonable, such as a description of a service. But each page should also have enough real local detail to be useful in itself. Pages that differ only by town name offer little value.

What should we do about a branch that has closed?

Update the business profile in line with Google's process, then decide what happens to its page. It might redirect to the nearest relevant branch or the locations hub, or explain the closure where people still look for it. Update other listings too.

Should a central team control every profile?

Central ownership of access is usually sensible, so nothing is lost when staff leave. Branch teams can still contribute local updates, photographs and review responses, within agreed guidelines.

Can a business rank in towns where it has no branch?

Possibly, but map results are heavily influenced by proximity and other factors. Do not create fake addresses. If you really serve an area, helpful service pages may be appropriate, and paid search may also fill gaps.

Next steps

Start the spreadsheet with your branch list and check the details against each profile. That alone can show where the bigger problems lie.
