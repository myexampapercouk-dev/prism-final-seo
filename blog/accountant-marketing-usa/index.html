How buyers choose an accountant

Most business owners and individuals compare several firms before getting in touch. They search for a service plus their city, check reviews and the Google Business Profile, ask peers, and increasingly ask AI assistants for suggestions. They then visit your website to answer three questions: do you understand my situation, can I trust you, and how do I start?

Your marketing should answer those questions everywhere a buyer looks. Our UK accountant marketing guide covers the same buyer journey for British firms, and most of the principles carry over.

Accountant marketing USA: the website comes first

A strong accounting firm website should include:

  • Clear service pages for tax preparation, bookkeeping, payroll, advisory, audit or whatever you offer.
  • Niche pages for the industries or client types you serve best.
  • Team and credentials information stated accurately, using only credentials the firm genuinely holds.
  • A simple onboarding path, such as a short form, call booking and a link to your client portal.
  • Helpful resources covering deadlines and common questions in plain language.
  • Office pages with address, hours and directions if you have more than one location.

SEO for US accounting firms

SEO helps prospective clients find you when they search for specific services. For accounting firms, we focus on:

  • Local SEO: a complete Google Business Profile, consistent firm details and office pages.
  • Service and niche pages that target what clients search, such as small business tax help in a particular city.
  • Helpful content that explains common questions without giving personal advice.
  • Technical health: speed, mobile layout, structured data and secure forms.
  • AI search readiness: clear, factual answers that AI tools can understand and quote.

Planning around tax season

Demand for accounting services is seasonal. A simple yearly plan helps:

  • Before the busy season: update service pages, deadline content and the Google Business Profile, and prepare ad campaigns.
  • During the busy season: keep ads focused on services you can still take on, and pause them if capacity runs out.
  • After the busy season: promote advisory, bookkeeping and planning work, and gather reviews from satisfied clients where your rules allow.

Professional rules, privacy and other notes

These notes are general information only. Please check official guidance or speak to your own adviser.

  • Professional rules. Accountants are often subject to rules on advertising, titles and claims, set by state boards and professional bodies. Approval of all marketing content rests with your firm and its compliance lead. We do not give professional or legal advice.
  • Privacy and data security. Accounting firms handle sensitive financial data. Forms on your website should collect only what is needed, and documents should go through a secure client portal rather than email. US privacy rules vary by state, and your adviser should approve your privacy policy.
  • Accessibility. Many US businesses use the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines as a reference. We follow good practice, but we do not give legal opinions on the ADA.
  • Sales tax. If your firm sells products or digital downloads online, sales tax rules may apply and vary by state. Your own tax team will know how they apply to you.

Common mistakes in accountant marketing

  • Trying to appeal to everyone. Clear niches usually attract better-matched clients.
  • One page listing every service. Separate pages are easier to find and easier to read.
  • Running ads with no capacity. Paying for leads you cannot serve wastes budget and goodwill.
  • Collecting sensitive data through basic forms. Use a secure portal for documents.
  • Out-of-date deadline content. Review it each year.

Choosing a niche and writing for it

Many accounting firms find that a clear niche makes every part of marketing easier. A firm that focuses on, say, restaurants, contractors, medical practices or online sellers can write pages that speak directly to those clients, use the words they use and answer the questions they actually ask.

A good niche page usually covers:

  • The common money problems that type of business faces, described in plain language.
  • The services that help, such as monthly bookkeeping, payroll, quarterly planning or year-end tax work.
  • How onboarding works, including which systems you connect to and what the client needs to send.
  • How fees are structured, even if exact prices depend on scope.
  • FAQs that reflect real questions from that type of client.

Niche pages also help with SEO and AI search, because they answer specific questions clearly. They give paid campaigns a focused landing page, and they make referrals easier because existing clients can share a page that describes exactly what you do for businesses like theirs.

You do not need to turn away other clients. A niche simply gives your marketing a sharper edge, while general service pages still cover everyone else.

Reviews and reputation

Reviews matter for local search and for trust. Ask satisfied clients at natural moments, such as after a return is filed or a year-end meeting, and reply to every review politely. Check first that your professional rules allow the way you ask, and never offer incentives for reviews.
