Electrician Website Design: Building a Site That Wins the Right Jobs

Electrician website design for UK sparkies and electrical contractors: service pages, quote forms, emergency call-outs, accreditations and areas covered.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • An electrician's website should separate urgent call-outs from planned work, because the two visitors behave very differently.
  • Registration and certification details are central to trust in electrical work, but describe them accurately and only if you hold them.
  • Service pages and a quote form that captures the right details help you filter jobs before you spend time on site visits.

Electrical work is one of those trades where customers care a lot about trust and very little about style. Someone with a tripping circuit at 9pm wants to know you can come out. Someone planning a rewire or an EV charger wants to know you are qualified, registered and likely to do a tidy job. A good electrician's website speaks to both and gets each of them to the right next step quickly.

This guide is about the website: the pages, forms, layout and trust details that suit domestic and commercial electricians in the UK. For promotion beyond the website, such as directories, reviews and local advertising for trades, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK.

Two kinds of visitor: urgent and planned

Most electricians get both types of enquiry, and they need different handling.

  • Urgent visitors have a fault: no power, a burning smell, a tripping board. They want a phone number, an idea of whether you cover their area and whether you do emergency call-outs at all. They will not read much.
  • Planned visitors are thinking about a rewire, consumer unit upgrade, extension wiring, lighting, EV charger or electrical inspection. They compare a few firms, read about your experience and want an easy way to request a quote.

If you offer emergency work, make that unmissable on mobile with a tap-to-call button and clear hours. If you do not, say so politely, so you are not woken by calls you cannot take. Both choices are fine; the mistake is leaving people guessing.

If your site mentions electrical emergencies, it is sensible to include basic safety signposting, for example advising people to contact their network operator in a power cut affecting the area, or to call the emergency gas number if they smell gas. Keep it short and accurate.

Pages that suit electrical businesses

  • Home. What you do, where you work, domestic or commercial or both, a phone number and a quote button.
  • Service pages. One page per main service rather than a single long list.
  • Areas covered. Towns and districts you work in.
  • About and accreditations. Who you are, how long you have been working, your registrations and insurance.
  • Recent work. Photos and short descriptions of real jobs.
  • Contact or quote. A form, phone, email and when you usually respond.

Service pages that do real work

A single services page listing rewires, fuse boards, sockets, lighting, EV chargers, testing and alarms gives search engines and visitors very little to go on. A dedicated page for each core service is more useful. Each page can explain:

  • What the service involves, in plain English.
  • Signs a customer might need it, for example an old fuse box or frequent tripping.
  • Roughly what to expect on the day, such as access needed or how long power may be off.
  • What paperwork they should receive, such as an electrical installation certificate or a minor works certificate, where relevant.
  • A clear call to action to request a quote.

Write for homeowners and landlords, not other electricians. Technical terms are fine if you explain them. Our service page design guide covers how to structure pages like these.

Landlords and electrical safety reports

Private landlords in England have legal duties around electrical safety inspections, and there are similar but not identical requirements in Scotland and Wales. If you carry out electrical installation condition reports for landlords, a dedicated page can help. Keep any explanation of the rules brief, avoid giving definitive legal advice and link to the official guidance on GOV.UK or the relevant devolved government site, as the details can change and vary by nation.

EV chargers and newer work

Electric vehicle charger installation, battery storage and solar connections are growing areas of interest. If you offer them, give each a page. Be careful with statements about grants, schemes or savings: these change and depend on individual circumstances. Link to official sources instead of quoting figures that may go out of date.

Registrations, certification and trust

In England and Wales, much domestic electrical work falls under building regulations, and electricians commonly join a competent person scheme so they can self-certify compliant work. Customers increasingly look for this. Your website can explain, in plain terms, which scheme you are registered with, what that means for the customer and how they can check your registration on the scheme's public register.

Some important cautions:

  • Only display logos and statements for schemes, memberships and qualifications you actually hold, and follow each scheme's rules on how their marks may be used.
  • Keep wording accurate. Do not imply an approval or status you do not have.
  • Update the site promptly if a registration lapses or changes.
  • State that you hold public liability insurance only if you do.

Rules differ in Scotland and Northern Ireland, so if you work there, describe your position accurately for that nation. Our guide to website trust signals has more on presenting credibility honestly.

Quote forms that save you wasted trips

A well-designed quote form can do a surprising amount of filtering. Useful fields might include:

  • Name, phone and postcode.
  • Type of work, chosen from a short list.
  • Property type and approximate age, which can matter for rewires.
  • A free text box for details.
  • An optional photo upload, for example of the consumer unit or the area to be worked on.
  • Preferred timing.

Photo uploads can be particularly helpful for electricians, letting you judge a job before visiting. Make sure your form handles uploaded files securely and that your privacy notice explains what you do with the information. Our website contact forms guide goes into form design in more detail.

Keep the form short enough that people finish it on a phone. If there is a lot you need to know, ask the essentials first and follow up by phone.

Showing your work

Tidy work is a selling point in electrical jobs, and photos help prove it. A recent work page might show neatly installed consumer units, finished lighting schemes, EV chargers and commercial fit-outs. Add a short caption explaining what the job involved and where it was, in general terms.

Be careful about customer privacy: avoid showing house numbers, faces or anything that identifies a property without permission. Reviews should be genuine and used with consent.

Areas covered and local search

Most electrician searches are local, often phrased as electrician near me or electrician in a particular town. Your website supports your Google Business Profile, so keep your name, phone number and service areas consistent across both.

A clear areas covered page listing towns and districts helps. If you create individual area pages, give them genuinely different content, such as typical jobs in that area or the housing stock you work on, rather than copying the same text with a new town name. Our local SEO guide explains more about how local rankings work.

Design priorities

An electrician's site should feel competent and straightforward. Some priorities:

  • A tap-to-call button and quote button that stay visible on mobile.
  • Clear, readable text with strong contrast.
  • Real photos of you, your van and your work.
  • Fast loading, especially on mobile data.
  • Simple navigation so people find a service in one or two taps.

Avoid heavy stock imagery of lightning bolts and generic hard hats. They make a site look like many others and say nothing about you.

Domestic versus commercial

If you do both domestic and commercial work, consider separating them clearly in your navigation. Commercial clients often care about different things: experience with similar premises, working out of hours to avoid disruption, testing and inspection schedules and documentation. A dedicated commercial section lets you speak to them directly without confusing homeowners.

Platforms and upkeep

Many electricians start with a hosted builder like Wix or Squarespace, which are easy to update. WordPress offers more flexibility for multiple service and area pages. The best choice is the one you or your web partner will keep current. Our small business website checklist is a useful sense check before launch.

FAQs

What pages should an electrician's website have?

Typically a homepage, individual service pages, an areas covered page, an about and accreditations page, recent work and a contact or quote page.

Should I add emergency call-outs to my website?

Only if you genuinely offer them. If you do, make the phone number and hours prominent. If not, say so clearly.

Can I display scheme logos on my website?

Only for schemes you belong to, and in line with each scheme's rules on using their marks.

Is a photo upload useful on a quote form?

It can be, helping you understand a job before visiting. Make sure files are handled securely and covered by your privacy notice.

Should I write separate pages for each town I cover?

Only if each page has genuinely useful, distinct content. Near-identical pages add little value.

Should domestic and commercial work be on separate pages?

Usually, yes. The two audiences care about different things, and separating them makes the site clearer for both.
