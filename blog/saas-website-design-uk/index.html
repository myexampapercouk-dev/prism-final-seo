SaaS Website Design: Messaging, Pricing Pages and Sign-Ups

Website design for UK SaaS and software startups: clear positioning, product pages, pricing tables, trials and demos, documentation, security and onboarding.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Visitors to a software website usually need to understand what the product does, who it is for and how to try it within moments of arriving.
  • Pricing pages, trial or demo routes and product explanations tend to do more work on a SaaS site than on most other kinds of business website.
  • Security, data protection and accessibility claims should be accurate and specific, so check what you can genuinely support before publishing them.

For a software company, the website is often the main place a prospective customer meets the product before they sign up, book a demo or start a trial. That puts a lot of weight on the marketing site. It has to explain something intangible, persuade busy people to invest time in trying it, and hand them smoothly into the product itself.

This guide covers website design for UK SaaS and software startups, from early-stage products looking for their first users to growing companies selling to larger teams. It focuses on the marketing website. For attracting visitors to it, see our guides to SaaS SEO and SaaS PPC, and for wider early-stage promotion see our guide to marketing for startups.

Start with positioning, not pages

Many software websites struggle because the product's positioning is unclear rather than because of the design. Before planning pages, it helps to be able to answer:

  • Who is the product for, specifically?
  • What problem does it solve for them?
  • What do they use today instead, even if that is a spreadsheet?
  • Why is your approach better for them?

The answers shape the headline, the order of the homepage and which features get attention. A headline that names the audience and the outcome is usually clearer than a clever phrase or a list of technologies. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing for clarity.

Homepage structure

A SaaS homepage often follows a broad pattern, adapted to the product:

  • A headline and short supporting line explaining what the product does and for whom.
  • A primary call to action, such as start a trial, sign up or book a demo.
  • A real view of the product, such as a screenshot or short clip.
  • Key benefits linked to the features that deliver them.
  • Proof, such as customer logos or testimonials, only where you have permission.
  • Answers to common objections, then a repeated call to action.

Show the actual interface where you can. Abstract illustrations may look polished but often leave visitors unsure what they would be using. Keep screenshots current, because an outdated interface can undermine trust when people sign up and see something different. Our guide to homepage design goes further.

Product and feature pages

As a product grows, a single homepage cannot explain everything. Separate pages can cover:

  • Major features or modules, explained in terms of the job they do.
  • Use cases or roles, such as finance teams or agencies.
  • Industries, where you genuinely serve them.
  • Integrations with other tools, listed accurately.
  • Comparison pages explaining how your approach differs from alternatives, kept fair and factual.

These pages also give search engines specific pages to show for specific searches. Avoid creating use case or industry pages for audiences you do not actually serve, and keep integration lists honest about what is native, what needs a third-party connector and what is planned.

Pricing pages

Pricing is one of the most visited pages on many software sites. Common decisions include:

  • Whether to publish prices or ask larger customers to contact sales.
  • How many plans to offer and how to name them.
  • Whether to show monthly and annual prices, and how to present any annual discount.
  • Which limits or features separate each plan.
  • Whether prices are shown with or without VAT.

A clear comparison table, a short FAQ beneath it and an obvious action for each plan tend to help. Make sure the page matches what happens in your billing system, and be clear about renewals, cancellation terms and what happens at the end of a free trial. UK consumer law has specific rules on subscriptions and pricing where you sell to consumers, so take advice if that applies. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout options.

Trials, freemium and demos

How people start using the product shapes the whole site. Self-serve products usually push a sign-up or trial button, while products sold to larger organisations often lead with a demo request. Many offer both. Think about:

  • How few fields the sign-up form can have.
  • Whether a payment card is required to start a trial.
  • What the first screen after sign-up looks like, since the handover from website to product matters.
  • For demos, whether people can book a time directly rather than waiting for an email.

Our guides to landing page design and online booking systems cover related decisions.

Security, privacy and compliance information

Business buyers, particularly larger ones, often need to check how you handle their data before they can sign up. A security or trust page can explain in plain terms where data is hosted, how it is protected, how you handle UK GDPR obligations and how customers can request further documentation. Only mention certifications, audits or standards you have actually achieved, and date the information. Make your privacy notice, terms of service and any data processing agreement easy to find. Where legal wording is involved, take professional advice rather than copying another company's documents.

Documentation, help and changelogs

Help centres, documentation and changelogs are often part of the buying process as well as support. Prospective customers read them to understand how the product really works, and technical buyers may check API documentation before agreeing to a trial. Decide whether these live on the main site or a separate subdomain, make them easy to reach from the navigation, and keep them current. A public changelog can show the product is actively developed, but only if it is updated regularly.

Blog and content

Useful articles can attract the people your product helps and give them a reason to come back. Content works best when it addresses real problems your audience searches for, rather than only announcing features. Connect relevant articles to the product pages that solve the problem they discuss, without making every post a sales pitch.

Design systems and consistency

Software companies ship changes quickly, and the marketing site needs to keep up. A small set of reusable page sections, such as feature blocks, testimonial strips and pricing tables, makes it easier to launch new pages without design drift. Keeping the website's visual style close to the product's interface also helps the move from marketing site to app feel seamless. Accessibility matters on both; our guide to website accessibility covers the basics.

Choosing a platform

Many SaaS teams want marketers to update the site without waiting for developers. A content management system such as WordPress, or a hosted builder for very early products, can allow this. Some teams build the marketing site within their own codebase, which gives developers control but can slow down content changes. Think about who will edit the site, how often and how it connects to sign-up and analytics. See our guides to website builders vs custom websites and website speed optimisation.

Measuring what matters

Set up analytics to track the actions that matter, such as trial starts, demo bookings and pricing page visits, and connect them where possible to what happens next in the product. Google Analytics and Google Search Console are common starting points. Make sure any tracking respects cookie consent rules under UK law.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A headline that describes technology rather than the outcome for the user.
  • Abstract illustrations instead of the real product.
  • Pricing pages that do not match billing or hide key terms.
  • Long sign-up forms that ask for details you could collect later.
  • Logos or certifications shown without permission or evidence.
  • Out-of-date screenshots and integration lists.

FAQs

What pages does a SaaS website need?

Typically a homepage, product or feature pages, pricing, a sign-up or demo route, security and legal pages, and help or documentation. More pages can follow as the product grows.

Should a SaaS company publish its prices?

Many self-serve products do. Products sold to larger organisations sometimes ask enterprise buyers to contact sales. Either way, be clear about what is included.

Should I offer a free trial or a demo?

It depends on how complex the product is and who buys it. Many companies offer both, with the website directing each visitor to the route that suits them.

Where should SaaS documentation live?

On the main site or a subdomain, as long as it is easy to reach, searchable and kept up to date.

How important are screenshots on a SaaS website?

Real, current screenshots help visitors understand what they would actually be using, which abstract illustrations often fail to do.
