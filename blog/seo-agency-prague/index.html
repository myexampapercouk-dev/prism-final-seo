What Prague businesses need from SEO

Prague is the Czech capital and the country's main business centre. It is home to software and technology firms, shared service centres, professional services, manufacturers with export sales, tourism and hospitality businesses, property firms and a growing number of online shops. Many sell to customers both inside the Czech Republic and abroad, so their search work often has two audiences:

  • Czech-speaking buyers. Local customers who search in Czech and expect Czech pages, prices and contact details.
  • International buyers and partners. Companies in Germany, the UK and elsewhere who search in English for suppliers, developers or service partners.
  • Visitors and newcomers. Tourists, students and people relocating to Prague who search in English for services they need on arrival.
  • Job seekers. Growing firms often need careers pages that rank for roles in both languages.

Each audience uses different words and different pages, so a single keyword list rarely covers a Prague business properly. We map the searches for each audience before we touch the site.

Google, Seznam and AI search

Google handles most searches in the Czech Republic, so it is where most SEO effort goes. Seznam, a Czech search engine and web portal, still has a loyal audience, and its search results reward many of the same basics: a crawlable site, clear titles, useful content and a good reputation. We build to those shared fundamentals first, then check how your key pages appear in both engines.

Searchers also ask AI assistants and see AI summaries in search results. Pages that answer questions directly, use clear headings and state facts plainly are easier for these systems to quote. Our guide to generative engine optimisation explains how we adapt content so it can be cited as well as ranked.

Czech and English keyword research

Translating an English keyword list into Czech rarely works. Czech is an inflected language, so the same idea can appear in several word endings, and people often search with or without diacritics. Buyers may also use a different term altogether from the one an English speaker would choose. We research each language separately, group searches by intent and match each group to one page so pages do not compete with each other.

Prism carries out the English research and drafts in-house. For Czech, we provide the structure, search intent and candidate terms, then a native speaker on your team or a translator confirms the phrasing that real buyers use. Our keyword research guide explains the process we follow for any language.

Technical SEO for bilingual sites

Many Prague websites were built in Czech first, with an English section added later. That often leaves technical problems that hold both versions back:

  • Missing or broken hreflang. Search engines cannot tell which page to show to which searcher, so the wrong language appears in results.
  • Language switchers that lose the page. A visitor on a Czech service page clicks English and lands on the English home page instead of the matching service.
  • Mixed URLs. Some pages use Czech characters in URLs, some use plain letters and some use parameters, which creates duplicates.
  • Slow templates. Heavy sliders, scripts and uncompressed images slow pages down on mobile.
  • Thin English pages. Short, machine-translated pages that rank poorly and do little to convince international buyers.

We start with a full audit using the same technical SEO audit checklist we use on every project, then fix the issues in order of impact. Our multilingual website design guide covers the language structure that makes bilingual SEO easier to manage.

Consent, privacy and accessibility

Czech websites fall under the GDPR for personal data, and the EU ePrivacy rules mean analytics and marketing cookies normally need consent before they load. That affects SEO reporting, because visitors who decline cookies are not counted in the same way. We set up analytics and a clear consent banner so the data you do collect is reliable. Our guide to cookie banner design covers the approach.

The European Accessibility Act has applied since June 2025 to many consumer-facing products and services, including a wide range of online shops. Microenterprises providing services are generally exempt, but building to WCAG AA, the level referenced by the harmonised standard EN 301 549, is good practice and supports SEO through clear headings, alt text and readable pages. Legal wording and decisions on compliance should come from your legal adviser, and official guidance should be checked before relying on any summary.

How content earns rankings

Technical fixes remove obstacles, but content is what earns rankings over time. For most Prague businesses, the content plan has three layers:

  1. Service and product pages. One strong page per service or category, written for the searches buyers actually use.
  2. Supporting guides. Answers to the questions buyers ask before they enquire, such as costs, timelines, comparisons and how a process works.
  3. Trust pages. About and process pages, the details you are happy to publish about how you work, and clear contact details.

We plan each piece around one search intent, link related pages together and update older pages rather than publishing near-duplicates. If you sell into several countries, our international SEO guide explains how to expand into new languages without splitting your authority.
