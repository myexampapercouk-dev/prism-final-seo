Ecommerce SEO Checklist: A Step-by-Step Guide for UK Online Stores

Ecommerce SEO checklist for UK stores: technical, category, product, content, links and tracking checks to work through in order, on any platform.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Work through ecommerce SEO in order: crawlability first, then category and product pages, then content, links and tracking.
  • Category pages usually carry the most commercial search demand, so they deserve the most attention.
  • Most checks apply to Shopify, WooCommerce and other platforms alike; only the settings you use to fix them differ.

An ecommerce SEO checklist is a structured list of the technical, on-page, content and tracking checks that help an online store rank in Google. Work through it in order: make sure search engines can crawl and index the right pages, then improve category and product pages, then build content, links and measurement on top.

The checks below apply to Shopify, WooCommerce and most other platforms. Use it for a new store before launch or to audit an existing one. For a deeper technical pass, pair it with our technical SEO audit checklist.

How to use this ecommerce SEO checklist

Do not try to fix everything at once. Start with anything that blocks indexing, because no amount of copywriting helps a page Google cannot see. Then prioritise pages with the most commercial value, usually your main categories and best-selling products. Keep a simple spreadsheet with each check, its status, the pages affected and who owns the fix.

1. Crawling and indexing

  • The robots.txt file does not block categories, products, CSS or JavaScript.
  • No important page carries a noindex tag by mistake, especially after a launch from a staging site.
  • An XML sitemap lists categories, products and key pages, and is submitted in Google Search Console.
  • Each page has a self-referencing canonical tag, and variant or filtered URLs canonicalise to the right parent.
  • There is one version of the site: HTTPS only, with or without www, and the other versions redirect.
  • Broken internal links and 404 errors are fixed or redirected to the closest relevant page.
  • Redirect chains are kept short, ideally one hop.
  • Search Console's page indexing report shows no unexpected spikes in excluded or duplicate pages.

2. Site structure and URLs

  • Main categories are reachable from the main navigation within a click or two of the home page.
  • URLs are short, readable and use hyphens, for example /womens-walking-boots/.
  • Breadcrumbs appear on category and product pages and reflect the real hierarchy.
  • Faceted filters do not create thousands of indexable URLs; only valuable filter combinations get their own indexable page.
  • Sorting and tracking parameters are not indexed.
  • Pagination is crawlable with normal links, so deeper products can still be found.
  • Out-of-stock products are handled deliberately: keep the page if the item returns, redirect if it is gone for good.

3. Category pages

Category pages often target the most valuable searches, such as "oak dining tables" or "men's running shoes". Check that:

  • Each main category targets one clear keyword theme, without two categories competing for the same term. Our keyword research guide explains how to map them.
  • The title tag includes the category keyword and a reason to click, within about 60 characters.
  • The meta description summarises the range and, where true, UK delivery or returns information.
  • The H1 is clear and matches the category.
  • There is a short, helpful intro and, where useful, buying advice below the products.
  • Related categories and guides are linked from the page.

4. Product pages

  • Every important product has a unique description, not just the manufacturer's text.
  • Product titles reflect how people search: brand, product type and key attributes.
  • Images are high quality, compressed, with descriptive file names and alt text.
  • Price, stock status, delivery and returns information are visible near the buy button.
  • Product structured data is present and accurate, including price in GBP and availability.
  • Variants (size, colour) do not create separate thin pages unless each genuinely deserves one.
  • Common questions are answered on the page, for example sizing, materials and care.

Our schema markup guide explains which structured data types are worth adding to product pages.

5. Speed, mobile and user experience

  • Core Web Vitals pass, or are improving, for mobile on key templates. See our Core Web Vitals guide.
  • Images are served at the right size in modern formats.
  • Unused apps, plugins and scripts are removed.
  • The site works well on mobile: readable text, tappable buttons and a simple checkout.
  • Pop-ups do not block content on arrival, especially on mobile.
  • Checkout friction is low, with guest checkout and familiar payment methods available.

Speed and usability affect sales as much as rankings. Treat them as one piece of work.

6. Content and links

  • Buying guides and comparisons answer questions your category pages cannot, and link to relevant products.
  • Content targets real search demand rather than a posting schedule.
  • Old, thin or overlapping blog posts are merged, improved or removed.
  • Your brand has a clear About page, contact details and policies, which help trust.
  • You earn links through genuinely newsworthy or useful content rather than buying them. Our digital PR and link building guide explains how.
  • Content is structured so AI search features can quote it: clear answers, headings and lists.

7. Marketplaces and shopping feeds

  • If you sell on Amazon or eBay, listings use the same keyword research and product data as your site.
  • Your product feed for Google Merchant Center is accurate, with titles and attributes that match the site.
  • Free product listings in Google Shopping are enabled where eligible.

We also offer marketplace listing SEO for Amazon and eBay sellers.

8. Tracking and reporting

  • Google Search Console is verified for the correct domain and the sitemap is submitted.
  • GA4 ecommerce events record product views, add to basket and purchases.
  • Organic revenue, not just traffic, is reported each month.
  • You review pages with high impressions and low click-through rate and improve their titles.
  • Major changes, such as launches or migrations, are logged so you can link them to ranking changes.

Common mistakes this checklist catches

When we audit online stores, the same problems come up again and again. Most are simple to fix once spotted:

  • Staging settings left on. A noindex tag or password protection that survived launch, quietly keeping pages out of Google.
  • Copied product descriptions. Manufacturer text used across the whole catalogue, giving Google no reason to rank the store.
  • Empty category pages. A title and a product grid, with no copy to explain what the range covers.
  • Filter bloat. Thousands of filtered URLs indexed, diluting the pages that matter.
  • Lost redirects. Old URLs from a previous platform returning 404 errors after a migration.
  • App and plugin build-up. Tools installed for a campaign and never removed, slowing every page.
  • Tracking gaps. No ecommerce events in GA4, so nobody knows which pages earn revenue.

Fixing these rarely needs a rebuild. It needs someone to work through the list methodically and check the result in Search Console afterwards.

Prioritising the fixes

Once you have worked through the checklist, you will probably have more fixes than time. Sort them by impact and effort. Anything that blocks indexing comes first. Next come quick wins on high-value pages, such as rewriting titles on categories with plenty of impressions but few clicks. Larger projects, like rewriting hundreds of product descriptions or restructuring navigation, can be planned in phases. Record each change with a date so you can see its effect later.

Platform notes: Shopify and WooCommerce

The checks are the same on any platform, but the fixes differ. Shopify handles hosting and much of the technical setup, but has fixed URL patterns and can create duplicate product paths through collections. WooCommerce gives full control but depends heavily on hosting, theme and plugin choices. Our Shopify SEO guide covers the Shopify specifics.

FAQs

What should an ecommerce SEO checklist include?

It should cover crawling and indexing, site structure, category pages, product pages, speed and mobile experience, content and links, shopping feeds and tracking. Work through them in that order.

Which pages matter most for ecommerce SEO?

Category pages usually target the most valuable searches, followed by best-selling product pages. Prioritise them before blog content.

How often should I run an ecommerce SEO audit?

Run a full audit at least once or twice a year and after any major change, such as a redesign, platform move or large catalogue update. Check Search Console monthly for new issues.

Do out-of-stock products hurt SEO?

Not if handled well. Keep the page live with clear stock information if the item will return. If it is discontinued, redirect it to the closest alternative or category.

Is this checklist different for Shopify and WooCommerce?

The checks are the same; only the way you fix them changes. Shopify limits some technical settings, while WooCommerce gives more control but needs more maintenance.

How long does ecommerce SEO take to work?

Technical fixes can be picked up quickly, but meaningful ranking and revenue changes typically take 3 to 6 months, depending on competition and the starting point.
