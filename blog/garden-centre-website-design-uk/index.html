Garden Centre Website Design: Plants, Seasons and Local Visits

Website design for UK garden centres and plant nurseries: opening times, seasonal content, selling plants online, click and collect, delivery and events.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Many garden centre visitors want practical details first: opening hours, location, parking and what is in stock or in season.
  • Selling live plants online raises particular questions about availability, delivery and plant health, so decide carefully what to sell online and how.
  • A garden centre site often serves several purposes at once, such as retail, a cafe, events and gift cards, so clear navigation matters.

Garden centres and plant nurseries sell something people love to see in person, yet more customers now browse online before visiting, and many expect to be able to buy plants, compost and gifts for delivery or collection. A good website supports both: it encourages visits and can also take orders. It has to keep up with the seasons too, since what customers want in spring is very different from what they want in December.

This guide covers website design for UK garden centres and plant nurseries, meaning businesses that grow or sell plants, not childcare nurseries, which we cover in our separate guide to nursery website design. If you design or build gardens for clients, our guide to landscaper website design is more relevant.

Put visiting information first

For a destination garden centre, a large share of visitors are simply planning a trip. Make the essentials easy to find from every page:

  • Opening hours, including seasonal and bank holiday changes.
  • Address, a map and directions.
  • Parking, accessibility and facilities such as toilets and baby changing.
  • Whether dogs are welcome.
  • Cafe or restaurant hours if they differ from the shop.

Keep this information accurate, especially around Christmas and Easter, and make sure it matches your Google Business Profile. Our guide to local SEO explains why consistent details matter.

Structure the site around how people browse

A garden centre may cover plants, garden furniture, tools, pet supplies, gifts, a cafe, events and more. Navigation should make the main areas obvious without overwhelming visitors. A typical top level might include plants, garden and outdoor living, gifts, cafe, events and a visit section. Within plants, customers often browse by type, such as perennials, shrubs, trees, houseplants and bedding, or by need, such as shade-tolerant plants or plants for pots. Our guide to website navigation covers structuring a large site.

Seasonal content and homepage updates

Gardening is seasonal, and a homepage that still promotes summer bedding in November looks neglected. Plan the homepage so key sections can be changed easily through the year, for example:

  • Spring: seeds, bedding plants, compost and lawn care.
  • Summer: outdoor living, barbecues and container plants.
  • Autumn: bulbs, trees and hedging.
  • Winter: Christmas trees, decorations, gifts and grottos where you run them.

Choose a content management system that lets your team update banners and featured products without a developer. Our guide to homepage design covers prioritising content, and our guide to seasonal sales preparation covers planning busy periods.

Selling plants online

Many garden centres and nurseries now sell online, but live plants bring challenges that standard products do not. Before building a shop, think about:

  • Availability. Plant stock changes daily and varies in size and quality. Can you keep online stock accurate?
  • Delivery. Will you deliver locally with your own vans, use a courier with suitable packaging, or both? Large items such as trees or bags of compost may need a different approach.
  • Plant size and condition. Describe pot size and typical height, and photograph real plants where possible.
  • Seasonality. Some plants are best sold only at certain times of year, such as bare-root trees.

Some businesses sell their full range online, while others sell only a selection that travels well, such as houseplants, seeds and dry goods. Starting with a focused range can be easier to manage. Our guides to stock availability display and delivery and returns pages cover related decisions.

Plant regulations and descriptions

Moving and selling plants in the UK is subject to plant health rules, and certain plants may require plant passports when sold to professional operators or sent by distance sale. Requirements depend on the plant and how it is sold, so check current guidance from the Animal and Plant Health Agency and GOV.UK before selling online. Be accurate in plant descriptions too: if a plant is toxic to pets or people, or invasive, say so clearly. Avoid making unqualified claims about how a plant will perform.

Click and collect and local delivery

For many garden centres, click and collect is a practical middle ground. Customers order online and pick up from a collection point, which avoids courier costs for heavy or fragile items. If you offer it, explain clearly how collection works, how long orders are held and where to go on arrival. For local delivery, a postcode checker showing whether you deliver to a given area, and the cost, can prevent disappointment at checkout.

Plant information and care guides

Helpful content can bring visitors to your site and support sales. Ideas include:

  • Care information on each plant page, such as light, watering and hardiness.
  • Seasonal "jobs in the garden" articles.
  • Guides to choosing plants for particular conditions.
  • Houseplant care advice.

Write from your own knowledge and avoid copying descriptions from suppliers or other websites, which adds little for readers or search engines. Our guide to product descriptions covers writing useful copy.

Events, workshops and the cafe

Many garden centres run events such as workshops, talks, wreath-making sessions, children's activities or Christmas grottos. Give events their own section with dates, prices and booking. Online booking reduces phone calls and helps you manage capacity; our guide to online booking systems covers the options. If you have a cafe, give it a page with menus, opening hours and any table booking, and keep allergen information accurate. Our guide to cafe website design covers this in more depth.

Gift cards and loyalty

Garden centre gift cards are popular presents. Selling them online, both physical and digital, can be a straightforward addition. If you run a loyalty scheme, explain how it works and how to join. See our guides to gift cards and vouchers and loyalty programmes.

Photography

A garden centre has a natural advantage: plants and displays are beautiful. Use real photos of your site, seasonal displays and plants rather than generic stock images. Photograph plants in good light and keep images current through the seasons. Compress images so pages load quickly on mobile. See our guide to website image optimisation.

Mobile use and accessibility

Many people check a garden centre website on a phone, whether planning a trip at the weekend or looking up a plant while standing in the aisle. Keep the mobile layout simple, make the phone number tappable and put opening hours within easy reach. Large product images are important for plants, but they should be compressed so pages still load quickly on a mobile connection.

Garden centres often attract a wide range of ages, so accessible design is worth particular attention. Use readable text sizes, good colour contrast and descriptive alternative text for images, and make sure forms and checkout can be completed with a keyboard or screen reader. Describe physical accessibility honestly on the visit page too, such as step-free access, wheelchair availability or the surfaces of outdoor areas, so visitors can plan ahead. Our guides to website accessibility and mobile website design cover practical steps.

Choosing a platform

If you plan to sell online, Shopify and WooCommerce are both common choices. Shopify also offers point of sale for physical shops, which can help keep stock in step; our guide to Shopify POS covers this. If you mainly want an informational site with events and visiting details, WordPress or a builder such as Squarespace may be enough. Consider how stock will be managed across shop floor and website. See our guide to Shopify vs WooCommerce.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Out-of-date opening hours, particularly over holidays.
  • Seasonal banners left up long after the season ends.
  • Selling plants online with stock levels that cannot be kept accurate.
  • Copied plant descriptions with no care information.
  • Stock photography instead of your own plants and displays.

FAQs

Should a garden centre sell plants online?

It can, but think carefully about stock accuracy, delivery and plant condition. Many start with a focused range or click and collect.

What should a garden centre homepage show?

Opening hours, location and current seasonal highlights, with clear routes to plants, the cafe, events and the shop.

Do I need plant passports to sell plants online?

Some plants and sales may require them. Check current GOV.UK and Animal and Plant Health Agency guidance for your situation.

Can a garden centre website take event bookings?

Yes. An online booking system can handle workshops and seasonal events and help manage capacity.

Which platform suits a garden centre?

Shopify or WooCommerce for selling online, or WordPress or a site builder for an information-led site. Stock management across shop and website is a key factor.
