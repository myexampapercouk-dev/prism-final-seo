Ecommerce Site Search: How to Help Shoppers Find the Right Products Faster

A UK guide to ecommerce site search: search bar design, autocomplete, synonyms, filters, zero-results pages and using search data to improve your online shop.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shoppers who use site search often know what they want, so a poor search experience can lose otherwise easy sales.
  • Good search handles misspellings, synonyms and product attributes, and never leaves a shopper at a dead end.
  • Search terms are a valuable source of insight into what customers want and the words they use.

For many online shops, the search bar is one of the most important features on the site. Shoppers who search usually have something specific in mind, which makes them valuable visitors. Yet site search is often left on default settings, returning irrelevant results, failing on simple misspellings or showing a blank "no results" page. Improving it can make the shop easier to use without changing anything else.

This guide explains what good ecommerce site search looks like, how to improve relevance, how to handle zero-results searches and how to use search data to improve your shop.

Why site search matters

Navigation suits shoppers who want to browse. Search suits those who know what they want, or who do not want to work out how your categories are organised. If search fails, these shoppers may assume you do not stock the product and leave. For shops with large or varied catalogues, search can be the main route to products. Our website navigation guide covers the browsing side.

Make the search bar easy to find and use

  • Place search where shoppers expect it, typically in the header.
  • On larger catalogues, consider showing a visible search box rather than hiding it behind an icon.
  • On mobile, make sure search is easy to reach and the box is large enough to tap and type into. Our mobile website design guide has more on this.
  • Use helpful placeholder text, such as an example of what can be searched.

Autocomplete and suggestions

Autocomplete suggests search terms, categories or products as the shopper types. Done well, it speeds up searching, corrects spelling before the search is submitted and helps shoppers discover the language your shop uses. Showing product images and prices in suggestions can let shoppers go straight to the right product. Keep suggestions relevant and quick; slow or cluttered autocomplete can be more frustrating than none.

Improve relevance

Relevance is about showing the products a shopper most likely wants first. Common improvements include:

  • Typo tolerance: returning sensible results for minor misspellings.
  • Synonyms: mapping terms customers use to those in your catalogue, such as "sofa" and "settee" or "trainers" and "sneakers".
  • Plurals and variations: treating "dress" and "dresses" the same way.
  • Attribute search: understanding queries that include colour, size, material or brand, such as "black leather boots".
  • Product codes: letting trade or repeat customers search by SKU or part number.
  • Merchandising rules: promoting in-stock or relevant products and demoting out-of-stock items where appropriate.

How much of this is possible depends on your platform. Built-in search on Shopify, WooCommerce and other platforms varies in capability, and dedicated search apps or plugins can add more advanced features. The right choice depends on catalogue size, budget and how important search is to your customers.

Good product data makes search work

Search can only match what is in your product data. Clear product titles, accurate descriptions, consistent attributes and well-organised tags all improve results. If products are missing colours, sizes or materials as structured data, attribute searches and filters will struggle. Tidying product data often improves search, filtering and SEO at the same time. Our product page SEO guide covers writing product content.

Design the results page

A search results page should work much like a good category page:

  • Show the search term clearly and the number of results.
  • Offer relevant filters and sorting options.
  • Display product images, names, prices and availability clearly.
  • Show matching categories or content where helpful, such as size guides.

Our ecommerce category page design guide covers filters and product grids in more detail.

Never leave a dead end

Zero-results pages are where many search journeys fail. Rather than a blank message, help the shopper continue:

  • Suggest corrected spellings or related terms.
  • Show popular products or categories.
  • Offer a way to contact you if they cannot find what they need.

Then review zero-results searches regularly. They often reveal missing synonyms, products you could stock or terms your catalogue does not use.

Use search data

What shoppers type into your search box is direct insight into what they want, in their own words. Review popular searches, searches with no results and searches followed by an exit. You can track site search in GA4; our GA4 setup guide explains the basics. Insights can feed into navigation, product ranges, content and keyword research for SEO.

Search on mobile

Mobile shoppers often rely on search more than desktop shoppers, because browsing deep menus on a small screen is fiddly. A few points matter particularly on mobile:

  • Make the search icon or box clearly visible in the header, not tucked inside a menu.
  • Open a full-width search field with the keyboard ready when search is tapped.
  • Keep autocomplete suggestions large enough to tap accurately.
  • Make filters easy to open, apply and clear on the results page.
  • Remember recent searches where your platform supports it, so shoppers can return quickly.

Test search on real phones, not just a resized desktop browser, as keyboard behaviour and tap targets can differ.

Search and SEO

Internal search results pages are generally best kept out of search engine indexes, as they can create large numbers of thin, near-duplicate pages. Platforms often handle this by default, but it is worth checking. Instead, use what you learn from search terms to improve indexable pages: if many shoppers search for a type of product you group under a broader category, a dedicated, well-written category page may serve them and search engines better. Our ecommerce SEO checklist covers related technical points.

Searching beyond products

Shoppers do not only search for products. They may also look for delivery information, returns, size guides, care instructions or your contact details. If search returns only products, these visitors may assume the information does not exist. Consider whether your search can include help pages, blog articles or guides, either mixed into results or shown in a separate section. This can reduce customer service queries and keep shoppers on the site.

Choosing a search solution

When deciding whether built-in search is enough or a dedicated search tool is worth considering, think about:

  • Catalogue size and complexity: a few dozen products may need little more than basic search; thousands of products with many variants usually benefit from more.
  • How customers search: by product name, by problem, by code or by attributes such as size and colour.
  • Merchandising needs: whether you want control over which products appear first for key terms.
  • Performance: search scripts can add weight, so check the effect on page speed.
  • Ongoing effort: synonym lists and rules need maintenance to stay useful.
  • Cost: many tools charge ongoing fees, sometimes based on catalogue size or search volume.

Before switching, it is often worth fixing product data and testing what the existing search can already do with better configuration.

Testing and improving over time

Site search is not a set-and-forget feature. Products change, seasons change and customers use new terms. A simple routine might include reviewing top searches and zero-results searches monthly, adding synonyms for common mismatches, checking that new products appear for the expected terms and testing search after any theme or platform update. Small, regular adjustments tend to keep search useful.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving search on default settings and never testing it.
  • Hiding the search bar on mobile.
  • Returning nothing for small misspellings.
  • Showing a blank zero-results page.
  • Ignoring search data.
  • Expecting search to fix poor product data.

FAQs

Does my online shop need advanced search?

Not always. Small catalogues may work well with built-in search. Larger or more complex catalogues often benefit from more advanced features such as typo tolerance and synonyms.

What is a zero-results search?

A search that returns no products. These are worth reviewing, as they often reveal missing synonyms, spelling issues or products customers want.

Can site search data help SEO?

Yes. The terms shoppers search for on your site can inform keyword research, category planning and content ideas.

Should out-of-stock products appear in search results?

It depends. Some shops show them lower down with clear availability; others hide them. Consider what is most helpful to your customers.

How do I test my site search?

Try common queries, misspellings, synonyms and attribute searches yourself, and review real search terms from your analytics.
