Building a Website for a Tutoring Business in the UK

How to build a website for a UK tutoring business: subjects and levels, reassuring parents, safeguarding, booking, children's data, local search, platforms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A tutoring website usually has two audiences, parents and students, and needs to reassure the person paying while speaking to the person learning.
  • Clear subjects, levels, formats, tutor details and practical information help families decide quickly.
  • Safeguarding information, children's data and payment terms need care, and regulatory points should be checked with official guidance.

Families looking for a tutor are often anxious. A child may be falling behind, an exam may be approaching or a parent may simply want extra support. They will usually compare several options, and the tutoring business that explains itself most clearly and reassuringly has a real advantage. That applies whether you are an independent tutor building a reputation in your area, or a small tutoring company with a team of tutors working online or in person.

This guide is for UK tutors and tutoring businesses planning a new website or improving an existing one. It covers who the site is for, the pages to include, how to present subjects and tutors, safeguarding information, booking and payments, handling children's data, local search and platform choice.

Two audiences: parents and students

For school-age tutoring, the parent or carer usually finds the website, compares options and pays. The student is the one who will actually sit through the sessions. Adult learners, such as university students or people preparing for professional exams, are both. A good tutoring website keeps this in mind:

  • Parents want to know that tutors are suitable, sessions are safe, the approach works for their child and the practical details fit their family.
  • Students, especially older ones, want to know sessions will be helpful, relevant to their course and not a chore.
  • Adult learners want clarity on expertise, flexibility and cost.

Most sites lead with parents, while including content that older students will find relevant, such as exam board specifics and study tips.

The pages a tutoring website needs

Home page

State clearly what you tutor, for which levels, and whether sessions are online, in person or both. If you work locally, name the area. Then provide a clear next step, such as booking an initial consultation or sending an enquiry.

Subjects and levels

Families search with specific needs in mind: GCSE maths, A level chemistry, 11 plus preparation, KS2 English, or adult numeracy. Dedicated pages for your main subjects or levels help visitors find what they need, and help search engines understand what you offer. On each page, explain what you cover, which exam boards or curricula you are familiar with, and how sessions are structured.

Tutors

For an independent tutor, this is your about page. For a tutoring company, a page introducing tutors can be very reassuring. Include relevant qualifications, teaching experience and subjects, but only with each tutor's agreement, and only accurate details.

How it works

Explain the journey from first enquiry to regular sessions: an initial conversation, perhaps an assessment, matching with a tutor, session frequency and how progress is shared with parents. Describe what you genuinely do rather than an idealised process.

Fees and policies

Practical information about costs, payment, cancellations and missed sessions. More below.

Safeguarding

A clear page or section on how you keep children safe. More below.

Contact

Simple, with a form, email and phone where practical.

Reassuring parents

Trust is central for tutoring. Things that help parents feel confident include:

  • Tutor credentials, such as degrees, teaching qualifications or relevant experience, described accurately.
  • A clear approach to how you assess needs, plan sessions and track progress.
  • Genuine testimonials from parents or students, published with permission. Avoid naming children. Our testimonials page guide has more tips.
  • Realistic expectations. Avoid promising specific grades or exam results. Outcomes depend on many factors, and marketing claims generally need to be supportable under UK advertising rules.
  • Professional presentation, with a clear contact route, business details and up-to-date information.

Our article on website trust signals covers more general ideas.

Safeguarding information

Parents reasonably want to know how children are kept safe, especially when sessions take place one-to-one, at home or online. What you say must be accurate. Depending on your setup, you might explain:

  • Whether tutors hold criminal record checks and what level, if applicable.
  • Your safeguarding policy and who to contact with a concern.
  • How online sessions are conducted, such as whether parents can be present or whether sessions are recorded.
  • Expectations around communication between tutors and students.

Rules on criminal record checks and safeguarding responsibilities can be complex and depend on the work involved. The tutoring sector in England is not regulated in the same way as schools, so it is worth reading official guidance from GOV.UK and the Disclosure and Barring Service, or the equivalent bodies in Scotland and Northern Ireland, and taking advice where needed. Never state that checks are in place unless they are.

Fees, booking and payment

Unclear pricing is a common reason families move on to another option. Consider including:

  • Session lengths and how fees are charged, or how they are worked out if they vary by level.
  • Whether you sell individual sessions, blocks or monthly plans.
  • Payment methods and when payment is due.
  • Your cancellation and missed session policy.
  • Any arrangements for school holidays.

If you publish fees, keep them up to date. Our pricing page design guide explains different ways to present them.

For booking, many tutoring businesses start with an enquiry form, because matching a student with the right tutor needs a conversation. Once a student is set up, a scheduling tool can make regular bookings easier. Our online booking systems guide and contact forms guide cover both approaches. Consumer law can give customers cancellation rights for services bought online or at a distance, so check how these apply to your terms.

Children's data and privacy

Tutoring businesses often handle information about children: names, ages, schools, learning needs and sometimes details of special educational needs or health conditions. This needs care.

  • Collect only the information you need, particularly on enquiry forms.
  • Provide a clear privacy notice explaining what you collect, why and how long you keep it.
  • Be aware that information about health or additional needs may be treated as special category data under UK GDPR.
  • Use secure tools for forms, scheduling, payments and video calls.
  • If your website or any online service is likely to be used by children directly, look at the ICO's Children's Code to see whether it applies.

The ICO publishes guidance for small businesses and on children's data. It is worth reading and, where you are unsure, taking advice.

Local search and online visibility

Many families search for tutors near them, even for online sessions. Useful steps include:

  • Including your town or area on relevant pages if you offer in-person tutoring.
  • Setting up a Google Business Profile if you are eligible, with accurate details.
  • Creating subject and level pages that match how people search.
  • Publishing helpful content such as exam preparation tips, revision timetables or guides to entrance exams, written carefully and kept current.

Our local SEO guide explains the basics, and our education marketing guide covers the wider sector.

Design considerations

  • Calm, clear design rather than overly busy or childish visuals, as parents are usually the decision makers.
  • Mobile-friendly pages, since many parents search on their phones.
  • Accessible content, including readable fonts and good contrast, which helps families with different needs. See our website accessibility guide.
  • Real photos of tutors, used with consent, rather than generic stock images. Take extra care before using images of children, and get clear consent from parents.

Choosing a platform

An independent tutor may be well served by Squarespace or Wix, which make it easy to build and update a small site. A tutoring company with several subjects, many tutors, location pages or a resource library may benefit from WordPress, which handles larger amounts of content well. If you plan to sell courses or learning materials, factor that into your choice. Our website builder vs custom website guide explains the trade-offs.

FAQs

What should a tutoring website include?

Subjects and levels, tutor details, how sessions work, fees and policies, safeguarding information, genuine testimonials, a privacy notice and an easy way to enquire.

Should tutors put their prices online?

Many do, as families often compare costs. If fees vary by level or format, explain how they are worked out and keep the information current.

Do I need to mention DBS checks on my website?

Parents often look for this information. Only state what is true, and check official guidance on which checks apply to your work.

Can I promise exam results on my website?

It is best not to. Results depend on many factors, and marketing claims generally need to be supportable, so describe your approach and realistic expectations.

How do I get more tutoring enquiries from Google?

Create clear subject and level pages, mention your area if you tutor in person, set up a Google Business Profile if eligible and publish helpful content.
