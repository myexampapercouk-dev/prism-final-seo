Web Design Quebec City: How Remote Bilingual Website Projects Work for Quebec City Businesses

Web design for Quebec City businesses: how remote bilingual website projects work, French content and language rules, Law 25 privacy, timelines and costs.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Web design for a Quebec City business should start with French as the main language, with a clean bilingual structure, fast pages and clear calls to action.
  • French content should come from your own team or a professional translator; check Quebec's language and Law 25 privacy requirements with official guidance or your adviser.
  • We are a UK-based studio building websites remotely with no Quebec office; small sites typically cost £1,000 to £2,000 and take 3 to 5 weeks, with other currencies quoted on request.

Web design for a Quebec City business means building a fast, clear website that works first in French, serves English-speaking visitors well where that makes sense, and turns visits into calls, bookings and sales. The design, build and testing all happen online, so a remote studio can deliver the project as long as the French content and local requirements are handled properly.

We are a UK-based studio that designs and builds websites remotely for Canadian businesses. We have no Quebec City office and no office in Canada. We write and work in English, and French content comes from your own team or a professional translator. This guide explains what good web design looks like for a Quebec City business, how bilingual sites should be structured, the language and privacy points to check, and what a project costs.

What web design in Quebec City needs to cover

Quebec City's economy leans on public administration, tourism and hospitality, healthcare, education, insurance and a growing technology and video game sector, alongside many independent shops, restaurants, trades and professional practices. Old Quebec and the surrounding districts draw visitors from across Canada and abroad, while local services depend on residents in areas such as Sainte-Foy, Charlesbourg, Beauport and across the river in Lévis.

That mix means most websites have two audiences: a mainly French-speaking local market and, for many businesses, English-speaking visitors or clients from elsewhere. A good website should cover:

  • Clear structure: service, product or room pages that answer what people need to know before they contact you.
  • French first: French as the main language, with English versions added where they serve real customers.
  • Speed and mobile use: lean pages and optimised images, because most visitors arrive on phones.
  • Calls to action: booking, calling, requesting a quote or buying, made obvious on every key page.
  • Search foundations: clean URLs, headings, page titles and language tags so search engines understand each version.
  • Ownership: domain, hosting and content in your name, with training so your team can edit pages.

Building a bilingual French and English website

A bilingual site is more than a translated copy. Each language version should feel natural to its readers and be easy for search engines to understand. We usually recommend:

  • Separate URLs per language: for example a French section and an English section, so each page can rank in its own language.
  • Language tags: hreflang annotations that link each page to its equivalent in the other language.
  • A simple language switcher: visible on every page and taking visitors to the equivalent page, not back to the homepage.
  • Translated metadata: page titles, descriptions, image alt text, forms, error messages and emails, not just body text.
  • Consistent design: layouts that allow for French text, which is often longer than English.

We build the structure and the English content, and your team or translator supplies the French. Our guide to multilingual website design explains the technical side in more depth, and our guide to web design in Montreal covers how bilingual projects work elsewhere in Quebec.

Language and privacy rules to check

Quebec has its own rules that affect websites. As general information only, and not legal advice:

  • Language requirements: Quebec's language laws generally require commercial content, including websites, to be available in French, and French should usually be at least as prominent as any other language. Check current guidance from the official language office or your adviser for what applies to your business.
  • Law 25: Quebec's private-sector privacy law sets requirements for how businesses handle personal information, including transparency about data collection, privacy policies and how tracking technologies such as analytics and advertising cookies are used.
  • Federal rules: PIPEDA can also apply in some situations, such as personal information crossing provincial or national borders.
  • Email: if your website collects email sign-ups, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) sets rules on consent and unsubscribe options.

We can build cookie consent tools, clear privacy pages and consent checkboxes, but the wording and decisions about compliance rest with you and your adviser. Our guide to cookie banner design explains how to make consent clear without frustrating visitors.

Websites for tourism and hospitality

Quebec City's visitor economy is a major part of local business. Hotels, inns, tour operators, restaurants and attractions compete with large booking platforms for the same visitors. A direct website helps by showing what makes you different, answering practical questions about location, parking, access and seasons, and making booking simple. Winter and summer can bring very different visitors, so seasonal content and offers should be easy to update.

Our guide to increasing hotel direct bookings covers the features and content that encourage visitors to book with you directly.

Choosing a platform: WordPress, Shopify or a simple build

The right platform depends on what your business needs to do online and who will update the site. For most Quebec City service businesses, WordPress is a flexible choice: it handles bilingual content well with a reliable multilingual plugin, has a mature editor your team can learn quickly and gives you full ownership of your site and content. For shops, Shopify is usually simpler to run, with built-in checkout, Canadian tax settings and translation support for French product pages.

Very small sites with a handful of pages may not need a full content management system at all, especially if content changes rarely. Whatever the platform, we keep the number of add-ons low, because each extra plugin or app adds cost, maintenance and potential speed problems. We also set up hosting, backups and updates so the site stays secure after launch, and explain clearly what ongoing care it needs.

Accessibility and usability

A website should work for everyone, including people using screen readers, keyboard navigation or larger text. We build with clear headings, readable contrast, descriptive link text and labelled forms in both languages. Accessibility expectations and rules vary by sector and organisation in Canada, so check what applies to you, but good accessibility helps every visitor and supports search visibility too.

Getting found after launch

A new website needs search visibility. For Quebec City businesses, that usually means French keyword research, a complete Google Business Profile in French, local citations and helpful content in both languages where relevant. Paid search can fill gaps while SEO builds. Our guides to SEO in Montreal and Google Ads in Montreal explain how search marketing works for bilingual Quebec audiences, and the same principles apply in Quebec City.

We use AI-powered tools to speed up tasks such as drafting page outlines and checking technical issues, but a person reviews everything before it goes live, and French content still comes from your team or translator.

How a remote web design project works

The UK is typically five hours ahead of Quebec City. We design and build during the UK day, and calls take place in your morning. A typical project runs like this:

  1. Discovery call and written brief covering goals, audiences, pages and languages.
  2. Sitemap and page plan agreed, including which pages need French and English versions.
  3. Design concepts for key pages, reviewed on a shared link.
  4. Build on a staging site, with English content from us and French content from your team or translator.
  5. Testing on phones, tablets and desktops, plus checks on forms, language tags and consent tools.
  6. Launch, redirects from any old site, and training for your team.
