Website Design for Therapists and Counsellors in the UK: A Practical Guide

Website design guide for UK therapists and counsellors: pages to include, building trust, booking, privacy, accessibility, local search and choosing a platform.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A therapist's website should help a nervous visitor feel understood, see clearly how you work and know exactly how to make contact.
  • Plain language, honest descriptions of your approach and visible professional details build more trust than polished marketing copy.
  • Contact forms and booking tools handle sensitive information, so privacy, security and data protection need care from the start.

For many people, contacting a therapist or counsellor for the first time is a big step. They may have been thinking about it for weeks, searching late at night on a phone, reading several websites before deciding who to approach. Your website is often where that decision is made. A good therapist website does not need to be elaborate. It needs to feel calm, explain clearly how you work, show that you are properly qualified and make getting in touch feel easy and safe.

This guide is for private therapists, counsellors, psychotherapists and small practices in the UK who are planning a new website or improving an existing one. It covers what to include, how to write it, the practical and privacy points to think about and how to help the right people find you.

What a therapist website needs to do

Most visitors arrive with a few unspoken questions:

  • Do you work with the kind of difficulty I am facing?
  • Will I feel comfortable with you?
  • Are you properly trained and accountable?
  • How does it work, how much does it cost and where or how do sessions happen?
  • What happens when I get in touch?

A website that answers these clearly and gently will usually do more good than one that relies on striking design. Everything else supports these goals.

Pages to include

Home page

Your home page should quickly tell visitors who you help, where or how you work and how to make contact. A short, warm introduction in your own voice, a clear photo and a simple route to the next step are often enough. Avoid filling it with jargon or long lists of modalities before the visitor knows who you are.

About page

People choose a therapist partly on how they feel about them. Your about page can describe your background, why you do this work and what sessions with you are like. Include your real qualifications and any professional body membership or registration, described accurately. If you are a member of a professional body, follow its guidance on how membership may be described.

How I work or approach

Explain your therapeutic approach in plain English. Rather than naming a model and stopping there, describe what it means in practice: how sessions usually run, whether you focus on the past, the present or both, and how you and the client decide on goals. Be careful to describe what therapy may help with rather than promising outcomes.

What I help with

Many therapists list areas of work such as anxiety, bereavement, relationship difficulties or work stress. Short, separate pages for your main areas can help visitors recognise themselves and can help search engines understand what you offer. Write them with care: be supportive and factual, avoid making clinical claims you cannot support and do not suggest therapy is a substitute for urgent help.

Fees and practical details

Uncertainty about cost puts people off. State your session fees, session length, any reduced-fee places, cancellation policy and how payment works. Explain whether you offer in-person, online or phone sessions, and where you are based if you see clients in person, including access information.

Contact page

Make contact as straightforward as possible. Give more than one option where you can, such as a simple form, email and phone, and explain what happens next, for example that you usually reply within a set time and offer a short initial call. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design in more detail.

Urgent help signposting

Private therapists are not usually an emergency service. A brief, clearly visible note pointing people in crisis towards NHS 111, their GP or emergency services is a responsible addition, ideally on the contact page and in the footer.

Writing for a nervous visitor

Tone matters on a therapist's site more than almost anywhere else. A few principles help:

  • Write as you speak. First person, warm and plain. Imagine you are talking to someone in your first session.
  • Lead with the person, not the method. Describe the feelings and situations people bring before the theory you use.
  • Keep paragraphs short. Many visitors read on a phone, sometimes while anxious.
  • Avoid pressure. Countdown offers, aggressive pop-ups and hard-sell phrasing feel out of place. An invitation works better than a push.
  • Be honest about fit. It is fine to say who you may not be the right therapist for. That builds trust.

Design that feels calm and trustworthy

Good design for a therapy practice tends to be quiet and uncluttered: plenty of white space, readable text, a restrained colour palette and real photographs rather than generic stock images. A genuine photo of you, and of the room if you see clients in person, helps people imagine arriving for a first session.

Make sure the site works well on a phone, loads quickly and has a contact route that is easy to find on every page. Our guide to mobile website design explains the basics.

Accessibility

People visiting a therapist's website may be dealing with stress, low mood, neurodivergence, sight loss or other conditions that make some websites hard to use. Good contrast, readable font sizes, descriptive link text, clear headings and forms that work with a keyboard and screen reader all help. Accessibility also relates to legal duties under the Equality Act 2010, although how they apply to an individual practice depends on the circumstances. Our website accessibility guide covers practical steps.

Privacy and data protection

Enquiries to a therapist often contain sensitive personal information, which may include health data. Under UK GDPR, health data is special category data and needs extra care. Points to consider include:

  • Keep forms short. Ask only what you need to respond, such as name, contact details and preferred session type. Invite people to share more in a call rather than in a form field.
  • Secure the site. Use HTTPS throughout, keep software updated and limit who can access enquiry data.
  • Know where data goes. Form submissions may be stored in your website, emailed to you or passed to booking or practice management tools. Understand each step.
  • Publish a clear privacy notice explaining what you collect, why, how long you keep it and people's rights.
  • Be careful with tracking. Analytics and advertising tags on pages about specific conditions may reveal sensitive information. Use consent banners correctly and consider whether you need those tags at all. Our cookie banner guide explains the basics.

The ICO publishes guidance for small organisations, and many practitioners need to pay the data protection fee. This is a general overview, not legal advice, so check the ICO's guidance or take professional advice for your situation.

Online booking

Some therapists like clients to book an initial consultation online. It can reduce back-and-forth emails and suit people who prefer not to phone. Others prefer a short conversation first to check fit. Either is valid. If you use a booking tool, make sure it is secure, works well on mobile, sends clear confirmations and fits your cancellation policy. Our guide to online booking systems compares the main approaches.

Helping the right people find you

Many people search for terms such as "counsellor near me", "therapist in" a town or "online counselling for anxiety". A few steps help you appear for the right searches:

  • Set up a Google Business Profile if you see clients in person, with accurate details, opening hours and categories. If you work only online, check Google's guidelines on eligibility. Our Google Business Profile guide explains how it works.
  • Use clear page titles that say what you do and where, for example counselling in a named town.
  • Create focused pages for your main areas of work, each with genuinely helpful content.
  • Keep listings consistent across professional directories you choose to use, with the same name, address and phone number.
  • Consider helpful articles that answer common questions, such as what happens in a first session, written carefully and without clinical claims.

Advertising health services is subject to advertising codes, so if you plan to run ads, check the CAP Code guidance from the ASA. Our SEO and GEO service page explains how we approach search visibility.

Choosing a platform

Most therapist websites are small: perhaps five to fifteen pages. Hosted builders such as Squarespace and Wix can work well if you want to manage the site yourself with minimal upkeep. WordPress gives more control and suits practices planning a larger site, several practitioners or more content. Whatever you choose, make sure the domain and accounts are in your name. Our guides to website builders vs custom websites explains the trade-offs, and our guide to domain and email setup covers the essentials of a professional email address.

Therapist website checklist

  • A clear statement of who you help, how and where.
  • A warm about page with real qualifications and memberships described accurately.
  • Plain-English explanation of your approach.
  • Fees, session length, formats and cancellation policy.
  • Simple, secure contact options and an explanation of next steps.
  • Urgent help signposting.
  • A clear privacy notice and careful use of tracking.
  • Good accessibility and mobile performance.

FAQs

What should a counsellor's website include?

A clear home page, an about page with qualifications, a plain explanation of your approach, the areas you help with, fees and practical details, contact options and urgent help signposting.

Should therapists put their fees on their website?

Many do, because uncertainty about cost can stop people getting in touch. Clear fees, session length and cancellation terms help visitors decide.

Do therapists need a privacy notice on their website?

If you collect personal data through your site, you generally need one. Enquiries may include health information, which UK GDPR treats as special category data, so check the ICO's guidance.

Is online booking a good idea for a therapy practice?

It can be, especially for initial consultations. Some therapists prefer a short call first. Choose whichever suits how you work and keep the tool secure.

How can a therapist get more clients from Google?

Set up a Google Business Profile if eligible, use clear page titles, create helpful pages for your main areas of work and keep your details consistent across directories.

Which platform is best for a therapist website?

Squarespace or Wix suit a small site you manage yourself. WordPress suits larger practices or more content. The best choice depends on who will maintain it.
