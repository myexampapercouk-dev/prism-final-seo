Ecommerce Loyalty Programmes: Rewarding Repeat Customers Without Eroding Margin

How online stores can design a loyalty programme that suits their customers, from points and tiers to perks, plus setup, costs to weigh and pitfalls to avoid.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A loyalty programme rewards customers for coming back, but only works if the rewards matter to them and the costs suit your margins.
  • Points, tiers, paid memberships and non-discount perks each suit different kinds of store.
  • Clear rules, simple wording and honest terms keep a programme trusted and easier to manage.

Winning a new customer takes effort and, often, advertising spend. Encouraging someone who has already bought to come back can be one of the most valuable things an online store does. A loyalty programme is one way to do that: a structured reason for customers to return, spend again and feel recognised for doing so.

Loyalty programmes are not right for every store, though, and a poorly planned one can quietly eat into margins without changing customer behaviour much. This guide covers the main types of programme, how to decide whether one suits your business, how to design it and how to set it up on common ecommerce platforms.

What is an ecommerce loyalty programme?

A loyalty programme rewards customers for repeat purchases or other actions, such as creating an account, leaving a review or referring a friend. Rewards might be discounts, free delivery, early access, gifts or exclusive products. The aim is to give customers a reason to choose your store again rather than a competitor.

Is a loyalty programme right for your store?

Before building anything, it is worth asking a few questions:

  • Do customers naturally buy more than once? Stores selling consumables, such as food, beauty, pet supplies or stationery, tend to have more natural repeat purchases than stores selling items bought once in many years.
  • Can your margins support rewards? Every reward has a cost. If margins are tight, a points programme offering discounts may not be sustainable.
  • Do you have the capacity to run it? Programmes need promoting, monitoring and occasional adjustment.
  • Would something simpler work? Sometimes excellent service, a thoughtful post-purchase email sequence or a subscription option does more than a formal programme.

If customers rarely buy twice, a referral scheme or a focus on reviews and word of mouth may be a better fit than a points programme.

Types of loyalty programme

Points-based programmes

Customers earn points for each purchase, and sometimes for other actions, then redeem them for discounts or rewards. Points programmes are familiar and flexible, but the value of points needs to be clear. If customers cannot easily work out what their points are worth, the motivation fades.

Tiered programmes

Customers move up levels as they spend more, unlocking better rewards at each tier. Tiers can motivate your most engaged customers and make them feel recognised, but they work best when there are enough repeat buyers to reach the higher levels.

Paid memberships

Customers pay a fee for ongoing benefits such as free delivery, member pricing or exclusive products. This can suit stores with frequent buyers, but the benefits must clearly outweigh the cost to the customer. Recurring payments also bring extra responsibilities around clear terms and easy cancellation, so check the current consumer protection requirements or take advice.

Value-based and perk programmes

Not every reward needs to be a discount. Early access to new products, invitations to events, members-only content, birthday treats, gift wrapping or donations to a cause connected with your brand can all make customers feel valued without simply cutting prices.

Referral programmes

Customers are rewarded for introducing friends, often with both sides receiving a benefit. Referral schemes can sit alongside or replace a loyalty programme, especially for stores where repeat purchases are infrequent.

Designing a programme that works

Make the rewards meaningful

The reward must matter to your customers. Think about what they value: saving money, getting things first, feeling part of a community or receiving something special. Speaking to customers or surveying them can help.

Keep the rules simple

Customers should understand the programme in a sentence or two. "Earn points on every order and use them for money off your next purchase" is clear. Complicated multipliers, expiry rules and exceptions make programmes harder to trust and harder to manage.

Make early rewards reachable

If the first reward takes many purchases to reach, many customers will lose interest. A welcome reward for joining, or a first reward within reach of a typical second order, can help people see the value quickly.

Work out the costs

Before launching, model what the programme might cost. Consider the value of rewards, how many customers might redeem them, the cost of any loyalty app or plugin and the time needed to run it. Look at your margins product by product, since some items may not support discounts. Your accountant can help you consider how rewards and unredeemed points should be treated in your accounts, as the tax and accounting treatment can vary.

Write clear terms

Publish clear terms covering how rewards are earned and redeemed, whether points expire, what happens with returns and refunds and how you might change the programme. Honest, easy-to-find terms help maintain trust and reduce disputes. UK consumer protection law applies to how promotions and rewards are described, so make sure claims are accurate.

Setting up a loyalty programme on your platform

Shopify, WooCommerce and other ecommerce platforms support loyalty programmes through apps, plugins or custom development. When choosing an approach, consider:

  • how points or rewards appear in the customer account, basket and checkout,
  • whether rewards can be applied easily without complicated codes,
  • how the programme connects with your email platform,
  • the effect on page speed, since some apps add scripts to every page, and
  • how easy it would be to change or remove later.

Loyalty programmes usually depend on customers having accounts. Our guide to customer accounts and wishlists covers making accounts genuinely useful without forcing them on shoppers who prefer guest checkout.

Promoting the programme

A programme nobody knows about will not change behaviour. Places to mention it include:

  • a dedicated page explaining how it works,
  • product pages and the basket, showing what a customer would earn,
  • order confirmation and post-purchase emails; see our guide to transactional emails,
  • regular marketing emails reminding members of their balance or perks, and
  • the customer account area.

Our guide to ecommerce email marketing covers using email to keep customers engaged, including messages about rewards. Remember that marketing emails generally need appropriate consent under UK rules.

Measuring success

To understand whether the programme is worth it, track measures such as:

  • how many customers join,
  • how often members buy compared with non-members,
  • how many rewards are redeemed,
  • the total cost of rewards against the extra revenue from members, and
  • how average order values change.

Be careful when comparing members and non-members. Your most loyal customers may be the ones most likely to join, so members might buy more often regardless of the programme. Looking at behaviour before and after joining, or testing changes carefully, gives a fairer picture. Setting up ecommerce tracking properly in GA4 helps; see our GA4 setup guide.

Common pitfalls

  • Rewards that cost more than they return. Generous discounts can erode margins without enough extra spending to compensate.
  • Rewards too hard to reach. If customers cannot see a realistic path to a reward, they disengage.
  • Confusing rules. Complex point values and exceptions undermine trust.
  • Training customers to wait for discounts. Programmes based purely on money off can make full-price purchases less appealing.
  • Launching and forgetting. Programmes need reviewing and refreshing.
  • Changing rules without warning. Reducing reward values suddenly can damage goodwill.

Loyalty beyond the programme

A formal programme is only one part of customer loyalty. Reliable delivery, accurate product information, easy returns, friendly service and a well-designed website all give customers reasons to come back. A loyalty programme works best on top of those foundations rather than as a substitute for them. Our guides to website trust signals and delivery and returns pages cover some of these foundations.

FAQs

Do small online stores need a loyalty programme?

Not necessarily. It depends on whether customers naturally buy more than once and whether margins can support rewards. Good service and email follow-up may be enough at first.

What is the best type of loyalty programme?

There is no single best type. Points suit frequent buyers, tiers suit stores with highly engaged customers and perks suit brands wanting to avoid heavy discounting.

Can I run a loyalty programme on Shopify or WooCommerce?

Yes. Both platforms support loyalty programmes through apps, plugins or custom development.

Should loyalty points expire?

Expiry can help manage costs, but it should be clearly explained in your terms. Some stores choose not to expire points to keep things simple.

How do I know if my loyalty programme is working?

Track membership, repeat purchases, redemptions and the cost of rewards against extra revenue, taking care to account for already-loyal customers joining.
