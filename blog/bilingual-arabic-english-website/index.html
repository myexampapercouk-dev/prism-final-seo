Bilingual Arabic-English Website Design: Layout, Content and SEO

Planning a bilingual Arabic-English website? Right-to-left layout, Arabic fonts, URL structure, translation workflow, SEO and cost, explained step by step.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A bilingual Arabic-English website needs a proper right-to-left layout for Arabic, not just translated text dropped into an English design.
  • Give each language its own URLs, metadata and carefully written content, and link the versions so search engines show the right one to each visitor.
  • Budget for Arabic copywriting or professional translation and for ongoing upkeep, because every update has to be made in both languages.

A bilingual Arabic-English website gives each language a complete, properly designed version: English reading left to right, Arabic reading right to left, each with its own URLs, navigation, metadata and well-written content. Done well, visitors in Dubai, Riyadh or Doha can switch language and feel equally at home, and search engines can show the right version to the right person.

Why bilingual matters in the Gulf

Gulf audiences are mixed. Many residents use English day to day, while Arabic-speaking customers often prefer to read, search and buy in Arabic. Government bodies, larger companies and many consumers expect to see Arabic, and an English-only site can feel less local and less trustworthy to part of your market.

A bilingual site lets you serve both groups without compromise, provided both versions get the same care. A neglected Arabic version, with out-of-date offers or awkward machine translation, can do more harm than having no Arabic version at all.

Bilingual Arabic-English website design: the layout essentials

Arabic is written right to left, so the whole interface needs to mirror, not just the text. This is often called RTL support. A theme or design system that claims to support it still needs careful testing.

ElementEnglish versionArabic version
Reading directionLeft to rightRight to left, set at page level
Navigation and logoLogo left, menu rightUsually mirrored: logo right, menu left
Directional iconsArrows point right for nextArrows flip to point left for next
Non-directional iconsAs designedNot flipped, such as search, play or phone icons
Image sliders and galleriesMove left to rightMove right to left
FormsLabels and text aligned leftLabels and text aligned right; some fields such as email stay left to right
Numbers and phone numbersWestern digitsWestern or Arabic-Indic digits by choice, applied consistently
TypographyLatin typefaceArabic typeface that pairs well and stays readable at small sizes

Mixed content is where problems appear. An Arabic sentence that includes a brand name, a product code or a price can display in the wrong order if the markup is not set up correctly. We test real content, not placeholder text, in both directions before launch.

Fonts and typography

Arabic letters join and change shape depending on their position in a word, and many Latin fonts do not include Arabic characters at all. You need a dedicated Arabic typeface that sits comfortably beside your English font, with similar weight and visual size.

  • Arabic text often needs a slightly larger size or more line height to stay readable on phones.
  • Avoid very thin weights for body text, as they can break up on smaller screens.
  • Load only the weights you use, to keep pages fast. Our guide to font loading explains how.
  • Check that headings, buttons and menus still fit when Arabic text is longer or shorter than the English.

Our article on website colour and typography covers pairing and hierarchy in more depth.

Structure, URLs and navigation

Each language should live at its own address. The most common structure for a single-country business is a subfolder per language, such as one folder for English and one for Arabic on the same domain. Businesses targeting several countries may also need country versions, which adds complexity.

A few principles keep things clean:

  • One page, one language. Avoid pages that mix both languages in the main content.
  • A visible language switcher that takes visitors to the equivalent page, not back to the homepage.
  • Matching structure so every important English page has an Arabic equivalent, unless there is a good reason not to.
  • Navigation that makes sense in both languages. Menu labels should be written naturally, not translated word for word. Our guide to website navigation structure covers how to plan menus.

SEO for both languages

Search engines treat each language version as its own set of pages, so each needs full SEO care.

  1. Separate keyword research. Arabic searchers do not always use the direct translation of English terms. Research each language on its own.
  2. Language-specific metadata. Titles, descriptions, headings and image alt text should be written in the language of the page.
  3. Hreflang annotations to tell search engines which pages are language alternatives of each other.
  4. Self-referencing canonicals on each version, so search engines do not treat the Arabic page as a duplicate of the English one. Our canonical tags guide explains how they work.
  5. Internal links within each language, so visitors and crawlers stay in the version they chose.

If you want ongoing search visibility in both languages, our SEO and GEO service can cover research, content and reporting across both versions.

Writing and translating the content

Content quality makes or breaks a bilingual site. Machine translation is useful for drafts and internal checks, but customer-facing pages should be written or carefully edited by a fluent Arabic writer who understands your sector. The same goes for English if your source content is in Arabic.

We suggest a simple workflow:

  1. Finalise the source content in one language first.
  2. Brief the Arabic writer on audience, tone and key terms, and agree a glossary for product and service names.
  3. Write or adapt rather than translate line by line, so the copy reads naturally. Our guide to website copywriting covers the principles of clear page copy.
  4. Have a second fluent reader review key pages, especially legal, pricing and policy text.
  5. Review the copy in the built design, as line breaks and layout can change meaning or emphasis.

Small details matter too. Form validation messages, button labels, cookie notices, emails and error pages all need both languages. Our article on form error messages shows why those small pieces of text affect conversions.

Legal, data and policy pages

Privacy policies, terms and cookie notices need to be accurate in both languages, and the versions should match. Data protection and consumer rules differ between the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Qatar, and some authorities may require or expect Arabic versions of certain information. These requirements change, so check current official guidance and have policy text reviewed by a qualified adviser. We build the pages and consent tools, while the wording and compliance remain your responsibility.

Cost, timeline and upkeep

A bilingual site takes more work than a single-language one: more templates to test, two sets of content to load and extra checks for right-to-left layouts. Timelines are typically three to five weeks for a business website and five to twelve weeks for an online store, with bilingual content often the deciding factor. After launch, plan who updates each language, so offers, opening hours and policies stay in step. A shared content calendar and a simple rule that no page goes live in one language without the other will prevent most mismatches.

FAQs

What is a bilingual Arabic-English website?

It is a site with complete English and Arabic versions, each with its own URLs, navigation and content, and with the Arabic version laid out right to left.

Can I use automatic translation for the Arabic version?

It can help with drafts, but customer-facing pages should be written or edited by a fluent Arabic writer. Automatic translation often reads awkwardly and can damage trust.

Does WordPress or Shopify support right-to-left layouts?

Both platforms support right-to-left languages, but support varies between themes and plugins or apps. Always test real Arabic content across key pages before launch.

Should Arabic be the default language of my site?

It depends on your audience. Consumer brands with mostly Arabic-speaking customers often default to Arabic, while international or B2B businesses may lead with English.

How do search engines know which language to show?

Hreflang annotations tell search engines which pages are language alternatives, and each version should have its own metadata, canonical tag and internal links.

How much does a bilingual website cost?

Our websites cost £1,000 to £2,000, or £1,500 to £3,500 for ecommerce, with bilingual builds usually towards the upper end. Translation is extra, and other currencies are quoted on request.
