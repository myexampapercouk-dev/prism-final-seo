Contact Page Design: Making It Easy for Customers to Reach You

How to design a contact page that helps visitors get in touch, with advice on forms, contact options, expectations, accessibility and handling enquiries.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good contact page offers clear options and tells people what will happen after they get in touch.
  • Short, accessible forms that ask only for what you need make enquiries easier to send.
  • Contact details should be easy to find from every page, not hidden on one page alone.

The contact page is where interest turns into conversation. Someone has read enough to want to ask a question, request a quote or get help with an order. If the page is confusing, hard to use or hides the information they need, that moment can be lost.

Despite its importance, the contact page is often treated as an afterthought, with a generic form and little else. This guide covers how to design a contact page that makes it easy for people to reach you, sets clear expectations and helps your team handle enquiries well.

What a contact page needs to do

A contact page has a few simple jobs:

  • Show people how they can reach you.
  • Help them choose the right route for their question.
  • Make sending a message quick and straightforward.
  • Explain what happens next.
  • Reassure them that their details are handled responsibly.

Everything on the page should support those jobs. Extra content that distracts from them usually makes the page worse, not better.

Offer the right contact options

Different people prefer different ways of getting in touch. Some want to fill in a form, some prefer email, and some want to talk to a person. Offer the options your business can genuinely support, and present them clearly.

Common options include a contact form, an email address, a phone number with opening hours, and a postal or business address where relevant. For a remote business without a public office, it is fine to say so, and to explain how meetings or calls are arranged instead.

Only list channels you will actually monitor. A phone number that is never answered, or a social account that is rarely checked, is worse than not listing it at all.

Help people choose the right route

If you handle several types of enquiry, such as new business, existing orders and support, help visitors pick the right one. This could be separate sections, a short set of options at the top of the page, or a dropdown in the form that routes messages to the right person.

For ecommerce sites, link clearly to order tracking, delivery information and returns. Many customer questions can be answered without a message if the right information is easy to find.

Design forms that are easy to complete

A contact form should feel quick. Ask only for information you genuinely need to respond well. Every extra field is another reason for someone to give up.

  • Keep fields to a minimum. Name, email and message are often enough. Add a phone field only if you will use it, and consider making it optional.
  • Use clear labels. Labels should sit outside the field and stay visible, rather than placeholder text that disappears when someone starts typing.
  • Explain any required fields. Mark them clearly and consistently.
  • Use helpful error messages. Say what went wrong and how to fix it, next to the relevant field.
  • Use the right input types. Email and phone fields that bring up suitable mobile keyboards make forms easier on phones.
  • Write a clear button. Something like Send message is clearer than a vague Submit.

Set expectations about responses

One of the most useful things a contact page can do is tell people what happens next. Will they get an automatic confirmation? Who will reply? When can they expect to hear back? Only promise what you can reliably deliver, and describe it honestly.

After someone submits a form, show a clear confirmation message on screen. If you send an automatic email, make sure it arrives, reads well and confirms the key details of their enquiry.

Reassure people about their data

Visitors are sharing personal details, so explain briefly how you use them and link to your privacy policy. Keep the wording plain. Avoid pre-ticked boxes for marketing, and keep any marketing consent separate from the enquiry itself. Data protection requirements can be detailed, so if you are unsure what applies to your business, take appropriate advice.

Spam protection is also important, but choose methods that do not make the form difficult for genuine people, particularly those using assistive technology.

Make the page accessible

A contact page must work for everyone, including people who use screen readers, keyboard navigation or magnification. Key points include:

  • Every field has a properly associated label.
  • The form can be completed and submitted using only a keyboard.
  • Focus states are clearly visible.
  • Error messages are announced and easy to locate.
  • Text and buttons have sufficient colour contrast.
  • Phone numbers and email addresses are written as text and can be tapped or clicked.

Design for mobile first

Many people will visit your contact page on a phone, often while doing something else. Make tap targets large enough, keep the form short, and allow phone numbers to be tapped to call. Check that any map or embedded content does not slow the page or push the important information too far down.

Add helpful context, not clutter

A little supporting content can increase confidence. A short introduction explaining who will read the message, a link to frequently asked questions, or a note about the areas or types of client you work with can all help. Keep it brief, and keep the contact options themselves near the top of the page.

If you include a map, make sure the address is also written as text. Maps alone are not accessible to everyone and do not help people copy the address.

Make contact details easy to find everywhere

The contact page should not be the only place people can find your details. Most visitors look for contact information in the main navigation or the footer. A clear link in the header, and key details in the footer, help people reach you from any page. Our guide to website footer design covers what to include there.

On longer or deeper sites, clear navigation aids such as breadcrumb navigation help visitors move around without getting lost, which makes it easier to reach the contact page when they are ready.

Keep enquiries organised behind the scenes

A good contact page is only half the story. Messages need to reach the right person and be answered. Test the form regularly to make sure submissions arrive. Check spam folders, confirm notification addresses are up to date when staff change, and keep a simple record of enquiries so nothing slips through.

Common contact page mistakes

  • Long forms that ask for unnecessary information.
  • No confirmation after a form is sent.
  • Contact details only available through a form.
  • Listing channels that nobody checks.
  • Forms that break on mobile or with keyboard navigation.
  • Notification emails going to an old or unmonitored inbox.

Writing the page copy

The words on a contact page are often an afterthought, but they shape how approachable you seem. A short, friendly introduction that invites questions works better than a cold heading followed by a form. Tell people what kinds of enquiry you welcome, and if there are things you cannot help with, say so politely to save everyone time.

Keep the tone consistent with the rest of the site. If your brand voice is warm and informal elsewhere, a stiff contact page will feel out of place.

Contact options on product and service pages

Not every enquiry starts on the contact page. Visitors reading about a particular service or product may have a question there and then. A short prompt near the bottom of key pages, linking to the contact page or offering a simple way to ask a question, catches people at the moment they are interested. Where possible, pass along which page they came from so your reply can be more relevant.

Testing your contact page

Before launch, and regularly after it, test the page as a visitor would:

  1. Complete and send the form on desktop and on a phone.
  2. Try submitting with missing or incorrect details to check the error messages.
  3. Navigate the form using only a keyboard.
  4. Confirm the notification email arrives at the right inbox.
  5. Check any automatic reply for accuracy and tone.
  6. Tap the phone number and email links to make sure they work.

Small problems here, such as a broken notification address, can mean enquiries go unanswered without anyone noticing.

FAQs

Should I use a contact form or just list an email address?

Offering both often works well. Forms help structure enquiries, while a visible email address suits people who prefer to write directly.

How many fields should a contact form have?

As few as you need to respond properly. Name, email and message are enough for many businesses.

Do I need to show a physical address?

It depends on your business and any legal requirements that apply to you. Remote businesses can explain how they work instead. If you are unsure, take appropriate advice.

How can I reduce spam from my contact form?

Use spam protection methods that do not create barriers for real visitors, and review them if genuine messages start being blocked.

Where should the contact page link appear?

A link in the main navigation and in the footer makes it easy to find from anywhere on the site.
