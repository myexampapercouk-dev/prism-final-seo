How to Set Up Shipping Rates in Shopify and WooCommerce (UK)

How to set up shipping rates for a UK online shop in Shopify or WooCommerce: zones, flat and weight-based rates, free delivery thresholds and testing.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Decide your delivery strategy first, then configure the platform; the settings are easy once you know what you want to charge.
  • Accurate product weights and sensible packaging assumptions are the foundation of any rate that is not a simple flat fee.
  • Test every zone and rate at checkout before launch, including awkward cases such as remote postcodes and very heavy baskets.

Delivery costs are one of the most common reasons shoppers abandon a basket. Charge too much and customers leave. Charge too little and you quietly lose money on every order. Getting shipping rates right in your online shop is a mix of business decisions and platform settings, and the business decisions come first.

This guide explains how to plan and set up shipping rates for a UK online shop, with practical notes for Shopify and WooCommerce. It covers the configuration side. For how to present delivery information to customers, see our guide to delivery and returns pages.

Start with your delivery strategy

Before opening any settings screen, answer a few questions:

  • What does delivery actually cost you? Include carrier charges, packaging, and the time it takes to pack an order.
  • Where will you deliver? Mainland UK only, or also Northern Ireland, the Highlands and Islands, the Channel Islands, Europe or further afield?
  • How fast? A standard option only, or also express or next-day delivery?
  • Will you offer free delivery? On everything, above a basket value, or not at all?
  • Will you offer collection? If you have premises, local pickup can be a useful option.

Write down the rules you want in plain English. For example: "Standard UK delivery is a flat charge, free over a set basket value. Express delivery costs more. Highlands and Islands cost extra. We do not ship abroad yet." Once you have that, the platform setup is mostly a matter of translating those rules.

Common ways to charge for delivery

Most UK shops use one of these approaches, or a combination.

Flat rate

One price for every order regardless of size. It is simple to understand and simple to set up. The risk is that heavy or bulky orders cost you more than you charge, so it works best when your products are similar in size and weight.

Free delivery over a threshold

Customers pay a delivery charge on small orders, and delivery is free above a set basket value. This can encourage larger orders. Set the threshold based on your margins and typical order value, not on what other shops seem to do.

Weight-based rates

Charges increase in bands as the order gets heavier. This tracks real carrier costs more closely, but depends on every product having an accurate weight. If weights are missing or wrong, rates will be wrong too.

Price-based rates

Charges change based on the basket value. This is simpler than weight-based shipping and works when value and size roughly go together.

Carrier-calculated rates

Some platforms can pull live rates from carriers at checkout based on the address, weight and dimensions. This can be accurate but depends on your plan, your platform and your carrier, and it can produce odd-looking prices that put customers off. Many small shops prefer simpler rates that they control.

Free delivery on everything

Delivery costs are built into product prices. It removes a barrier at checkout, but you need to be confident your margins can absorb the cost on every order, including small ones.

Shipping zones: where you deliver

Both Shopify and WooCommerce organise shipping around zones: groups of locations that share the same rates. A typical UK setup might include:

  • UK mainland: your standard rates.
  • Highlands, Islands and remote areas: often more expensive for carriers to reach, so you may need a surcharge or a separate rate.
  • Northern Ireland: handled differently by some carriers, and with its own trading arrangements for some goods.
  • Channel Islands and Isle of Man: not part of the UK for some customs and VAT purposes.
  • International: Europe and rest of world, if you ship abroad.

Remote postcodes are where many UK shops come unstuck. If your carrier charges more for certain postcodes and your shop does not, you absorb the difference. Check your carrier's terms and decide how you want to handle them. Customs, VAT and trading rules for Northern Ireland, the Crown Dependencies and international destinations are complicated and change, so check current HMRC guidance or take professional advice before you start shipping there.

If you are planning to sell abroad, our guide to selling internationally from a UK online store covers the wider considerations.

Setting up shipping in Shopify

In Shopify, shipping is managed in the shipping and delivery section of your settings. The general approach is:

  1. Check your shipping origin. Make sure the address you ship from is correct.
  2. Review your shipping profiles. Shopify uses profiles so different groups of products can have different rates. Most small shops only need the general profile. You might add a separate one for oversized or fragile products.
  3. Create zones. Add a zone for the UK and further zones for any other regions you serve.
  4. Add rates to each zone. Create flat rates, or rates with conditions based on weight or order price. For a free delivery threshold, add a free rate with a minimum order value and a paid rate with a maximum order value just below it.
  5. Set up local delivery or pickup if you offer them.
  6. Add package sizes if you use carrier-calculated rates or print labels, so dimensions are accounted for.

Shopify's options vary by plan and change over time, so check what is available on your plan. If you are setting up a new store, our Shopify store setup guide covers the wider setup steps.

Setting up shipping in WooCommerce

WooCommerce has shipping settings built into its core settings area. The general approach is:

  1. Create shipping zones. Add regions such as the UK. You can narrow zones using postcodes, including wildcards and ranges, which is useful for remote areas.
  2. Add shipping methods to each zone. The built-in methods are flat rate, free shipping and local pickup.
  3. Configure costs. Flat rate costs can include simple calculations, such as a charge per item or a percentage of the order, which gives more flexibility than it first appears.
  4. Set up shipping classes if some products need different charges. For example, a "bulky" class can add an extra cost to the flat rate.
  5. Configure free shipping rules, such as a minimum order amount or a coupon requirement.

Zone order matters in WooCommerce. A customer is matched to the first zone that fits their address, so put specific zones (such as remote postcodes) above broader ones (such as the whole UK). More complex rules, such as weight-based tables or live carrier rates, usually need an extension. Keep extensions to a minimum and review them regularly, as our WordPress plugin audit guide explains.

Product weights and packaging

Any rate that depends on weight or size relies on accurate product data. Before relying on weight-based or carrier-calculated rates:

  • Weigh your products, ideally in their packaging, and record weights on each product or variant.
  • Record dimensions for bulky items, since carriers often charge by size as well as weight.
  • Think about how products are packed together. Three small items might go in one parcel; one large item might not fit a standard box.
  • Check that imported products, for example from a supplier spreadsheet, came through with weights intact.

It is worth running a few real past orders through your new rates on paper. Compare what the customer would have paid with what the delivery actually cost you. If the gap is large in either direction, adjust before launch.

Express, next-day and named-day delivery

Faster delivery options can be attractive to customers, but only offer what you can reliably deliver. Consider:

  • Cut-off times: next-day delivery usually means the order must be placed and dispatched by a certain time.
  • Weekends and bank holidays: make it clear how these affect delivery.
  • Stock location: if some products are made to order or shipped from a supplier, they may not be suitable for express delivery.

Label each rate clearly at checkout so customers know what they are choosing. "Standard delivery (2 to 4 working days)" is far more helpful than "Option 1".

VAT and delivery charges

If you are VAT registered, delivery charges may need VAT applied, often at the same rate as the goods being delivered, but the rules can be more involved for mixed baskets. Make sure your tax settings on Shopify or WooCommerce treat shipping correctly, and check current HMRC guidance or ask your accountant if you are not sure.

Showing delivery costs early

Customers dislike surprise costs at the final step. Help them by:

  • Showing delivery costs or a free delivery threshold on product pages and in the basket.
  • Adding a short delivery summary in the header or near the add to basket button.
  • Showing progress towards the free delivery threshold in the basket, if you offer one.
  • Making sure your delivery page and checkout show the same prices.

These details often have a meaningful effect on conversion. Our guide to ecommerce conversion rate optimisation covers this kind of improvement in more depth.

Test before you launch

Once rates are configured, test them properly. Place test orders, or go as far as checkout, using:

  • A standard mainland UK address with a single small item.
  • A basket just below and just above any free delivery threshold.
  • A heavy or bulky basket.
  • A Highlands or Islands postcode.
  • A Northern Ireland address and an international address, if you ship there.
  • A mix of products from different shipping profiles or classes.

Check that the right rates appear, that nothing shows "no shipping available" by mistake, and that confirmation emails show the correct delivery method. Our guide to website staging and testing explains how to test changes safely before they reach customers.

Review rates regularly

Carrier prices, packaging costs and your own margins change. Review your shipping rates periodically and whenever your carrier changes its pricing. Look at your orders to see how many qualify for free delivery and whether your threshold still makes sense.

Before busy periods, check that cut-off dates for Christmas delivery are clear and that your rates can cope with any extra demand. Our guide to seasonal sales preparation covers this.

FAQs

Should I offer free delivery?

It depends on your margins and order values. Free delivery above a threshold is a common middle ground that can encourage larger orders without absorbing the cost on very small ones.

How do I charge more for Scottish Highlands and Islands postcodes?

Create a separate zone for those postcodes. WooCommerce supports postcode ranges and wildcards in zones. On Shopify, options for postcode-level rates depend on your plan and setup, so check what is available.

Do delivery charges include VAT?

If you are VAT registered, delivery charges often need VAT, but the rules can vary. Check current HMRC guidance or ask your accountant.

Are carrier-calculated rates better than flat rates?

Not necessarily. They can be more accurate but depend on good product data and your plan, and can produce prices customers find confusing. Many small shops do well with simple rates.

Why does my checkout say no shipping is available?

Usually the customer's address does not match any zone, or a product sits in a profile or class without rates for that zone. Check zones and product assignments.
