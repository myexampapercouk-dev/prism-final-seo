How Much Does SEO Cost in the UK? A Plain-English Guide

How much SEO costs in the UK: monthly retainers, one-off audits and project pricing explained, what each level includes and how to judge an SEO quote.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most UK SEO is sold as a monthly retainer; the price reflects hours, content volume and how competitive your market is.
  • A cheap quote is only good value if the scope is clear: ask exactly what is delivered each month and how progress is reported.
  • SEO typically takes 3–6 months to show meaningful results, so budget for at least that long before judging it.

SEO in the UK is usually priced as a monthly retainer, with one-off audits and technical projects quoted separately. Small local businesses tend to spend a few hundred pounds a month, established SMEs more, and businesses in competitive national markets considerably more. What you actually pay depends less on the label "SEO" and more on how many hours, pages and links the work involves.

This guide explains how SEO pricing works, what you should expect at different budget levels and how to compare quotes so you are paying for work that moves the needle rather than a report nobody reads.

How much does SEO cost in the UK?

There is no official price list for SEO, which is why quotes vary so much. Freelancers, small agencies and large agencies all price differently, and two quotes for "SEO" can describe completely different amounts of work. The useful question is not "what does SEO cost?" but "what will be done each month for this price?"

As a reference point, our own published retainers start at £350 a month for small local businesses, £750 a month for established SMEs and £1,500 a month for growing businesses that also want ads management. Those figures are starting prices; the final quote depends on scope.

The main ways SEO is priced

Monthly retainers

Most ongoing SEO is sold this way. You pay a fixed amount each month for an agreed set of activities: technical fixes, content, on-page improvements, link building and reporting. Retainers suit SEO because results compound over months, and the work needs to keep pace with your competitors.

One-off audits

A technical or content audit reviews your site and produces a prioritised list of fixes. It is a good starting point if you have a developer or in-house team who can implement the recommendations, or if you want to understand the size of the job before committing to a retainer.

Project pricing

Some SEO work has a clear start and finish: a website migration, a new set of location pages, or a structured data roll-out. These are usually quoted as fixed projects. A migration in particular is worth paying for properly, because a badly handled redesign can wipe out years of rankings. Our guide on redesigning a website without losing rankings explains why.

Hourly or day rates

Consultants sometimes charge by the hour or day for advice, training or ad-hoc fixes. This works for small, well-defined tasks but makes long-term budgeting harder.

What drives the price up or down

  • Competition. Ranking a plumber in one town is a different job from ranking a national ecommerce brand. Competitive markets need more content and more authority.
  • Size and state of the website. A 10-page site with clean code needs less technical work than a 5,000-product store with duplicate pages and slow templates.
  • Content volume. Content takes time to research and write well. Our retainers, for example, include 4, 8 or 12 posts a month depending on level.
  • Links and digital PR. Earning coverage on relevant websites is labour-intensive. See our guide to digital PR for link building.
  • Geography. Local, national and international SEO each need different amounts of work. International sites add hreflang and localised content.
  • Who implements. If the agency also makes the changes on your site, the fee covers more hours than an advice-only arrangement.

SEO and GEO: is AI search included?

Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT and Perplexity now answer many questions directly. Being cited in those answers draws on the same foundations as SEO: well-structured pages, consistent facts about your business and authority from trusted sources. Many agencies, including us, treat generative engine optimisation as part of SEO rather than a separate bill. If a quote lists GEO as an expensive add-on, ask exactly what extra work it involves. Our explainer on what GEO is covers the basics.

Red flags in cheap SEO quotes

Low prices are not automatically bad, but some patterns should make you cautious:

  • Guaranteed rankings. No one controls Google. A guarantee usually means the agency is targeting terms nobody searches for.
  • Bulk links. "100 backlinks a month" for a small fee usually means low-quality links that can do harm.
  • No access to your accounts. You should own your Google Search Console, Analytics and Business Profile, and the agency should work inside them.
  • Vague deliverables. If the proposal does not say what happens each month, you cannot judge whether it happened.
  • Long lock-in with no review points. Commitment is reasonable for SEO, but there should be regular reviews.

How to compare SEO quotes

Put each quote side by side and ask the same questions:

  1. What will be delivered each month, in specific numbers (pages, posts, fixes)?
  2. Who does the work, and who makes changes on the website?
  3. How is progress reported, and which metrics matter? Enquiries and sales beat raw traffic.
  4. What happens in the first 90 days?
  5. What is the notice period, and who owns the content and accounts if you leave?

Our guide on how to choose a digital marketing agency in the UK covers more questions to ask, including how to check claims and case studies.

What a typical month of SEO work looks like

It helps to picture the work behind a retainer. In a typical month for an established SME, an agency might fix a handful of technical issues found in Search Console, improve the titles and content of pages that already rank on page two, publish new articles that answer buyer questions, add internal links from those articles to service pages, and check that enquiries are being tracked properly.

At the end of the month you should receive a short report that explains what was done, what changed in visibility and enquiries, and what happens next. If a monthly report is mostly charts with no explanation, ask for a plain-English summary. You are paying for decisions and actions, not screenshots.

The first month or two often look different: more audit and set-up, fewer visible changes. That is normal, as long as you can see the plan that comes out of it.

Is SEO worth the money?

SEO is worth it when people search for what you sell and when you can wait a few months for returns. It builds an asset: pages that keep attracting visitors without paying for every click. If you need leads this month, paid media is faster, and many businesses run both, using ads for immediate demand while SEO builds up.

It is less likely to pay off when the website itself is the problem. If the site is slow, confusing or does not convert, fixing that first often gives better returns than any SEO budget. Our website and ecommerce development team can look at both together.

FAQs

How much does SEO cost per month in the UK?

It varies widely with scope and competition. As a reference, our published retainers start at £350, £750 and £1,500 a month, with the final price depending on hours, content volume and market.

How long does SEO take to work?

SEO typically begins showing meaningful results within 3–6 months. Technical fixes can help sooner; competitive national terms usually take longer.

Is a one-off SEO audit enough?

An audit is enough if you have someone to implement the fixes and keep publishing content. Without implementation, an audit on its own changes nothing.

Should SEO prices include content writing?

Usually, yes. Content is one of the main drivers of SEO results, so check how many pages or posts a quote includes each month.

Can an agency guarantee first place on Google?

No. Rankings depend on Google and on competitors. Be wary of guarantees; ask instead what work will be done and how progress will be measured.
