What makes web design in Ghent different

Ghent is a university city, a port and logistics hub, a centre for biotech and technology firms and a busy destination for visitors who come for the medieval centre, the festivals and the food scene. That mix shapes what a good website needs to do. A local restaurant needs a menu and booking path that works on a phone in a few seconds. A technology company needs to explain a complex product to international buyers. A shop in the centre needs opening hours, directions and an online store that ships across Belgium and beyond.

Language is the other big factor. Ghent sits in Flanders, so Dutch is the main language for most local customers, but many businesses also serve French-speaking customers from elsewhere in Belgium and English-speaking students, visitors and international clients. A website that treats languages as an afterthought usually ends up with mixed menus, missing pages and confusing signals for search engines. Planning the structure first avoids that.

Planning a multilingual site for Flanders

For most Ghent businesses we recommend one domain with a folder for each language, such as a Dutch section, a French section and an English section. This keeps all of your authority in one place and makes it simple to add a language later. Each page gets its own translated title, description and URL, and we add language tags so search engines show the right version to the right visitor.

We also plan the language switcher carefully. It should sit in the same place on every page, use the name of each language rather than a flag, and take the visitor to the matching page rather than back to the homepage. Our guide to multilingual website design covers the structure in more detail.

Because we write in English, we normally build the English version first and then add the Dutch and French text your team or translator supplies. We check that every language fits the layout, because Dutch and French lines often run longer than English ones, and buttons or headings that look neat in one language can break in another.

Speed, accessibility and trust

Visitors in Ghent browse on phones while cycling between meetings, waiting for a tram or standing in a shop queue. A slow site loses them. We keep pages light, compress images, limit heavy scripts and test on real mobile connections before launch.

Accessibility matters too. The European Accessibility Act brings accessibility requirements to many consumer-facing digital services, including a lot of online shops. We are not legal advisers, and you should check official guidance or your own adviser about how the rules apply to you, but we build with clear contrast, readable text, keyboard navigation and proper labels as standard. Our article on the European Accessibility Act and your website explains the practical side.

Trust signals finish the job. That means a clear company name and address, a real contact route, honest descriptions of what you do and a privacy notice that matches how you collect data. Cookie consent should follow European rules, and we design banners that give visitors a real choice without wrecking the page.

SEO foundations built into every Ghent website

A new website should be ready to rank from launch, even if you do not start an SEO retainer straight away. Every site we build includes:

  • A clear page structure, with one main topic per page and a logical menu.
  • Unique titles and meta descriptions written around what your customers actually search for.
  • Clean URLs in each language, with language tags in place.
  • Structured data for your business details, so search engines understand who you are and where you serve.
  • An XML sitemap, sensible redirects from any old pages and fast loading on mobile.

For businesses that rely on local customers, we also help you set up and complete your Google Business Profile and keep your name, address and phone number consistent across directories. If you want ongoing search work after launch, our SEO and GEO service covers content, technical fixes and visibility in AI search answers.

Choosing a platform: WordPress, Shopify or something lighter

We build on the platform that suits your business rather than forcing one choice on every client. For service businesses that publish articles, case pages or news, WordPress gives your team an easy editor and plenty of flexibility. For shops, Shopify handles products, payments, taxes and shipping with less maintenance, and it supports several languages and currencies through its markets features, which we explain in our guide to Shopify Markets for Europe.

If you only need a few pages that rarely change, a lighter build can be the fastest and cheapest option. We will explain the trade-offs on the first call and recommend what we would choose if it were our own business.

How a remote web design project with Ghent works

Working remotely does not mean working at arm's length. A typical project runs like this:

  1. Discovery call. We talk through your business, customers, languages, competitors and goals, and agree the pages and features you need.
  2. Structure and wireframes. We map every page and sketch layouts so you can see the flow before any design work starts.
  3. Design. We create the visual design in a shared file you can comment on directly.
  4. Build and content. We build the site on a private staging address. Your team or translator supplies Dutch and French text, and we place it.
  5. Testing and launch. We test speed, forms, languages, mobile layouts and accessibility, then launch and submit the sitemap.

Belgium is one hour ahead of the UK for most of the year, so there is plenty of overlap in the working day for calls and quick replies.

Common mistakes to avoid on a Ghent website

Many sites we review share the same problems. Avoiding them is often worth more than any design flourish:

  • Machine-translated pages that read awkwardly and erode trust with local customers.
  • Only one language indexed because the others sit behind a script or a pop-up.
  • Huge images and sliders that slow the homepage on mobile data.
  • Contact forms that ask for too much and give no confirmation.
  • No clear next step on service pages, so visitors read and leave.
