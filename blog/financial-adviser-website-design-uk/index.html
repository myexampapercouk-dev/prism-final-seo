Financial Adviser Website Design: Clarity, Trust and Regulatory Care

Website design for UK financial advisers and planners: client-type pages, charges, regulatory disclosures, team profiles and first meetings.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Prospective clients often want to understand who you help, how advice works and how you charge before they get in touch.
  • Website content is likely to count as a financial promotion or communication with clients, so involve your compliance function and check current FCA rules before publishing.
  • Regulatory status, charges and any claims about outcomes need careful, accurate wording, and this guide is not a substitute for compliance advice.

Choosing a financial adviser is a significant, personal decision. People often arrive at an adviser's website at a turning point: approaching retirement, receiving an inheritance, selling a business or simply realising their finances have become complicated. They want to understand whether you work with people like them, how advice works, what it might cost and whether they can trust you. An adviser's website that answers those questions clearly and calmly can make the first conversation easier.

This guide covers website design for UK financial advisers, financial planners and wealth management firms. It focuses on structure, content and practical features. For regulatory considerations in marketing, see our guide to FCA-compliant marketing for financial advisers, and for search, our guide to financial adviser SEO.

An important note before going further: financial services websites are subject to regulation. This guide is general information about website design, not regulatory or legal advice. Your compliance function, network or compliance consultant should review content before it goes live, and you should check the current FCA rules and guidance that apply to your firm.

What prospective clients want to know

Visitors to an adviser's site are often asking:

  • Do you work with people in my situation?
  • What does financial advice or planning actually involve?
  • How do you charge, and roughly what might it cost?
  • Are you regulated, and what does that mean for me?
  • Who would I be dealing with?
  • How do I take the first step, and is there any commitment?

The homepage should make clear who you help and how to get in touch. Our guide to homepage design covers prioritising content.

Organise around clients, not products

Advisers sometimes structure their sites around products, such as pensions, investments and protection. Clients more often think in terms of their own circumstances. Structuring pages around client situations can make the site easier to use, for example:

  • Planning for retirement.
  • Business owners and directors.
  • Families and later-life planning.
  • People who have received a lump sum or inheritance.
  • Professionals in a particular field, if that is a genuine specialism.

Each page can explain the kinds of questions those clients have and how your advice process helps, without promising particular outcomes. Only describe areas where you actually advise, and make sure any specialist descriptions are accurate. Our guide to website navigation covers structuring menus.

Explaining how advice works

Many people are unsure what happens when they see an adviser. A clear "How we work" page can explain, in general terms and reflecting your actual process, the first conversation, information gathering, recommendations and ongoing reviews. It can also explain the difference between one-off advice and an ongoing service, if you offer both. Plain language matters: avoid unexplained jargon, and make sure the description matches what clients really experience and what your client agreements say.

Charges and fees

How advisers charge is a common source of confusion. The FCA has requirements on disclosing charges, and your client agreement and initial disclosure documents set out the formal position. On the website, a clear explanation can help people decide whether to get in touch, for example how initial and ongoing fees are structured and what an ongoing service includes. Any figures or examples shown should be accurate, consistent with your formal documents and approved by your compliance function. If you prefer not to publish figures, explain how charges are worked out and when they will be confirmed. Our guide to pricing page design covers presenting this kind of information clearly.

Regulatory status and disclosures

Advisory firms typically need to show their regulatory status accurately on their website, which may include the firm's name, authorisation details and registered address, and status as an appointed representative of a principal firm where that applies. The exact wording and placement requirements depend on your firm's circumstances and the current rules, so check them with your compliance support and the FCA's guidance. Common places for this information include the footer and a dedicated legal or regulatory page. Our guide to footer design covers fitting essential information into the footer without clutter.

Other points to check with your compliance function include:

  • Risk warnings where you discuss investments, and how prominently they need to appear.
  • Statements about whether advice is independent or restricted, and what that means.
  • Complaints information and the role of the Financial Ombudsman Service.
  • Information relating to the Financial Services Compensation Scheme, where relevant.
  • Your privacy notice and cookie policy.

Content, promotions and the Consumer Duty

Much of what an adviser publishes online, including service pages, articles and social posts, may count as a financial promotion or a communication with clients. The FCA expects these to be fair, clear and not misleading, and the Consumer Duty sets expectations around consumer understanding. In practice, that means avoiding guarantees about returns or outcomes, presenting benefits and risks in a balanced way, and making sure content is understandable for the people it is aimed at. Have content reviewed and approved in line with your firm's procedures, and keep records of what was published and when.

Articles and guides can help visitors understand topics such as pension options or inheritance tax planning in general terms, and can support search visibility. Date each article, review it when rules change, link to official sources such as government guidance or MoneyHelper where useful, and make clear content is general information rather than personal advice.

Introducing the people

Clients are choosing someone they may work with for many years. A team page with real photos, roles and qualifications described accurately helps visitors get a sense of who they will deal with. Use professional designations and chartered titles only where the person or firm genuinely holds them and in line with the awarding body's rules. Avoid stock imagery that could belong to any firm. See our guide to website trust signals.

Testimonials and reviews

Client reviews can be persuasive, but in financial services they need extra care, because they may form part of a financial promotion. Don't select or present reviews in a way that gives a misleading impression, never invent or alter them, and check with your compliance function before featuring them on the website. Our guide to testimonials pages covers general good practice.

The first meeting and contact

Many prospective clients would rather book an initial conversation than complete a long form. If you offer an initial meeting, explain how long it lasts, whether there is any charge and what it covers. An embedded booking tool can make this easy; see our guide to online booking systems.

Keep enquiry forms short and avoid asking for detailed financial information at this stage. Never encourage people to send sensitive documents by ordinary email or a basic form; if you use a secure client portal, link to it clearly. Make sure your forms and data handling align with UK GDPR. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design.

Client portals and existing clients

Many firms provide a client portal for valuations and documents. Put a clear "Client login" link in the header, separate from new enquiry routes. Make sure links point to the genuine portal address and explain how clients can recognise legitimate communications from you, which can help reduce the risk of impersonation scams.

Accessibility and vulnerable customers

Financial advice often involves older clients and people in vulnerable circumstances. Clear typography, good contrast, plain language and alternative contact routes, such as a phone number, all help. The FCA expects firms to consider the needs of vulnerable customers, so think about how the website supports that. Our guide to website accessibility covers the practical basics.

Choosing a platform

WordPress is common for adviser sites because it supports structured pages and articles and gives control over content approval workflows. Hosted builders such as Squarespace or Wix can suit very small firms. Some networks have their own requirements or preferred approaches for member websites, so check those first. Whatever you choose, make sure you can update regulatory information quickly and keep a record of changes. See our guides to website builders vs custom websites and website maintenance.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Product-led pages that don't say who you help.
  • Missing, inaccurate or out-of-date regulatory information.
  • Language that implies guaranteed returns or outcomes.
  • Charges that are hard to find or inconsistent with your formal documents.
  • Publishing content without compliance review.
  • Asking for sensitive financial details through a basic form.

FAQs

What regulatory information should a financial adviser's website show?

Usually your regulatory status and firm details, among other disclosures. The exact requirements depend on your circumstances, so check current FCA rules with your compliance support.

Should financial advisers show their fees online?

Explaining how you charge can help visitors. Any figures should match your formal disclosures and be approved by your compliance function.

Can advisers use client testimonials?

Possibly, but they may form part of a financial promotion. Check with your compliance function before using them.

Do website articles count as financial promotions?

They may do. Have content reviewed under your firm's procedures and make clear it is general information, not personal advice.

How should an adviser's website handle sensitive information?

Keep enquiry forms brief, avoid collecting detailed financial data, and use a secure client portal for documents.
