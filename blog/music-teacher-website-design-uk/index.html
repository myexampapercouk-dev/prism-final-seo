Music Teacher Website Design UK: Getting Lesson Enquiries from Your Website

Music teacher website design for UK instrument and singing teachers: lesson pages, availability, audio and video, policies and what to ask a designer.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A music teacher website should answer who you teach, what instruments and levels, where lessons happen and how to start.
  • Short, well-recorded audio or video of your teaching style often does more than long written biographies.
  • Clear lesson policies on cancellations, payment and safeguarding prevent misunderstandings before the first lesson.

A music teacher website helps prospective pupils, or their parents, decide whether you are the right teacher and makes it easy to arrange a first lesson. Good music teacher website design is personal and practical: it shows what you teach, who you teach, where lessons take place, what to expect and how to get in touch.

This guide is for independent instrument and singing teachers and small music teaching practices in the UK. If you run a broader tutoring business covering academic subjects, our tutoring business website guide is a better fit. Here we focus on what is specific to music lessons.

Why music teaching websites are different

Choosing a music teacher is a personal decision. Pupils often stay with the same teacher for years, so people want a sense of personality, teaching approach and musical background before they commit. A music teacher website therefore has to do more than list services. It needs to give a feel for what lessons are like.

There are also practical differences from other tutoring:

  • Location varies. Lessons may happen at your home studio, at the pupil's home, at a school, at a rented room or online.
  • Levels vary widely. From complete beginners to advanced pupils preparing for graded exams, diplomas or auditions.
  • Audiences vary. Parents booking for children, adults returning to an instrument and adult beginners all read the site differently.
  • Sound matters. Audio and video can show something words cannot.

Pages a music teacher website usually needs

PageWhat it should do
HomeState instruments, ages and levels, location or online, and the next step
AboutYour musical background, teaching approach and qualifications you can evidence
LessonsA section per instrument or type of lesson, with who it suits
Fees and policiesHow payment works, cancellation terms and notice periods
Exams and performanceWhether you prepare pupils for graded exams, and how
Listen or watchShort clips that show your playing or teaching style
ContactAn enquiry form that asks the right questions

Write the about page for the pupil, not the CV

Many music teacher about pages read like a performance biography. Performance experience is relevant, but a prospective pupil mainly wants to know how you teach.

  • Your approach. Do you focus on reading music, playing by ear, technique, improvisation, or a mix? How do you adapt for different ages?
  • Who you enjoy teaching. Beginners, nervous adults, exam candidates, people preparing for auditions.
  • Your background. Training and qualifications, stated accurately. Only mention memberships, accreditations or checks you actually hold.
  • A real photo. Ideally with your instrument, in the space where you teach.

Keep claims specific and verifiable. "I teach piano to beginners and intermediate pupils of all ages" is clearer and more trustworthy than general statements about results. Our website copywriting guide has more on writing in a clear, honest voice.

Lesson pages that help people choose

If you teach more than one instrument, or offer different types of lesson, give each its own page or clear section. Someone searching for adult guitar lessons near them wants a page about adult guitar lessons, not a general list.

A useful lesson page covers:

  • Which ages and levels you teach on that instrument.
  • Lesson lengths available and how they suit different pupils.
  • Whether lessons are in person, online or both.
  • What a pupil needs before starting, such as an instrument or practice space.
  • Whether you prepare pupils for graded exams, and with which exam boards, if relevant.

Be careful not to promise exam outcomes. Describe how you support exam preparation rather than claiming pass rates you cannot evidence.

Using audio and video well

Sound is the obvious advantage a music teacher website has over other tutoring sites. Used well, a short clip can tell a visitor a lot about you.

  • Keep clips short. A minute or two of playing, or a short explanation of a technique, is enough.
  • Do not autoplay sound. Unexpected audio is frustrating and an accessibility problem. Let people choose to press play.
  • Host video sensibly. Embedding video from YouTube keeps your site light and avoids storing large files on your own hosting.
  • Get consent for any pupil content. If a clip features a pupil, especially a child, get clear, recorded permission from the pupil or their parent first.
  • Provide captions or a short description. This helps people who cannot listen at that moment and supports accessibility.

Availability and booking

Solo music teachers often have a limited number of slots, and they fill up. The website can help manage expectations.

Show availability honestly

A simple note such as "Currently taking enquiries for weekday afternoon lessons" or "Waiting list for Saturday slots" saves both sides time. Update it when it changes.

Ask the right questions in the enquiry form

A short form that asks for the instrument, the pupil's age group, their current level, preferred days and whether they want in-person or online lessons lets you reply with something useful. Avoid asking for more personal information than you need. Our contact forms guide covers form design.

Decide whether you need online booking

Weekly lessons with the same pupil rarely suit self-service booking, because slots are arranged once and repeated. A trial or consultation lesson, however, can sometimes be offered through a booking tool. Our online booking systems guide explains the options.

Policies that prevent awkward conversations

Missed lessons, late payments and term arrangements cause friction for many teachers. Setting them out clearly on the website, and pointing new pupils to them, helps.

  • Payment. Whether you charge per lesson, in advance per term, or monthly.
  • Cancellations. How much notice is needed and what happens to missed lessons.
  • Ending lessons. Any notice period you ask for.
  • Safeguarding. If you teach children, explain your approach, for example whether a parent should be present at home lessons, and describe any background checks accurately.
  • Privacy. How you handle the personal details people send you. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for sole traders and small businesses.

Terms like these may have legal implications, particularly around consumer rights, so it is worth checking them with a suitable adviser or a teaching association if you belong to one. A website can present your terms clearly, but it should not be the only place they are agreed.

Show fees or not?

Many teachers are unsure whether to publish lesson fees. Showing at least a starting point, or how fees are worked out, can filter out enquiries from people expecting a very different price. Others prefer to discuss fees once they know what a pupil needs. Either approach can work if it is consistent and clear. Our pricing page design guide covers the options.

Being found locally

Most music lesson searches include a location or are made on a phone close to home. The website supports local visibility when it clearly states where you teach, which areas you travel to and which instruments you offer, and when this matches your Google Business Profile. If you teach online across the UK, say so plainly, but local in-person pages are usually where enquiries start. Our local SEO guide explains the wider picture.

Platform and maintenance

A solo teacher rarely needs a complex build. Squarespace, Wix or a straightforward WordPress site can all work well, provided the design is clean, fast and easy for you to update. The bigger risk is a site that is never updated, with old availability and out-of-date fees. Choose something you are comfortable editing. Our Squarespace vs WordPress guide and website maintenance guide may help.

What to ask a web designer

  • How will audio and video be added without slowing the site down?
  • Can I update availability and fees myself?
  • How will the enquiry form handle personal information?
  • Will the site meet a recognised accessibility standard?
  • Who owns the domain and the site once it is built?

FAQs

Does a private music teacher really need a website?

Not always, but a simple website gives people a place to check who you are, what you teach and how to get in touch, which supports word of mouth and local search alike.

Should I include recordings of my own playing?

Short, good-quality clips can help people get a sense of your musicianship. Clips that show how you explain things can be just as useful as performance recordings.

Can I show pupils on my website?

Only with clear, recorded consent, and with particular care for children. Many teachers avoid identifiable pupil content and use their own playing or studio photos instead.

Should I offer online lessons on my website?

If you teach online, say so on each relevant lesson page and explain what equipment a pupil needs. It widens who can book, but local in-person pages still matter for nearby pupils.

How is a music teacher site different from a tutoring site?

Music lessons are usually long-term, personal and sound-based, so the about page, audio or video and lesson policies carry more weight than on a typical academic tutoring site.
