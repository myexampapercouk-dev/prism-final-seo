Website Accordions and Tabs: When to Use Them and How to Design Them Well

When website accordions and tabs help visitors and when they hide too much, with practical design, mobile, accessibility and SEO tips for small business sites.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 6 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Accordions and tabs can make long or varied content easier to scan, but they also hide it, so never tuck away information most visitors need.
  • Accordions tend to suit stacked questions and small screens, while tabs suit a small number of parallel, short-labelled views.
  • Clear labels, keyboard support and a sensible default state matter more than animation or visual polish.

Accordions and tabs are two of the most familiar components in web design. An accordion shows a list of headings that expand when clicked. A set of tabs shows one panel at a time, with labels along the top to switch between them. Both promise the same thing: lots of content in less space.

That promise is appealing when a page starts to feel long, but there is a trade-off. Anything collapsed is content a visitor may never open. This guide explains when these components help, when they get in the way, how to choose between them, and how to build them so they work on phones, with keyboards and for screen readers. It applies whichever platform your site uses.

What accordions and tabs are for

Both patterns use what designers call progressive disclosure. Rather than showing everything at once, you show an overview and let people reveal details as they need them. This suits content where different visitors want different pieces, and few want all of it.

Typical uses on a small business website include:

  • Frequently asked questions on a service or support page.
  • Product details such as materials, care instructions and delivery information.
  • Stages of a process, with detail available for each stage.
  • Course modules or menu sections.
  • Different options for different audiences, such as home and business customers.

When not to hide content

The main risk is simple: hidden information is easier to miss. Before you collapse something, ask whether most visitors need it to make a decision. If they do, it should probably stay visible.

Think twice about collapsing:

  • Your core description of a service or product.
  • Prices or key terms that people expect to see openly.
  • The main reason to choose your business.
  • Short content where collapsing saves very little space.
  • Instructions someone needs to read in order, from start to finish.
  • Information visitors will want to compare side by side.

Collapsing can also make a page feel more work than it really is. If every section requires a click, a reader has to do a lot of opening and closing. Sometimes the better fix is shorter text, clearer headings, or splitting one crowded page into several pages. Our guide to service page design looks at how to structure this kind of content.

A long page is not automatically a problem. People will scroll when the content is relevant and easy to follow. Well-spaced headings and short paragraphs may solve the problem without any interactive component at all.

Accordions or tabs: how to choose

The two are not interchangeable. This table sets out the usual differences.

ConsiderationAccordionTabs
Best forLists of separate items people dip intoA few parallel views of related content
Number of itemsCan handle a longer listWorks best with only a handful
Label lengthCopes with full questionsNeeds short labels
Small screensNaturally suited to narrow layoutsOften needs adapting
Viewing several at oncePossible if multiple panels can stay openUsually one panel at a time

As a rough guide, choose an accordion for FAQs and long labels, and tabs for a small set of short categories. If you are not sure, an accordion tends to be the simpler option, because it adapts to mobile with less work. If neither fits, leave the content visible. For product comparisons, a table or a dedicated page may do the job better, as our guide to product comparison pages explains.

Designing a good accordion

Write labels people can scan

The label is doing all the work, because it may be the only thing a visitor reads. It should say exactly what lies beneath. Do we deliver to Northern Ireland? is far better than Delivery info. Wherever possible, start with the words people are looking for, as readers often scan down the left edge of a list.

Make the whole header clickable

Do not limit the clickable area to a tiny plus sign. The whole heading row should respond, with enough space to tap easily on a phone.

Show state clearly

Use an icon to show whether a panel is open or closed. Plus and minus symbols or a rotating arrow are familiar choices. Keep the same icon and position throughout the site.

Choose one open or many

Some accordions close the previous panel when a new one opens. This keeps the page tidy, but it can also make the page jump, and it stops people comparing two answers. Allowing several panels to stay open usually gives readers more control. If opening a panel moves the content a visitor just clicked out of view, the design needs another look.

Decide the default state

All closed is common, but opening the first panel can signal how the component works and show useful content immediately. If one item matters more than the others, consider leaving it open by default.

Keep the contents short

A panel containing a screen of text, several images and another accordion is a sign that the content needs its own page. Avoid nesting accordions within accordions, as visitors can easily lose track of where they are.

Designing good tabs

Use short, distinct labels

One or two words per tab is ideal. If labels wrap on to two lines or sound alike, tabs may not be the right pattern.

Limit the number

Keep to as many as fit comfortably on one row. Once tabs start wrapping to a second row or sliding out of view, the design gets harder to use.

Make the selected tab obvious

The current tab should look connected to its panel, for example through a matching background or a strong underline. Do not rely on colour alone, as some visitors may not perceive the difference. Our colour and typography guide covers contrast and readable text.

Keep the content parallel

Each tab should hold the same kind of information. Tabs for Description, Delivery and Care make sense together. A random collection of unrelated panels does not.

Do not use tabs as site navigation

If clicking takes people to a different page, that is a menu. It should behave and be built like one. Our website navigation guide explains how to plan that structure.

Making them work on mobile

Accordions usually behave well on phones, and they can be particularly useful there because long pages take more scrolling on a narrow screen. Tabs need more care. A row of labels that fits on a desktop may be cut off on a phone, and a sideways-scrolling tab row can hide options without anyone realising.

A common solution is to show tabs on larger screens and switch to an accordion on smaller ones. If you take this approach, check the content remains identical in both views, and test it on real devices.

Also pay attention to page jumps. When a long panel closes, the content below moves up and a visitor can lose their place. A well-built component keeps the header they tapped in view. Our guide to mobile website design covers broader principles for small screens, including comfortable tap targets.

Accessibility basics

Interactive components are a common source of accessibility problems, largely because they often look finished long before they work for everyone. Ask whoever builds your site to check the following.

  • All headers and tabs can be reached and operated using a keyboard alone.
  • A visible focus indicator shows which item is selected.
  • Screen readers are told whether a panel is open or closed, and which panel a control belongs to.
  • Accordion headers are built as real buttons within a proper heading structure.
  • Animations are short, and they respect a visitor's setting for reduced motion.
  • Content is not locked away if the script fails to load.

Native HTML offers a details and summary element that gives basic accordion behaviour with little extra code. It will not suit every design, but it is worth asking your developer about. Our website accessibility guide gives more background.

What about SEO?

A concern often raised is whether search engines ignore hidden content. Google has indicated that content in collapsed sections on mobile-friendly pages can be treated like other content, provided it is present in the page, but search engine behaviour can change. Keep two simple points in mind.

  • Make sure the content is in the page. Content loaded only when a visitor clicks may be harder for search engines to find.
  • Keep your key message visible. The main topic of a page should be obvious without any clicks, for visitors as well as search engines.

Our guide to schema markup covers structured data, including how to think about marking up questions and answers. Rules and rich result eligibility do change, so check current search guidance when adding it.

Adding them to your website

On WordPress, the block editor includes a Details block that provides simple expand and collapse behaviour. Many themes and page builders also offer accordion and tab elements, though their quality varies, so check keyboard use and mobile behaviour before you commit. Our block editor guide is a helpful starting point.

On Shopify, many themes include collapsible content sections, and the options available depend on your theme. Our guide to theme sections and blocks explains how those are managed.

Prefer a built-in or well-tested component over adding scripts for one small feature, and test after theme updates. Once in place, your website analytics may be able to show whether people use them, if clicks are set up to be tracked. If nobody opens a panel holding important information, consider moving that information into view.

FAQs

Are accordions bad for SEO?

Not inherently. Content in an accordion can still be found if it is present in the page. Keep your most important message visible, and avoid loading text only after a click.

Should an FAQ page use an accordion?

Often, especially with many questions. With only a few short answers, showing them openly may be easier. See our FAQ page guide for more on this.

How many tabs are too many?

There is no fixed number. If the labels no longer fit on one row at common screen sizes, or become hard to tell apart, consider an accordion or separate pages.

Should the first accordion item be open?

It can be helpful, since it shows how the component works and displays useful content immediately. Do so when the first item is relevant to most visitors.

Can visitors search for text inside a collapsed panel?

It depends on the browser and how the component is built. Some collapsed content may not be found with the browser find function, which is another reason to test important content.

Should product details go in tabs or an accordion?

Either can work. Accordions often suit mobile shoppers well, but keep key details such as price, size and availability visible rather than hidden in a panel.
