Car Garage and Mechanic Website Design: MOTs, Servicing and Repairs

Car garage and mechanic website design for UK workshops: MOT and service booking, repair pages, honest pricing, courtesy cars and opening hours.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Garage visitors usually want one of three things: to book an MOT, to book a service, or to get a fault looked at, so make each path obvious.
  • Customers are booking a drop-off rather than a visit to their home, so opening hours, location, parking and how to get to work matter more than for most trades.
  • Only describe MOT testing if you are an authorised test centre, and avoid promising fixed repair prices before the car has been inspected.

A car garage is not quite like other trades. The customer comes to you, not the other way round. They are usually organising their day around being without a car, they often feel uncertain about the technical side, and they may be worried about being overcharged. A garage website that answers practical questions quickly, makes booking straightforward and explains things in plain language has a real advantage over one that simply lists services.

This guide covers the garage website itself: structure, booking, pages and wording. For wider promotion, such as reviews, local listings and advertising, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK.

The three main reasons people visit

  • The MOT. A reminder has arrived, or the date is close. This visitor wants to know whether you test their type of vehicle, when you have availability and roughly what it costs.
  • The service. The car is due a service. The visitor wants to understand what kind of service you offer and whether it suits their car and its warranty.
  • The problem. A warning light, a noise, a leak or a car that will not start. This visitor wants reassurance that you can diagnose it and an idea of when you can look.

Putting three clear routes on the homepage, such as "book an MOT", "book a service" and "something wrong with your car", lets each visitor find their way in seconds.

MOT pages

MOT testing in Great Britain is overseen by the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency, and only authorised test stations can carry out MOTs. If you are an authorised test centre, say so clearly and state which vehicle classes you test, for example cars and light vans. If you are not, but you prepare cars for MOT or carry out repairs after a failure, describe that accurately instead. Northern Ireland has a separate testing system, so make sure your wording fits where you operate.

Useful content for an MOT page includes:

  • How to book and how long to allow, in general terms.
  • Whether customers can wait, or should drop off.
  • What happens if the car fails, including how you handle repair quotes.
  • The price, if you publish it. The government sets a maximum fee for MOT tests, and many garages charge less, but avoid stating the maximum as a fixed figure on your site unless you keep it up to date.

It is also helpful to remind visitors that they can check their MOT due date and history on the official government website. Linking to official tools builds trust.

Servicing pages

Servicing is where customers often feel least confident. Terms like interim, full and major service mean different things at different garages, so explain what you mean by each one in your own words. A simple table can help visitors compare.

Warranties are a frequent question. Many drivers believe they must use a main dealer to keep their manufacturer warranty. In general, independent garages can service cars under warranty provided the work follows the manufacturer's schedule and uses suitable parts, but you should word this carefully and avoid presenting it as a legal guarantee. Suggest customers check the terms of their own warranty, and only describe the parts and procedures you actually use.

If you specialise in certain makes, or in hybrid and electric vehicles, say so and explain what that means. Working on high-voltage systems requires specific training and equipment, so only promote electric and hybrid servicing if you are properly equipped.

Repairs and diagnostics

Repair pages work best when grouped by what the customer notices, as well as by the technical name of the job. Someone may not search for "clutch replacement" but might search for a clutch slipping or a gear that is hard to select. Pages for common areas such as brakes, clutches, exhausts, suspension, batteries, tyres, air conditioning and engine management lights can each explain symptoms, what you check and how you quote.

Avoid diagnosing problems on the website itself. General information about what a warning light might indicate is fine, but be clear that a proper inspection is needed, and never suggest a car is safe to drive with a serious fault. If a symptom could be dangerous, such as brakes not working properly, it is responsible to advise not driving the car and to explain how you can help.

Honest pricing

Fear of being overcharged is a common worry with garages, so how you talk about price matters. Some garages publish fixed prices for MOTs, standard services and simple jobs such as replacing a battery or a pair of brake pads on a typical car. Others explain their approach instead: diagnostic charges, labour rates and how parts are priced.

Either way, a few principles help:

  • Be clear about whether prices include VAT.
  • Say that you will contact the customer for approval before carrying out any additional work, if that is your policy.
  • If prices vary by vehicle, say so rather than listing a single "from" figure that most customers will exceed.
  • Keep published prices up to date.

Consumer law sets expectations around services being carried out with reasonable care and skill and for a reasonable price where none is agreed, but the details are best checked with official guidance or professional advice rather than summarised on your site. Our guide to pricing page design covers how to present prices clearly.

Booking online

Garages are well suited to online booking, because MOTs and services are predictable jobs with fairly standard durations. An online system can let customers choose a date, enter their registration and select a service. Some systems look up the vehicle from the registration automatically.

Points to consider:

  • Keep the booking form short. Registration, service type, preferred date and contact details may be enough.
  • Say whether bookings are confirmed instantly or are requests you will confirm.
  • Make it clear what happens if the car needs more work than expected.
  • Offer a phone number for anyone who prefers to call.

Our guide to online booking systems covers the options in more detail. For repairs where the fault is unknown, a simple enquiry form with space to describe the problem, and optional photos, usually works better than a fixed booking slot.

Practical details matter more than usual

Because the customer has to bring the car to you and then get on with their day, practical information is especially important:

  • Opening hours, including early drop-off or key drop arrangements if you offer them.
  • Location and directions, with a map and clear notes on how to find the entrance, especially on industrial estates.
  • Parking for customers dropping off or waiting.
  • Waiting area, if you have one, and what is available.
  • Courtesy cars, collection and delivery, only if you offer them, with any conditions.
  • Getting onward, such as nearby bus routes or a lift to the station, if that is something you provide.

Make sure your opening hours and address match your Google Business Profile exactly. Mismatched details confuse customers.

Trust signals for a garage

Photos of your actual workshop, equipment and team tend to reassure more than stock images of shiny engines. If you belong to a trade association or an approved garage scheme, you can mention it accurately and follow its rules on logos. Explaining that you will show customers the old parts, or send photos of a worn component, can help, if that is something you do. Our guide to website trust signals covers the broader principles.

Clear terms, including how payment works and your approach to guarantees on repairs, also help. Keep your own guarantee on workmanship separate from any manufacturer warranty on parts.

Mobile first

Many people look up a garage on their phone, sometimes from the roadside. Fast loading, tap-to-call, a clear address that opens in a map app, and booking that works well on a small screen are more valuable than visual effects. Our guide to mobile website design goes into detail.

Platform choice

Most garages do not need ecommerce. A WordPress site with an integrated booking tool, or a hosted builder such as Squarespace or Wix with booking features, can work well. If you sell tyres or parts online, an ecommerce platform such as Shopify or WooCommerce may be worth considering, though this adds complexity around fitting appointments.

FAQs

Can I advertise MOTs on my garage website?

Only if you are an authorised MOT test station for the vehicle classes you mention. Otherwise, describe pre-MOT checks or repairs accurately.

Should I publish my prices?

Many garages publish prices for standard jobs and explain how other work is quoted. Be clear about VAT and keep prices current.

Is online booking worth it for a garage?

Often, yes, because MOTs and services are predictable. Keep a simple enquiry form for unknown faults.

Can I say independent servicing keeps the manufacturer warranty?

Word this carefully and suggest customers check their warranty terms. Only describe the parts and procedures you actually follow.

Does a mobile mechanic need a different website?

The structure is similar, but the focus shifts to the areas you cover and how call-outs work, rather than workshop location and parking.
