Cookie Banner Design: How to Build a Clear, Compliant Consent Banner for a UK Website

Cookie banner design guide for UK websites: consent rules in plain terms, equal choices, layout, wording, mobile, analytics and how to test your banner works.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • UK rules generally expect consent before non-essential cookies are set, and accepting and rejecting should be equally easy.
  • A good banner is short, plainly worded, works on mobile and does not block the content people came for longer than needed.
  • Check that the banner actually controls what loads: a banner that looks right but sets tracking cookies anyway is the most common failure.

Almost every UK website now shows a cookie banner, and most of them are an afterthought. They cover half the screen on mobile, bury the reject option behind a settings menu or, worse, look compliant while tracking scripts load in the background regardless of what the visitor chooses. A well-designed cookie banner does the opposite: it explains things briefly, offers a genuine choice and gets out of the way.

This guide covers the design side of cookie consent for UK businesses: what the rules broadly expect, how to lay out the banner, how to word it, how it should behave on mobile and how to check it does what it says. It is general guidance rather than legal advice. If your site handles sensitive data or you are unsure about your obligations, take advice from a qualified professional and read the guidance published by the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO).

What the UK rules broadly expect

Cookie consent in the UK sits mainly under the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR), alongside UK GDPR, which sets the standard for what valid consent looks like. The ICO is the regulator. In broad terms, the position as the ICO describes it is:

  • You need to tell people what cookies and similar technologies you use and why.
  • For cookies that are not strictly necessary to provide the service the user asked for, you generally need consent before setting them.
  • Consent should be freely given, specific, informed and an unambiguous positive action. Pre-ticked boxes and "by continuing to browse you accept" wording are unlikely to count.
  • People should be able to withdraw consent as easily as they gave it.

Strictly necessary cookies, such as those that keep items in a shopping basket or remember that someone is logged in, are generally exempt from the consent requirement, though you should still explain them. Analytics and advertising cookies are generally not treated as strictly necessary. The rules in this area have been subject to change, including reforms that may relax requirements for some low-risk cookies, so check the current ICO guidance rather than relying on what was true a few years ago.

Equal choices: the heart of good banner design

The single most important design principle is that accepting and rejecting non-essential cookies should be equally easy. The ICO has said publicly that it expects websites to make rejecting as straightforward as accepting, and it has written to organisations whose banners did not.

In practice, that means:

  • Reject on the first layer. If "Accept all" is on the banner, "Reject all" (or "Reject non-essential") should be there too, not hidden behind "Manage settings".
  • Similar visual weight. Two buttons of the same size and similar prominence. A bright accept button next to a faint grey text link is a classic nudge.
  • No tricks with wording. Avoid labels like "No thanks, I prefer a worse experience" or double negatives that make the reject option confusing.
  • A clear settings option. Offer a third route for people who want to choose by category, but do not make it the only way to say no.

Some businesses worry that equal choices will reduce the number of people who accept analytics cookies. It may. But a banner designed to manipulate is a compliance risk, and it can undermine trust in a brand at the very first interaction. It is better to plan your measurement around realistic consent levels, which we come back to below.

Layout and placement

There is no single correct layout, but some patterns work better than others.

Bottom bar or corner panel

A bar along the bottom of the screen, or a compact panel in a corner on desktop, lets visitors see the page they arrived on while they decide. This tends to feel less hostile than a full-screen overlay. The trade-off is that people can ignore it, so non-essential cookies should simply stay off until a choice is made.

Centred modal

A centred box with a dimmed background forces a decision. It can be appropriate where you want a clear answer before anything loads, but it interrupts the visit. If you use one, keep it small and make both choices obvious.

Things to avoid

  • Banners that cover the main call to action or the "Add to basket" button on mobile.
  • Banners that reappear on every page because the choice is not being stored.
  • Stacking the cookie banner on top of a newsletter pop-up and a chat widget at the same moment. If you use pop-ups, our guide to website pop-ups done well covers timing so they do not collide.

Writing the banner copy

Cookie banners are often written by copying a template full of legal jargon. Visitors skim them, so short and plain is better. A good banner usually covers three things in two or three sentences:

  1. What you use cookies for, in everyday language: "to keep the site working, understand how it is used and, if you agree, show relevant ads".
  2. That the choice is theirs and non-essential cookies stay off unless they agree.
  3. Where to find more detail: a link to your cookie policy and the settings panel.

Match your brand's tone of voice, but resist being cute. A jokey banner about biscuits can come across as trivialising a privacy choice. If you are working on voice more broadly, our tone of voice guide may help.

The settings panel

The second layer should list cookie categories with a short description of each, for example strictly necessary, analytics, functionality and marketing. Non-essential categories should be off by default, with toggles people can switch on. Include a "Save choices" button and, ideally, "Accept all" and "Reject all" here as well. Avoid listing dozens of individual vendors on the first view; put the detail in an expandable section or in the cookie policy.

Mobile design

Most banners are designed on a desktop monitor and then squeezed onto a phone. On a small screen, a banner that took a sliver of a desktop page can fill most of the viewport. Check:

  • The banner takes up a modest part of the screen and the page behind is still recognisable.
  • Buttons are large enough to tap comfortably and are not crammed side by side with tiny labels.
  • Text is readable without zooming.
  • The settings panel scrolls properly and the save button is reachable.

Our guide to mobile website design covers tap targets and layout in more depth.

Accessibility

A cookie banner is often the first interactive element on the page, so if it is not accessible, some visitors cannot get past it. Make sure it can be operated with a keyboard, that focus moves to the banner when it appears and returns sensibly afterwards, that buttons have meaningful labels for screen readers and that colour contrast meets accessibility standards. Our website accessibility guide explains the wider principles.

Speed and stability

Consent tools are scripts, and a heavy one can slow the page or cause content to jump as the banner loads. Things to look for:

  • The banner should not cause noticeable layout shift. Reserving space for it, or overlaying it rather than pushing content down, helps.
  • The consent script should load early enough to block non-essential tags, but should be as light as possible.
  • Avoid loading several overlapping consent or tag tools left over from previous site versions.

Core Web Vitals can be affected by a poorly implemented banner. Our Core Web Vitals guide explains how these measures work.

Make sure the banner actually controls what loads

This is where many sites fall down. A banner can look perfect and still be ineffective because analytics, advertising pixels or embedded content load before the visitor has chosen, or carry on loading after they click reject. Common causes include tracking code hard-coded into the theme, plugins that add their own scripts, and embedded videos or maps that set cookies on load.

To check, open the site in a private browser window, open the developer tools and look at the cookies and network requests before you click anything. Then reject, browse a few pages and check again. Only strictly necessary cookies should appear until consent is given. Repeat after accepting to confirm analytics and marketing tags do start working.

If you use Google tools, Google provides a consent mode that lets its tags adjust their behaviour based on the visitor's choice. It needs setting up correctly alongside your banner. Our GA4 setup guide covers measurement more broadly.

Living with lower consent rates

Honest banners mean some visitors will decline analytics and marketing cookies, so your reports will not capture every visit. That is a normal consequence of giving people a real choice. Ways to work with it include:

  • Looking at trends and relative changes rather than treating analytics totals as exact.
  • Cross-checking against data that does not depend on cookies, such as orders in your ecommerce platform or enquiries received.
  • Using the consent-aware features available in major analytics and advertising platforms, set up properly.

Platform notes

Shopify includes customer privacy settings and supports consent banners, and there are apps that add more control. WordPress and WooCommerce sites usually rely on a consent plugin. Whichever route you take, the same checks apply: equal choices, sensible defaults, a stored decision, an easy way to change it later, and verification that scripts respect the choice. A footer link such as "Cookie settings" is a simple way to let people revisit their decision; our footer design guide covers what else belongs there.

A quick cookie banner checklist

  • Non-essential cookies are off until the visitor agrees.
  • Accept and reject are both on the first layer, with similar prominence.
  • Copy is short, plain and links to a clear cookie policy.
  • The settings panel groups cookies into categories with non-essential ones off by default.
  • The banner works well on mobile and with a keyboard.
  • The choice is remembered and can be changed later.
  • You have tested that scripts actually respect the choice.

FAQs

Do I need a cookie banner if I only use analytics?

Analytics cookies are generally not treated as strictly necessary under current UK rules, so consent is usually expected. The rules have been under review, so check the latest ICO guidance or take advice for your situation.

Can I just use an "OK" button?

A banner with only an acknowledgement button does not give people a genuine choice, so it is unlikely to meet the consent standard for non-essential cookies.

Should the banner appear on every page?

It should appear until the visitor makes a choice, then stay hidden while that choice is stored. A link to cookie settings should let them change it later.

Does a cookie banner hurt SEO?

Not by itself, but a banner that blocks content, slows the page or shifts the layout can affect user experience. Keep it light and well placed.
