Hair Salon and Barber Website Design: Pages, Booking and Portfolio Done Properly

Hair salon and barber website design for UK shops: online booking, service and price lists, stylist profiles, portfolios and walk-in info built for mobile.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • For most salons and barbers the website's main job is getting a booking made with as few taps as possible.
  • A clear service list, honest duration and price guidance, and real photos of your own work build confidence.
  • Barbers relying on walk-ins need different information up front from appointment-only salons.

When someone looks up a hair salon or barber, they are usually close to making a decision. They have a cut in mind, a rough idea of when they want it and perhaps two or three places to choose between. Your website has a short window to show them your work, tell them what it costs and let them book. Anything that slows that down, such as a menu buried in a PDF or a booking button that opens a confusing page, can lose the appointment to the shop down the road.

This guide covers the website itself: structure, booking, service lists, portfolios and practical design choices for UK hair salons and barbershops. For promotion beyond the website, such as social media, local listings and reviews, see our guide to beauty salon marketing in the UK, which covers hair businesses alongside beauty and nail salons.

Salon or barber: start with how people visit

The single biggest design decision is how customers come to you.

  • Appointment-led salons need booking front and centre. Colour work, long treatments and stylist-specific appointments all depend on time being reserved.
  • Walk-in barbers need opening hours, location and an idea of how busy you tend to be. A big booking button may matter less than a clear note saying walk-ins are welcome.
  • Mixed shops should say plainly that both are possible, and explain the difference, for example that booking guarantees a slot while walk-ins are served as chairs free up.

Getting this right on the homepage removes a common source of uncertainty. People do not want to turn up and be told it is appointment only, or to hunt for a booking system that does not exist.

The core pages

Most hair businesses can work with a compact structure:

  • Home. A strong image of your work or your space, a short line about your style, and a single clear action such as Book now or Walk-ins welcome.
  • Services and prices. Every service you offer, grouped sensibly, with guidance on price and duration.
  • Team. Stylists or barbers with a photo, what they specialise in and, if your booking system supports it, a link to book with them directly.
  • Gallery or portfolio. Your own work, organised so people can find relevant examples.
  • Visit. Address, map, hours, parking, accessibility and anything else useful such as whether children are welcome.
  • Policies. Cancellation, late arrival, deposits and patch tests, written in plain English.

Online booking that actually gets used

Most salons use a dedicated booking system rather than building one into the website. That is sensible: these tools handle staff calendars, reminders and deposits far better than a custom form would. The website's job is to connect to that system cleanly.

Points to consider:

  • Embed or link? Some booking tools can be embedded in a page; others open in a new window or app. Embedding can feel smoother, but test it carefully on phones, where embedded widgets sometimes become cramped.
  • One button, everywhere. Use the same wording and style for the booking button across the site, and keep it visible in the header on mobile.
  • Book with a person. If clients have favourite stylists, link from each team profile directly to that person's availability where your system allows.
  • Explain what happens next. If a deposit or card details are required, say so before people start, so it does not feel like a surprise.

Our guide to online booking systems explores the options and their trade-offs in more detail.

Writing a service and price list people understand

Service menus in hair are notoriously hard to read. Terms like balayage, root shadow, skin fade or restyle mean different things to different people, and prices often depend on hair length, thickness or which stylist you see.

A clearer list usually:

  • Groups services by what the client wants to achieve: cuts, colour, treatments, styling, beard and shave.
  • Adds a one-line plain English explanation for less familiar services.
  • Shows a starting price or price range and approximate time, with a note on what affects the final cost.
  • Explains if prices vary by stylist level, so the difference does not appear only at checkout.
  • Points people towards a consultation if they are unsure what to book.

Keep the list in normal page text, not an image. It will be easier to read on small screens and to update when prices change. Make sure the website and the booking system show the same prices; mismatches cause awkward conversations at the till.

Patch tests and consultations

Many colour products carry manufacturer instructions recommending a skin allergy alert test before use. If your salon requires one, explain on the website when and how it is done and how far in advance clients need to come in. This avoids people booking a colour appointment they cannot go ahead with. The exact requirements depend on the products you use and your insurer's terms, so base the wording on those rather than a general rule.

Portfolio and gallery

Hair is visual. A portfolio of your own work does more to persuade than any description. A few principles:

  • Use your own photographs, taken in consistent, flattering light. Avoid stock images of models, which visitors spot quickly.
  • Organise by type, such as colour, short cuts, curly hair, bridal or fades, so visitors find relevant examples rather than scrolling through everything.
  • Show a range of hair types and textures if you work with them. People want to see someone like them.
  • Get clear consent from clients before posting photos of them, and record it. Images that identify a person are personal data, and the ICO publishes guidance for small businesses on handling it.
  • Compress images so the gallery loads quickly on mobile data.

Many salons put most of their effort into social media. That is fine, but a social feed embedded on the website is not a substitute for a curated gallery: feeds mix in promotions and memes, can slow pages down and may break if the platform changes how embedding works. A small, well-chosen gallery on your own site is more dependable.

Team pages

Clients often choose a stylist rather than a salon. Team profiles help them do that. A good profile includes a natural photo, their specialisms, the kinds of clients they enjoy working with and a booking link. Keep them up to date when people join or leave. An out-of-date team page leads to clients asking for someone who is no longer there.

Design choices that suit hair businesses

The look of the site should match the experience in the chair. A minimalist colour studio, a traditional gentleman's barber and a lively family salon will rightly look very different. Some general points still apply:

  • Let photography lead and keep text tight.
  • Choose fonts and colours that reflect your brand and are easy to read. Our colour and typography guide explains how to balance style and legibility.
  • Design for phones first. Most salon visitors will be on a mobile.
  • Avoid autoplaying video with sound and heavy animation that delays the booking button.
  • Keep accessibility in mind: sufficient contrast, readable text sizes and alt text on images. Our website accessibility guide covers the basics.

Selling products online

Some salons sell the haircare products they use. If that is a small sideline, a simple product list with a note that items are available in the salon may be enough. If you want to ship products, you are effectively running an online shop, with delivery, returns and stock to manage. Platforms such as Shopify or WooCommerce can handle this, but check any supplier terms first, as some professional brands restrict where and how their products are sold online.

Local search and multiple locations

Most salon and barber searches are local. Your Google Business Profile will often be the first thing people see, so your website should agree with it on name, address, phone and hours. If you have several shops, give each its own page with its own team, hours and map. Our local SEO guide covers how listings and websites work together.

Policies, gift vouchers and the small details

Cancellation policies, deposit rules and late arrival terms matter to clients and protect your diary. Put them on a dedicated page and summarise them near the booking button. Write them politely and clearly. If you offer gift vouchers, a simple way to buy them online is often welcome, especially around holidays. Make expiry terms clear and check they are consistent with consumer law; if in doubt, take advice.

FAQs

Can my booking system be built into my website?

Many booking systems offer an embeddable widget or booking link. Whether embedding or linking works better depends on the tool and how it behaves on phones.

Should I show prices on my salon website?

Usually, yes. Starting prices or ranges, with a note on what affects the final cost, help clients choose and reduce surprises.

Is an Instagram feed enough instead of a gallery?

A feed can complement a site, but a curated gallery of your best work, organised by style, is clearer and more reliable.

Do barbers need online booking?

Not always. Walk-in barbers may only need clear hours and location, though many offer booking alongside walk-ins.

Do I need permission to post client photos?

You should get clear consent from clients before posting identifiable photos. The ICO has guidance on handling personal data for small businesses.

Which platform suits a salon website?

Squarespace, Wix and WordPress can all work. The main thing is that it connects well with your booking tool and is easy for you to update.
