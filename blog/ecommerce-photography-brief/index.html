How to Write an Ecommerce Photography Brief for Your Online Shop

How to brief ecommerce product photography: what to include on products, shot lists, style, file delivery and usage rights, so online shop images work.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good photography brief turns what shoppers need to know about a product into a clear shot list, so images answer questions rather than simply look attractive.
  • Agree the practical details in writing before the shoot, including samples, style references, crops, file formats, naming, retouching limits and how images may be used.
  • A brief is just as useful when you shoot in-house, because it keeps images consistent as your range grows and different people take the photographs.

An ecommerce photography brief is a short document telling a photographer, or your own team, exactly which products to shoot, which images each one needs, how they should look and how the files must be delivered. A useful brief covers your goals, a product list, a shot list, style references, technical requirements, retouching rules and usage rights. Writing it first reduces the risk of reshoots and helps your images match what your product pages, marketplaces and adverts actually need.

This guide is for UK online shop owners and ecommerce managers preparing a shoot. It focuses on the brief and the preparation around it. For shot types, lighting and editing, read our practical guide to ecommerce product photography.

Why write a brief at all?

Photographs can be beautiful and still fail on a product page. A jacket shot only from the front does not show how the back falls. A lovely lifestyle image of a candle may not tell anyone how big the candle is. Without clear direction, a photographer has to guess at those priorities, and you may notice the gaps only when you start uploading.

A brief also gives you something to check the finished work against. It makes quotes easier to compare, because each photographer is pricing the same job. And it becomes a reference for future shoots, helping new products sit comfortably beside older ones.

What to include in your brief

Background and purpose

Begin with a few lines about your brand, who you sell to and how you want to come across. Then state where the pictures will appear: product pages, collection pages, the homepage, email, social posts, paid ads or marketplaces. Each may have different needs, so listing them upfront avoids finding out later that a crop will not work.

Product list

Set out every item in a simple spreadsheet. Useful columns include:

  • Product name and SKU, so files can be matched to your catalogue.
  • Variants to be shot, such as colours or finishes.
  • Size and any handling needs, such as fragile, heavy or perishable items.
  • Priority, so the important products get shot first if time runs short.
  • Number of images and types of shot needed.
  • Notes on key selling features that must be visible.

Shot list

This is the heart of the brief. Base it on questions shoppers ask before buying: what does it look like, how big is it, what is it made from, how is it used and what comes in the box? Then translate each answer into a picture.

Shopper questionPossible shot
What exactly is it?Clean main image on a plain background
What does the rest look like?Back, side, top or inside views
What is the quality like?Close-ups of material, stitching, texture or finish
How big is it?Item in a hand, on a model or beside a familiar object
How would I use it?Lifestyle or in-use image
What do I receive?Everything included, along with packaging if relevant

Not every product needs every shot. Group similar products into templates, such as a set of views for clothing and another for homeware. It keeps the brief manageable and gives your pages a consistent look.

Style direction

Words such as clean, premium or natural mean different things to different people, so back them up with examples. Collect a small set of reference images and say what you like about each one, whether the lighting, the angle, the props or the colour. Note what you want to avoid as well.

Be specific about these points:

  • Background colour and surface.
  • Light quality, such as soft and even or more directional.
  • Angles and how much space to leave around the product.
  • Props and settings, and whether they might confuse people about what is included.
  • Models, including the kind of look, poses and whether faces are shown.

Your existing brand guidelines should inform this. If you do not have any, our guide to brand identity design explains what they usually cover.

Technical requirements

Before you specify anything, check what your platform and theme need. A theme may crop images to a fixed shape, so if your collection page expects a square, a tall image could lose the product's top and bottom. Shoot with a consistent framing and leave enough room to crop. Our Shopify product media guide explains how images behave on that platform.

Your brief might set out:

  • Aspect ratios for each use.
  • Delivery resolution and file format, including any high-resolution master files you want.
  • Colour profile, particularly where colour accuracy matters.
  • A file naming convention, ideally built around SKU and view.
  • How files should be delivered and organised.

Delivered files may still need preparing for the web, so decide who will compress and resize them. Our guide to website image optimisation covers that stage.

Retouching rules

Say what is acceptable. Removing dust, fixing small marks and correcting colour may be fine. Altering the product's shape, hiding a real feature or changing a colour could mislead customers and increase returns. As UK consumer law and advertising rules can apply to how products are represented, keep images truthful and take advice if you have concerns, particularly for claims in categories such as beauty or supplements.

Marketplace and advertising needs

Sales channels can have their own image rules, which change over time. Google Shopping, for example, restricts things like promotional overlays on images, and marketplaces can have specific rules for main images. Check current requirements for each channel you use and record them in the brief. If you run Shopping campaigns, see our guide to Google Merchant Center set-up.

Usage rights and credits

Do not leave this to chance. In the UK, copyright in a commissioned photograph does not necessarily pass to the client automatically, so the agreement should say how you may use the images, for how long, in which territories and channels, and whether you can edit them. Check model releases if people appear, and be careful with anything showing third-party brands or artwork. A solicitor can advise on specific wording.

Preparing the products

A good brief can still fall down on the day if products are not ready. Allow time to:

  • Send the correct samples, including every variant listed, and consider spares for anything easily damaged.
  • Clean, steam or assemble items so they look as they should.
  • Remove swing tags or stickers, unless they need to be shown.
  • Check that samples match what customers will actually receive, as prototypes can differ.
  • Label everything so that items can be matched to your list.
  • Agree how samples get back to you, and who is responsible for them while they are away.

Food, cosmetics and other sensitive products may need particular care with storage and handling, so mention this in advance.

Briefing yourself: shooting in-house

If you photograph products in-house, the brief matters no less. Turn it into a simple standard: where the camera goes, how lights are set, how far away the product sits and what files are saved where. Photographs of the set-up can help someone else recreate it later. Over time, this consistency can matter more than having expensive equipment, particularly if you add products regularly.

Checking the images on delivery

Ask to see a test image or a first batch early, if the arrangement allows it. A small sample lets you catch issues with style or framing before a whole shoot is affected. When files arrive, check them against the brief:

  • Is every product, variant and view present?
  • Do colours look true compared with the physical item, on more than one screen?
  • Does each image still work when cropped by your theme, including on a phone?
  • Are file names and formats as agreed?
  • Do the images look consistent with each other when seen together on a collection page?

Agree in advance how many rounds of amends are included and how reshoots are handled if something is missed.

After the shoot: getting images on to your site

The job is not done until the images help someone buy. Upload them in a sensible order, starting with the clearest view. Write useful alt text for each image, using our image alt text guide if you need it. Make sure each variant shows the correct image when selected. Then look at the page as a shopper would, and consider how the pictures work alongside your wording, as covered in our guide to ecommerce product descriptions. For a broader view of what strong pages need, see our product page SEO guide.

FAQs

How long should a photography brief be?

Long enough to prevent guessing, and no longer. A short written overview, a product spreadsheet, a shot list and a few reference images are often enough for a straightforward job.

Should I shoot every colour variant?

Where colour affects the buying decision, shoppers will usually want to see it. If budget is limited, you might shoot the full set for your main sellers and fewer views of other variants. Avoid digitally recolouring images unless the result accurately represents the product.

Do I need lifestyle images as well as plain ones?

That depends on the product. Plain images show what an item is, while lifestyle images can show scale, use and mood. Many shops find a mixture useful, but start with whatever answers your shoppers' questions.

Who owns the photographs after the shoot?

It depends on your agreement, so do not assume. Ask the photographer to confirm ownership or licence terms in writing, covering the uses you have planned, and seek legal advice if the rights are important to you.

Can I reuse the manufacturer's images instead?

Sometimes, if you have permission. Bear in mind that they will also appear on other shops, so they will not help you stand out, and their style may not match yours. Check any supplier's terms first.

Next steps

Pick one product category and draft a shot template for it, based on the questions your customers ask. Test the template against your product page before committing to a whole range.
