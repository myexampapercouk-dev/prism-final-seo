Web Design in Aberdeen: Websites for Energy Sector and Supply Chain Businesses

Web design for Aberdeen energy, engineering and supply chain firms: capability pages, safety credentials, new energy markets and tender support.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Aberdeen supply chain websites are often checked by procurement teams, so capabilities, certifications and contact routes should be easy to verify.
  • Firms moving into renewables and other sectors need sites that present transferable capability honestly rather than rebranding overnight.
  • Prism Works Studio works with Aberdeen businesses remotely, planning, writing and building sites over video calls and shared drafts.

Web design in Aberdeen for an energy or supply chain business is usually about one audience above all: the buyer, engineer or procurement specialist deciding whether your company is credible enough to shortlist. They might be based in the city, elsewhere in the UK or overseas, and they will often visit your website before, during and after a tender. Companies across the area, from industrial estates in Dyce, Altens and Bridge of Don to offices in the city centre and firms out towards Westhill and Portlethen, all face that scrutiny. Prism Works Studio works with Aberdeen businesses remotely, so planning, writing and reviews happen over video calls and shared documents.

Who actually reads a supply chain website

Energy sector purchasing usually involves several people, each looking for different things. A site that serves only one of them can leave the others unconvinced.

ReaderWhat they want to find
Engineers and technical leadsDetailed capabilities, equipment, specifications, standards worked to and technical data sheets
Procurement and contracts staffCertifications, approvals, company details, locations and a reliable contact route
Health, safety and quality teamsSafety approach, management systems and how quality is controlled
Senior decision makersA clear summary of who you are, what you do best and the sectors you serve
Job seekersCurrent roles, training, site locations and working patterns, including rotation work if relevant

Structuring navigation around these readers, for example with clear sections for capabilities, sectors, quality and safety, and careers, makes it easier for each to find their way.

Capability pages that stand up to scrutiny

A single services page listing everything you can do rarely convinces a technical buyer. Separate pages for each core capability let you go into useful detail.

  1. A plain summary of the service and the problems it solves.
  2. The scope: what you deliver, where and in what environments, such as onshore, offshore, subsea or in workshop.
  3. Equipment, facilities and standards you work to, described accurately.
  4. Relevant sectors and applications.
  5. Downloadable data sheets or capability statements where useful.
  6. A direct contact for that service line.

Only describe capabilities you actually have in-house, or be clear where you work with partners. Buyers will check, and overstating capacity can cause problems later in a contract. Our guide to service page design and our engineering consultancy website guide cover structure in more detail.

Safety, quality and certification information

Health, safety, environmental and quality credentials matter a great deal in this sector. They should be visible and verifiable, not tucked into a footer logo strip.

  • Management system certifications genuinely held, with scope and certifying body, and ideally certificate numbers or links where the scheme allows.
  • Supplier registrations and pre-qualifications you hold, named accurately and kept current.
  • A clear statement of your safety approach, written in your own words rather than generic slogans.
  • Policies such as health and safety, environmental and modern slavery statements, where you publish them.

Certifications expire and registrations change, so someone should be responsible for checking these pages regularly. Displaying a certification logo you no longer hold, or using a scheme's mark outside its rules, can cause real reputational damage. Our article on website trust signals covers how to present credentials well.

Presenting a move into new energy markets

Many Aberdeen companies built their capability in oil and gas and are now applying it to offshore wind, hydrogen, carbon capture, decommissioning or entirely different sectors. The website is often where that shift is most visible, and it needs handling with care.

A few principles help:

  • Lead with transferable capability, explaining how skills such as offshore operations, inspection, fabrication or project management apply to the new market.
  • Be specific and honest about what you have already delivered in a new sector and what you are positioned to deliver. Avoid implying experience you do not yet have.
  • Keep existing customers in view. If oil and gas remains a large part of your work, removing it from the site can confuse current clients.
  • Avoid vague environmental claims. Statements about sustainability or net zero should be specific and supportable, in line with the Competition and Markets Authority's guidance on environmental claims.

Sector pages, one for each market you serve, are often a cleaner way to show this range than rewriting the whole site around a single new message. Our rebranding guide covers the wider decisions if the change goes beyond the website.

Writing for international buyers

Aberdeen supply chain companies often sell to operators and contractors around the world. International readers may be reading in a second language and may be unfamiliar with UK-specific terms.

  • Use clear, direct English and define abbreviations the first time they appear.
  • State where you can work, including any overseas bases or mobilisation capability, accurately.
  • Give contact details with international dialling codes and note UK time zones.
  • Consider whether translated capability statements for key markets are worth producing, rather than translating the whole site.

Recruitment for skilled roles

Hiring skilled technicians, engineers and offshore personnel is a major task for many energy businesses, and the careers section is often one of the most visited parts of the site. Clear role pages with responsibilities, qualifications and certificates required, location or rotation details and how to apply help candidates self-select. If you run training or apprenticeship schemes, give them their own page. Applicant data should be handled in line with UK GDPR and explained in your privacy notice.

Supporting tenders and pre-qualification

When you submit a tender or pre-qualification questionnaire, evaluators may visit your website to check what you have said. Keeping key documents in a downloads area, such as a capability statement, company brochure, certificates and policies, saves time for both sides. Make sure the documents match what the website says and are dated so readers know they are current. Our guide to website content audits covers how to keep everything consistent.

Search for specialist B2B services

Energy supply chain businesses rarely chase broad, high-volume searches. The people searching tend to use specific technical terms for a service, a type of equipment or a standard. Dedicated, detailed pages for each capability, written using the language buyers actually use, are usually more effective than a general page. Our B2B SEO guide and manufacturer website design guide explain related approaches, and our B2B sector page sets out how we approach the area.

Security and reliability

Energy sector clients are often security-conscious, and a website that is out of date, slow or showing browser warnings can raise questions. Use HTTPS, keep software and plugins updated, limit administrator access and keep backups. Our guide to WordPress security covers practical steps for one of the most widely used platforms.

Case studies without breaching confidentiality

Project examples are persuasive for technical buyers, but energy sector work is often covered by confidentiality agreements. Check what each contract allows before naming a client, a field or an asset. Where you cannot name anyone, an anonymised summary describing the challenge, the environment, the scope you delivered and the approach taken can still show capability. Get written approval for anything that identifies a client, and keep a simple record of what has been agreed.

FAQs

Should certifications be shown as logos or text?

Both can work, but text with the certification name, scope and certifying body is easier to verify. Follow each scheme's rules on logo use and remove anything that lapses.

How should we present a shift from oil and gas into renewables?

Explain transferable capability, be honest about what you have delivered so far and use sector pages to show each market, rather than removing existing work from the site.

Do we need separate pages for each service line?

Usually, yes. Technical buyers look for detail, and dedicated pages let you describe scope, equipment, standards and contacts for each capability.

What documents should a supply chain website offer for download?

Commonly a capability statement, certificates, key policies and data sheets. Keep them dated and consistent with the website content.

Is it worth translating our website for overseas clients?

Sometimes. Translating a few key documents or pages for priority markets is often more practical than translating the whole site.
