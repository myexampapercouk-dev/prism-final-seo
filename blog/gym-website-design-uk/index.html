Gym Website Design: Memberships, Timetables and First Visits

Website design for UK gyms and fitness studios: membership pages, class timetables, online joining, free trial offers, facilities photos and first-visit nerves.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most gym website visitors want three things quickly: what memberships cost, what the timetable looks like and how to join or try it out.
  • Many potential members feel nervous about walking into a gym for the first time, so showing the space, the people and what a first visit involves can make a real difference.
  • Membership terms should be clear and easy to read before someone signs up, including contract length and how to cancel.

A gym website is often the deciding moment between someone thinking about getting fitter and actually walking through the door. Visitors are usually comparing a few options nearby, often on their phone, and they want answers quickly: how much, when, what is it like and how do I start. Independent gyms and studios have a chance to stand out here, because they can show the personality, community and coaching that larger chains may not offer.

This guide covers website design for UK gyms and fitness businesses, including independent gyms, strength and conditioning gyms, boxing and martial arts clubs, and boutique studios for yoga, Pilates, spin or similar. It focuses on the website itself. For promotion more broadly, including social media and local search, see our guide to gym marketing. Individual coaches may find our guide to personal trainer website design a closer fit.

The questions every visitor brings

Whatever kind of gym you run, most visitors want the same few answers:

  • How much does membership cost, and what are the options?
  • Is there a contract, and how do I cancel?
  • What are the opening hours?
  • What classes are on, and when?
  • What equipment and facilities are there?
  • Can I try it first?
  • Where is it, and is there parking?

Design the homepage and navigation around getting people to those answers fast. A gym site that hides prices behind a contact form or buries the timetable three clicks deep is asking visitors to give up and look elsewhere. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise content.

Membership and pricing pages

The membership page is often the most visited page after the homepage. It should make the options easy to compare. Consider:

  • A clear layout of membership types, such as monthly, annual, off-peak, student or class packs.
  • What each option includes, such as classes, induction or access hours.
  • Any joining fee, stated upfront.
  • Contract length, notice period and how to cancel, in plain language.
  • Pay-as-you-go or day pass options for people not ready to commit.

Clear terms help visitors and reduce disputes later. Consumer protection rules on subscriptions and contract terms apply to memberships, and these have been changing, so check current guidance and make sure your terms, renewal and cancellation processes comply. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout ideas.

Timetables that actually work on a phone

For gyms with classes, the timetable is critical, and it is often where gym websites struggle. A timetable laid out as a wide table can be unreadable on a phone. Better approaches include:

  • A day-by-day view that fits a mobile screen.
  • Filters by class type, instructor or time of day.
  • Clear class descriptions, including intensity and who it suits.
  • Direct booking from the timetable, if your system supports it.

Many gyms use a booking and membership management system that provides an embeddable timetable or widget. The website design should work with that tool rather than duplicating it, so the timetable stays accurate without manual updates. If you embed a third-party widget, test it carefully on mobile and check it matches the look of your site as closely as the tool allows. Our guide to online booking systems covers how these tools connect to a site, and our guide to mobile website design covers getting small screens right.

Joining online

Letting people join online can remove friction, especially for those who decide late in the evening. If your membership system supports online sign-up and payment, consider:

  • Linking to it prominently from the membership page.
  • Keeping the sign-up process as short as possible.
  • Showing the full terms before payment, not after.
  • Explaining what happens next, such as booking an induction or collecting an access fob.

Health questionnaires or medical declarations collect sensitive data, so make sure your privacy notice covers them and only ask what you need.

Free trials and first visits

A free or discounted trial can be a strong offer, but only if it is easy to claim. A short form or online booking for a trial session works well. Explain what the trial includes and whether a card is needed.

Just as important is easing first-visit nerves. Many people feel intimidated walking into a gym. A page about your first visit might cover:

  • What happens when you arrive and who will greet you.
  • Whether an induction is included and what it covers.
  • What to wear and bring.
  • Changing facilities, lockers and showers.
  • Beginner-friendly classes or quieter times.

Reassuring, friendly copy and real photos can help someone who is on the fence take the step.

Showing facilities and atmosphere

Photos and video carry a lot of weight on gym websites. Visitors want to see the space, the equipment and the kind of people who train there. Useful content includes:

  • Wide shots of each training area.
  • Close-ups of key equipment, such as racks, rigs or cardio machines.
  • Classes in action, with consent from anyone identifiable.
  • Changing rooms and other facilities.
  • Short video tours, kept light so pages load quickly.

Real photos show who the gym is for. If your members include beginners, older adults or families, let the imagery reflect that. Overly polished stock images can make a gym feel less welcoming. Our website video guide covers adding video without slowing the site.

Coaches and personal training

If your gym has coaches, instructors or personal trainers, give them a page with photos, specialisms and genuine qualifications, with their consent. Explain how personal training works at your gym and how to book it. For boutique studios, instructor profiles help people find classes with someone they connect with.

Trust and community

Independent gyms often win on community. Your website can reflect this with:

  • Genuine member reviews, linked to their source.
  • Photos from events, challenges or socials, with consent.
  • Information about any beginner programmes, women-only sessions or other specific offers, if you genuinely run them.
  • Accessibility information about your building and equipment.

Avoid before-and-after claims or promises of specific results. Body transformation claims can be misleading and may fall under advertising rules, so if you share member stories, keep them genuine and avoid implying typical outcomes. Our guide to website trust signals covers how to present social proof honestly.

Practical details

Make the basics easy to find on every page:

  • Address with a map and parking information.
  • Opening and staffed hours, including any 24-hour access.
  • Bank holiday and seasonal changes.
  • Phone number and email, tappable on mobile.

If you have more than one site, give each its own page with location-specific details and timetables.

Helping people find you in search

People often search for a gym near them, or for a specific type of training such as a boxing gym, a reformer Pilates class or a strength gym in their town. The website can support this by giving each main type of training its own page, written in the language people actually use, and by making the location clear in page titles, headings and content. Separate pages for classes, personal training and facilities give search engines more to work with than a single long page. Pair this with an accurate business listing; our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile cover the details.

Selling products and add-ons

Some gyms sell branded clothing, supplements or online programmes. A small shop can sit alongside the main site using Shopify, WooCommerce or a website builder's shop feature. Keep it secondary to the membership journey unless retail is a major part of the business.

Choosing a platform

WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all suit gym websites. The bigger decision is often how the site works with your membership and booking system, since that system usually handles timetables, sign-ups and payments. FAQs

Should gyms show membership prices on their website?

Showing prices clearly helps visitors compare and decide. Include joining fees, contract length and cancellation terms so there are no surprises.

How should a gym display its class timetable?

Use a mobile-friendly layout, such as a day-by-day view, ideally pulled from your booking system so it stays accurate and lets people book directly.

Should gyms let people join online?

If your membership system supports it, online joining removes friction. Show full terms before payment and explain what happens next.

How can a gym website help nervous beginners?

Explain what a first visit involves, show real photos of the space and people, and highlight beginner-friendly classes or quieter times.

Can gyms use before-and-after photos?

Be cautious. Transformation claims can be misleading and may fall under advertising rules, so keep any member stories genuine and avoid implying typical results.
