Ecommerce Subscriptions: How to Add Repeat Orders to Your Online Store

A practical guide to selling subscriptions from a UK online store: choosing a model, pricing, the customer portal, cancellations, payments and legal points.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Subscriptions work best for products people genuinely run out of or want to receive regularly, not as a bolt-on for everything.
  • Make the subscription easy to understand, easy to manage and easy to cancel; trust is what keeps subscribers paying.
  • Plan the operational side, such as stock, failed payments and dispatch dates, before you launch the offer.

Repeat orders are one of the most valuable things an online store can have. A customer who buys once is welcome; a customer who receives a delivery every month without having to think about it is the foundation of a steady business. That is why so many UK stores look at adding subscriptions, whether for coffee, pet food, skincare, supplements, cleaning products or curated boxes.

Done well, a subscription offer makes life easier for customers and gives you more predictable revenue. Done badly, it creates confused buyers, support headaches and complaints about payments people did not expect. This guide covers how to decide whether subscriptions suit your products, the main models, how to design the experience and the practical points to plan before launch.

Do subscriptions suit your products?

Not every product makes sense as a subscription. Before building anything, ask a few honest questions:

  • Do customers use it up? Consumables such as food, drink, toiletries and refills are natural candidates because people need them again.
  • Is there a predictable rhythm? If most customers reorder roughly every few weeks, a subscription simply formalises what they already do.
  • Is there novelty or curation? Boxes built around discovery, such as a monthly selection, can work even for non-consumables because the contents change.
  • Would you want it yourself? If you would find a regular delivery annoying rather than helpful, your customers probably would too.

If your products are one-off purchases, such as furniture or a single piece of equipment, a subscription is unlikely to fit. You may be better served by encouraging repeat custom through email and accessories instead.

The main subscription models

Subscriptions come in a few broad shapes. Choosing the right one shapes everything else, from pricing to fulfilment.

Replenishment

The customer picks a product they already like and chooses how often it arrives. This is the simplest model and suits consumables well. The value to the customer is convenience: they never run out.

Curated boxes

You choose the contents, often around a theme, and the customer receives a different selection each time. The value is discovery and surprise, so the quality of curation matters as much as the products themselves.

Membership or access

Customers pay regularly for benefits rather than a fixed product, such as member pricing, early access to new ranges or free delivery. This can sit alongside normal shopping and suits stores with a loyal, engaged audience.

Subscribe and save

A variation on replenishment where customers can choose a one-off purchase or a recurring order on the product page, often with a modest incentive for subscribing. This is a common starting point because it fits into an existing store without a separate offer.

Pricing and incentives

Many stores offer something extra for subscribing, such as a small discount, free delivery or a gift on the first order. Incentives can help, but think carefully about margins. A discount that looks generous on one order adds up over many months, and delivery costs repeat with every dispatch.

Some points to weigh up:

  • Margin after repeat costs: work out what each recurring order earns after product, packaging, postage and payment fees.
  • First order versus ongoing: an introductory offer can attract sign-ups, but be clear about what the price will be afterwards.
  • Price changes: decide how you will handle future price rises and how much notice you will give subscribers.
  • Value beyond discount: convenience, priority stock or exclusive products can be more sustainable than ever-deeper discounts.

Be wary of building a subscription around an incentive so large that customers sign up, take the offer and cancel. Those subscribers cost money to acquire and rarely stay.

Designing the subscription experience

On the product page

Customers need to understand exactly what they are agreeing to before they commit. Make the choice between one-off and subscription obvious, and state clearly:

  • how often orders will arrive and whether the customer can change it,
  • how much each order costs, including delivery,
  • when they will be charged, and
  • how to pause, skip or cancel.

Hiding the recurring nature of a purchase or making it the default without a clear choice damages trust and is likely to cause chargebacks and complaints. Clear wording on the product page helps both customers and search engines understand the offer.

At checkout

Repeat the key terms at checkout: frequency, price, first charge date and how to cancel. Ask for explicit agreement rather than relying on small print. A clean, reassuring checkout matters even more for subscriptions, because the customer is committing to more than a single payment.

The customer portal

Once someone has subscribed, they need a simple place to manage it. A good subscriber area lets customers:

  • see their next delivery date and what it contains,
  • skip an order or change the date,
  • change frequency, quantity or products,
  • update delivery address and payment details, and
  • pause or cancel without needing to contact you.

Self-service reduces support emails and, perhaps counter-intuitively, often helps retention. A customer who can skip a month when they are overstocked has less reason to cancel altogether.

Cancellations: make them easy

It can be tempting to make cancelling difficult in the hope of holding on to subscribers. Resist it. Obstructive cancellation flows frustrate customers, generate complaints and can put you on the wrong side of consumer protection rules.

A better approach is to make cancellation straightforward while offering useful alternatives along the way, such as pausing, skipping or reducing frequency. Asking a short, optional question about why someone is leaving can also tell you a lot about how to improve the offer.

UK consumer law around subscription contracts has been evolving, with rules covering pre-contract information, reminders and ending subscriptions. Requirements can change and depend on your circumstances, so check the current position or take legal advice before launching rather than relying on a general guide.

Payments and failed charges

Recurring payments bring their own challenges. Cards expire, accounts run low and banks sometimes decline charges for security reasons. Plan how your store will handle this:

  • Retry logic: most subscription tools can retry failed payments over a few days before pausing a subscription.
  • Customer notifications: clear, friendly emails asking the customer to update their details, with a direct link to do so.
  • What happens to the order: decide whether you hold dispatch until payment succeeds.

Check how your chosen platform and payment provider support recurring billing, including strong customer authentication requirements. On Shopify, subscriptions are typically handled through the platform's subscription features and compatible apps; on WooCommerce, through extensions. Each option handles portals, payments and emails a little differently, so compare carefully against your needs.

Operations: stock, dispatch and forecasting

One advantage of subscriptions is that you can see future demand more clearly. To benefit from that, your operations need to keep up:

  • Dispatch dates: decide whether everyone ships on a fixed day or on a rolling schedule from their sign-up date.
  • Stock planning: use upcoming renewals to forecast what you need, particularly for curated boxes.
  • Substitutions: have a policy for when a product is out of stock, and tell customers in advance.
  • Packaging: letterbox-friendly packaging can make regular deliveries much more convenient.

Your delivery and returns pages should also explain how subscription orders are handled, including any differences from one-off purchases.

Communicating with subscribers

Subscribers are your most engaged customers, so the emails they receive matter. Useful messages include a welcome email explaining how the subscription works, reminders before each charge or dispatch, confirmation when changes are made and occasional content that adds value, such as tips for using the product. Our guide to ecommerce email marketing covers building these flows in more detail.

Keep the tone helpful rather than pushy. The goal is for each email to reassure subscribers that the arrangement is working in their favour.

Measuring whether it works

Once live, keep an eye on a handful of measures:

  • Sign-up rate: the share of product page visitors or buyers who choose to subscribe.
  • Retention: how many subscribers are still active after one, three and six months.
  • Cancellation reasons: patterns in why people leave.
  • Skips and pauses: a sign of whether frequency options suit customers.
  • Revenue per subscriber: what an average subscriber is worth over time.

These figures help you decide whether to adjust frequency options, incentives or products. They also tell you how much you can sensibly spend acquiring new subscribers through advertising.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Launching subscriptions on products customers do not actually reorder.
  • Unclear terms that lead to surprise charges.
  • Making cancellation difficult.
  • Incentives that erode margin on every order.
  • No plan for failed payments or stock shortages.
  • Forgetting to update policies, emails and help pages.

FAQs

Can I add subscriptions to an existing Shopify or WooCommerce store?

Usually, yes. Both platforms support subscriptions through built-in features, apps or extensions. The right option depends on how you want the customer portal, payments and emails to work.

Should I offer a discount for subscribing?

It can encourage sign-ups, but check that the discount still leaves a healthy margin across repeat orders. Convenience and other benefits can be just as persuasive.

How do I stop subscribers cancelling?

Focus on value and flexibility rather than barriers. Letting customers skip, pause or change frequency easily often keeps them subscribed for longer.

Are there legal rules for UK subscriptions?

Yes. Consumer protection rules cover the information you give, how you take recurring payments and how customers can cancel. The rules can change, so check the current requirements or take professional advice.

What is the best subscription model for a small store?

For many small stores, a simple subscribe-and-save option on existing products is the easiest place to start, because it fits the current range and checkout.
