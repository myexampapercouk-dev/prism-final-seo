Website Terms and Conditions and Privacy Policy: A UK Business Guide

The basics of website terms and conditions, privacy policies and cookie notices for UK businesses and online shops. General guidance, not legal advice.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Websites that collect personal data generally need a privacy notice, and online shops have extra information duties under consumer law.
  • Terms and conditions, privacy notices and cookie information do different jobs, so treat them as separate documents.
  • Use guidance from UK regulators as a starting point, keep documents accurate to how your site actually works, and take legal advice for your situation.

Legal pages are easy to overlook when building a website. They are not exciting, they do not usually win customers, and it is tempting to copy something from another site and move on. But these pages exist for real reasons. They tell visitors how you handle their personal data, set out the rules for using your site or buying from you, and help you meet obligations under UK law. This guide explains the main legal pages a UK business website or online shop is likely to need, what each one is for and how to approach them sensibly.

Important: this article is general information, not legal advice. Requirements depend on your business, what your site does and how you handle data. Rules can change. For documents tailored to your situation, speak to a qualified solicitor and check the latest guidance from the relevant UK regulators.

The main legal pages and what they do

Privacy notice (privacy policy)Explains what personal data you collect, why and how it is usedGenerally any site that collects personal data, including via forms or analytics

Privacy notices

If your website collects personal data, UK data protection law generally requires you to tell people how you use it. Personal data includes names, email addresses and phone numbers submitted through a contact form, customer details captured at checkout, and potentially information collected by analytics or advertising tools. The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) is the UK regulator for data protection and publishes guidance on what a privacy notice should contain.

A privacy notice typically explains:

  • Who you are and how to contact you about data protection
  • What personal data you collect and how you collect it
  • Why you use it and the lawful basis you rely on
  • Who you share it with, such as payment providers, email platforms or delivery companies
  • Whether data is transferred outside the UK
  • How long you keep it
  • People's rights over their data and how to exercise them
  • How to complain, including the right to complain to the ICO

The key principle is accuracy. A privacy notice copied from another business will describe their tools and practices, not yours. Before writing or reviewing yours, list every way your site collects data: forms, newsletter sign-ups, checkout, account creation, live chat, booking tools, analytics and advertising pixels. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design, and the ICO's own guidance is the place to check what your notice should say.

Depending on your circumstances, you may also need to consider whether you must pay the data protection fee to the ICO. The ICO provides a self-assessment tool to help businesses check.

Cookies and consent

Cookies and similar technologies are covered by the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR) alongside data protection law. In broad terms, you generally need to tell people about cookies and get consent for those that are not strictly necessary for the service they have requested, such as many analytics and advertising cookies. The ICO's guidance on cookies explains the current position, which has been subject to legislative change, so check it directly rather than relying on older articles.

Cookie information can sit within your privacy notice or on a separate page. Either way, it should match what your site actually sets. Our guide to cookie banner design explains how to present choices clearly, and our GA4 setup guide touches on how analytics interacts with consent.

Website terms of use

Website terms of use set rules for how people can use your site. They might cover acceptable use, intellectual property in your content, disclaimers about information on the site, links to other websites and which law applies. For a simple brochure site, terms of use are often considered optional, but many businesses include them for clarity. If your site lets users post content, create accounts or download resources, terms of use become more relevant.

Keep them proportionate. A small service business does not usually need pages of dense terms for a five-page site. If you are unsure what is appropriate, ask a solicitor.

Terms and conditions for online shops

If you sell to consumers online, your terms and conditions of sale form part of the contract between you and the customer. UK consumer law sets out information you generally need to give before a customer buys and rights they have that your terms cannot remove. Relevant rules include the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and the Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, which cover areas such as pre-contract information and the right to cancel many distance purchases. Government guidance on selling online and the Competition and Markets Authority's guidance on consumer law are useful starting points.

Shop terms commonly address:

  • Who you are, your contact details and, where relevant, your company and VAT details
  • How the contract is formed and when an order is accepted
  • Prices, including taxes and delivery charges
  • Payment methods
  • Delivery arrangements
  • Cancellation rights and how to exercise them, plus any exceptions
  • Returns, refunds and faulty goods
  • Limitations of liability, within what the law allows
  • How complaints are handled

Terms that try to remove statutory consumer rights are unlikely to be enforceable and can create problems with regulators. Business-to-business sales are treated differently, which is another reason to get advice specific to your situation. Our guide to ecommerce delivery and returns pages covers how to present this information clearly to shoppers.

Platforms such as Shopify, WooCommerce, Squarespace and Wix often provide policy templates or generators. These can be a helpful starting point, but they are generic. Review them carefully and adapt them to your business, or have them checked.

Business information you should show

UK businesses have obligations about identifying themselves. For example, limited companies are generally required to show certain details, such as their registered name, registered number and registered office address, on their websites. Companies House guidance sets out what applies. Online sellers also have information duties under consumer law. A clear contact page and footer can carry much of this. Our guides to website footer design and website trust signals cover how to present it.

Where to place legal pages on your site

  • Footer links to the privacy notice, cookie information and terms on every page.
  • Next to forms, a short note explaining how the data will be used, with a link to the privacy notice.
  • At checkout, clear links to terms, delivery and returns before the customer completes payment.
  • In the cookie banner, a link to fuller cookie information.

Make the pages readable. Plain English, headings and short paragraphs help visitors understand what you are telling them, which is part of the point.

Keeping legal pages up to date

Legal pages are not a one-off task. Review them when you add new tools to the site, change payment or delivery providers, start selling to new countries, launch a newsletter or change how you use customer data. Selling abroad brings further considerations; our guide to selling internationally from a UK online store explains some of them. Include a date showing when each document was last updated.

How a web designer can help, and where they cannot

A web designer or developer can make sure legal pages exist, are linked in the right places, are formatted clearly and match the technology on the site, such as which cookies are set or which form tools are used. They can also set up cookie consent tools and checkout links. What a designer generally should not do is draft legal documents as a substitute for legal advice. FAQs

Does every website need a privacy policy?

If your site collects personal data, which includes contact forms and many analytics tools, you will generally need to provide privacy information. Check the ICO's guidance for your circumstances.

Can I copy another website's terms and conditions?

It is not advisable. Their terms reflect their business, not yours, and copying text may also raise copyright issues. Use templates only as a starting point and adapt them properly.

Do I need terms and conditions for a brochure website?

Website terms of use are often optional for a simple brochure site, but a privacy notice is still likely to be needed if you collect personal data.

Are platform policy templates good enough?

They can be a reasonable starting point, but they are generic. Review and adapt them so they accurately describe your business, and take advice if you are unsure.

Who regulates this in the UK?

The ICO covers data protection and cookies. Consumer law is enforced by bodies including the Competition and Markets Authority and Trading Standards.
