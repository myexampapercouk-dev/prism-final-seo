Holiday Let Website Design: Direct Bookings for Cottages and Self-Catering

Website design for UK holiday lets and cottages: property pages, availability calendars, direct booking, house rules and local guides that help guests book.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A holiday let website works best when each property has its own detailed page with honest photos, sleeping arrangements, accessibility notes and a live availability view.
  • Direct booking only helps if the calendar is reliable, so keep availability synced with any listing platforms you also use to avoid double bookings.
  • Pet, cancellation, deposit and payment terms should be written clearly and checked against your own policies and consumer law, ideally with professional advice.

Owners of holiday cottages, self-catering apartments, lodges and barn conversions often rely heavily on large listing platforms for bookings. Those platforms can bring guests, but they also take commission, control the guest relationship and set many of the rules. A well-built website of your own gives returning guests and people who have found you by name a direct place to book, and it gives you a home for the detail a listing page cannot hold.

This guide is about the website itself for self-catering properties. If you run a hotel, guest house or bed and breakfast with staffed rooms and breakfast service, our guide to hotel and B&B website design is a closer fit. For the wider strategy of moving guests away from commission-based channels, see our guide to hotel direct bookings, much of which applies to holiday lets too.

What makes a holiday let website different

A self-catering guest is renting a whole place for several nights, often with family, friends or pets. They are not choosing a room type; they are imagining a week of their life in your property. That changes what the site needs to do:

  • Show the whole property in enough detail that guests can picture how they will use it.
  • Answer practical questions about beds, bathrooms, kitchen equipment, parking, heating, Wi-Fi and outdoor space.
  • Make availability and the booking route obvious, because guests usually have fixed dates in mind.
  • Set expectations about arrival, departure, cleaning and house rules before anyone books.

Whether you have a single cottage or a small portfolio, the same principles apply. The difference is mainly in how you structure the navigation.

Structuring the site for one property or several

With one property, the home page can effectively be the property page, supported by pages for availability and booking, the local area, FAQs and contact. Keep it simple and let the photos lead.

With several properties, give each one its own page and add a way to compare them. Useful ways to group or filter properties include:

  • Number of guests and bedrooms.
  • Dog-friendly or not.
  • Features such as a hot tub, enclosed garden, wood burner or ground-floor bedroom.
  • Location, if properties are spread across different villages or areas.

A summary card for each property, showing a main photo, sleeps, bedrooms and a short line about its character, helps visitors narrow the list before diving into detail.

The property page is the heart of the site

Each property page should answer almost every question a guest might ask. A practical order is:

  1. A short introduction that captures what the place is like.
  2. A photo gallery covering every room, the outside space and the view, in a sensible order.
  3. Sleeping arrangements, bedroom by bedroom: bed sizes, whether twins can be zipped together, and any cots or sofa beds.
  4. Bathrooms, including whether there is a bath, shower or both.
  5. Kitchen and living areas, listing the main appliances and equipment.
  6. Outdoor space, parking and access.
  7. Availability and the booking route.
  8. House rules, arrival and departure times, and what is provided.

A floor plan, even a simple sketch, can be very helpful, particularly for groups deciding who sleeps where or for guests with limited mobility.

Photography that sets honest expectations

Photos sell holiday lets, but they also shape reviews. A wide-angle lens that makes a small room look huge can lead to disappointment on arrival. Aim for bright, well-composed images that represent rooms fairly. Include the less glamorous but important things too: the parking area, the steps to the front door, the size of the garden.

On the website, make sure galleries load quickly on a phone. Large uncompressed images are a common cause of slow pages. Images should be sized and compressed properly, with descriptive alt text for each one. Our guide to website speed optimisation covers the technical side.

Availability calendars and direct booking

For many owners, the main reason to have a website is direct booking. There are broadly three approaches:

  • An enquiry form, where guests request dates and you confirm by email. This is simple, but slower, and some guests will book elsewhere while they wait.
  • A calendar with a booking request, showing availability and letting guests request specific dates for you to approve.
  • Instant online booking with payment, using a booking system or channel manager that takes a deposit or full payment at the time of booking.

Whichever you choose, the calendar must be accurate. If you also list on other platforms, availability needs to stay in sync, usually through calendar feeds or a channel manager, so a week booked in one place is blocked everywhere else. Test this regularly. A double booking is stressful for you and far worse for the guests who arrive to find someone already there.

Many booking tools for holiday lets can be embedded in sites built on WordPress, Squarespace or Wix. Check how the booking widget looks and behaves on a phone, since it is often the least polished part of the page. Our guide to online booking systems covers what to look for more generally.

Showing prices clearly

Guests want to know the cost of their stay before they invest time. If your prices vary by season, show a clear guide, such as a price range or a seasonal table, and make sure the booking system shows the full price for the chosen dates before payment, including any cleaning fees, pet charges or other extras. Avoid surprises at the final step. Hidden or unclear charges can frustrate guests and may raise consumer protection concerns, so take advice if you are unsure how to present pricing.

Terms, deposits and cancellation

Your booking terms deserve a clear, readable page, linked from every booking step. These typically cover deposits and balance dates, cancellation, security deposits or damage policies, maximum occupancy and what happens if a guest arrives late or leaves early. Write them in plain English and make sure they match how you actually operate.

Consumer law applies to bookings taken from guests, and the rules around cancellations, refunds and unfair terms can be complex. It is sensible to have your terms reviewed by a solicitor or an appropriate trade body rather than copying another owner's wording. The website should present your terms clearly; it cannot make them fair or compliant on its own.

Dogs, accessibility and special requirements

Dog-friendly properties attract a lot of interest, and dog owners have specific questions. If you accept dogs, state how many, whether there is a charge, which areas they can use, whether the garden is enclosed and any rules about furniture. If you do not accept dogs, say so plainly to save everyone time.

Accessibility information is valuable and often missing. Rather than simply labelling a property accessible or not, describe it: steps to the entrance, ground-floor bedrooms and bathrooms, door widths if you know them, and shower type. Let guests judge for themselves. The site itself should also be usable for people with disabilities; see our guide to website accessibility.

Local area guides

A local area section helps guests plan their stay and gives search engines useful context about where your property is. Write about walks, beaches, villages, places to eat and things to do in your own words, based on what you genuinely know. Keep it updated; a recommended pub that closed two years ago undermines the rest of the page.

This content can help people searching for holiday cottages in your area find you, especially when combined with a clear location on every property page. Avoid inflated claims such as calling a property the best in the region unless you can stand behind them.

Guest information before and after booking

Your website can hold arrival information, directions and house guides for booked guests, either as private pages or as downloadable documents sent after booking. This reduces repeated questions by email and phone. Be careful not to publish key safe codes, alarm codes or anything sensitive on public pages.

Reviews and trust

Guests booking directly may feel less protected than when booking through a big platform, so trust signals matter. Genuine guest reviews, clear contact details, a named owner or business, secure payment and transparent terms all help. Only publish reviews that guests have actually given, and do not edit them to change their meaning. If you display a quality grading from a recognised tourism scheme, make sure it is current. Our guide to website trust signals has more on this.

Licensing and registration

Rules for short-term and holiday lets vary across the UK and have been changing. Scotland, for example, introduced a licensing scheme for short-term lets, and other parts of the UK have introduced or proposed registration or planning requirements. Fire safety and other obligations also apply to holiday accommodation. Check the current rules for your location with your local authority or a qualified adviser. Where you hold a licence or registration number that should be displayed, include it accurately on the site.

Common mistakes on holiday let websites

  • Out-of-date availability calendars that do not match listing platforms.
  • Prices that only appear at the very end of the booking process.
  • Vague sleeping arrangements, such as "sleeps 6" with no detail on beds.
  • Photo galleries that are slow or awkward to browse on a phone.
  • Terms and conditions buried or missing entirely.
  • No mention of dogs, accessibility or parking, leading to repeated questions.

FAQs

Do I need a website if I already list on booking platforms?

It is not essential, but a website gives returning guests and people who find you by name a direct way to book, and lets you present your property in more depth.

Can I take bookings directly on my website?

Yes, using a booking system that handles availability and payments. Make sure it syncs with any other platforms you use.

Should every property have its own page?

Yes. Separate pages let you describe each property fully and give each one a better chance of being found in search.

Should I show prices on the website?

Generally, yes. At least a seasonal guide, with the full price for chosen dates shown clearly before payment.

Do holiday lets need a licence?

It depends on where the property is. Rules differ across the UK and have been changing, so check with your local authority or a qualified adviser.
