Ecommerce Size Guides: How to Help Customers Choose the Right Fit Online

How to create ecommerce size guides for UK online shops: measurements, fit notes, placement on product pages, mobile design and reducing size-related returns.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Customers buying clothing, footwear and many other products online need to judge fit without trying anything on; a clear size guide fills that gap.
  • Use real garment or product measurements, explain how to measure, and add fit notes that describe how items actually wear.
  • Put size help right beside the size selector, make it work on mobile and keep it specific to the product where fit varies.

Choosing a size is one of the hardest parts of buying online. In a shop, customers can try something on or hold it up. Online, they have a chart, a photo and their own guesswork. When the guess is wrong, the result is a return, a disappointed customer and the cost of handling the parcel twice. A good size guide will not remove every sizing problem, but it gives customers what they need to choose with more confidence.

This guide is for UK online shops selling clothing, footwear, accessories and other products where size matters, from rings and hats to furniture and bedding. It covers what to put in a size guide, how to present it on product pages, how to handle fit and how to keep improving it over time.

What a size guide is for

A size guide has one job: help the customer pick the size that will fit them, first time. That sounds obvious, but many size guides fall short because they:

  • Use a generic chart copied from a supplier that does not match the actual products.
  • List body measurements without explaining how to take them.
  • Mix UK, EU and US sizing without making it clear which is which.
  • Sit in a pop-up that is unreadable on a phone.
  • Ignore the fact that two items labelled "M" can fit very differently.

Treat the size guide as part of the product information, not an afterthought in the footer.

Body measurements or product measurements?

There are two kinds of measurement you can show, and many shops benefit from both.

Body measurements

These tell customers which size is designed for their chest, waist, hips, inside leg or foot length. They are useful for helping people map themselves to your size labels. They do depend on customers measuring themselves accurately, so instructions matter.

Product (garment) measurements

These describe the item itself: chest width laid flat, length from shoulder, sleeve length, rise, inside leg, and so on. They help customers compare with something they already own that fits well, which can be more reliable than a body measurement. They are particularly useful for loose, oversized or unusually cut items.

If you only have time for one, think about what your customers find most useful. For tailored or fitted items, body measurements are a common starting point. For relaxed fits, vintage or one-off items, product measurements are often essential.

What to include

  • A clear table with sizes as rows or columns and measurements in centimetres, with inches if your customers expect them.
  • Size conversions where useful, such as UK, EU and US equivalents, labelled clearly. Conversions vary between brands, so present them as a guide.
  • How to measure, with simple illustrations or a diagram showing where each measurement is taken.
  • Fit notes describing how the item is meant to fit: slim, regular, relaxed, oversized, true to size, or whether to size up or down.
  • Model information, such as the model's height and the size they are wearing, if you show products on people. Only include this where you have the real details.
  • Fabric and stretch, since a stretchy fabric and a rigid one fit differently even at the same measurements.
  • A route to help, such as a contact link for people who are between sizes.

Make it specific where fit varies

A single store-wide size chart is easy to maintain but can be misleading if your products come from different manufacturers or have different cuts. Consider:

  • Category-level guides for broad groups, such as tops, trousers, shoes and children's wear.
  • Product-level fit notes for anything that differs from the norm, such as "this style runs small; we suggest sizing up".
  • Product-level measurements for items with distinctive cuts, one-off pieces or second-hand stock.

Most platforms can handle this. On Shopify, size guides can be attached to products through theme settings, metafields or apps. On WooCommerce, you can use custom fields, tabs or plugins. The aim is to show the right guide on the right product without manually editing hundreds of pages.

Where to put the size guide

Placement matters as much as content. The best place for a size guide link is right next to the size selector, because that is the moment the customer is deciding. A link labelled "Size guide" or "Find your size" beside the size buttons is a widely used pattern.

When clicked, the guide should open without taking people away from the product page, typically in a drawer, panel or modal. Closing it should return them to exactly where they were, with any selections intact. Some shops also repeat key fit notes in the product description or a dedicated tab, which helps customers who scroll rather than click.

A standalone size guide page is also useful for customers who want to check before browsing, and for search: people do search for sizing information. Link it from the footer or help section. Our guide to footer design covers what usually goes there.

Designing for mobile

Size tables are wide, and phones are narrow. A table with eight columns of measurements is a common mobile problem. Ways to handle it include:

  • Letting the table scroll horizontally within its container, with the size column fixed.
  • Showing one size at a time, with a selector to switch.
  • Splitting measurements into separate, smaller tables.
  • Offering a centimetres/inches toggle rather than showing both side by side.

Test the guide on actual phones. If people have to pinch and zoom to read it, many will guess instead. Our mobile website design guide covers responsive layouts more broadly.

Accessibility

Size tables should be built as proper HTML tables with headers, not as images. Images of charts cannot be read by screen readers, cannot be zoomed cleanly and are harder to update. The drawer or modal containing the guide should be keyboard-accessible and should move focus correctly. Our website accessibility guide has more detail.

Beyond the chart: other ways to help with fit

A chart is the foundation, but other content can help customers decide:

  • Product reviews that mention fit. Prompting reviewers to say whether the item was true to size can be very useful for other shoppers. Our guide to product reviews covers collecting them.
  • Photos on different body types, where you can produce them, with honest captions.
  • Clear product photography showing length and silhouette. See our product photography guide.
  • Fit finder tools, which ask customers questions and recommend a size. These can help for some shops, but they add cost and complexity and depend on good underlying data.
  • Customer service by email or chat for people between sizes.

Size guides and returns

Wrong size is commonly cited as a reason for clothing returns, and online shops in the UK generally have to allow customers to cancel most distance purchases within a set period. A better size guide cannot remove that right, nor should it try to; but it can reduce avoidable returns. It is worth tracking return reasons in your ecommerce platform so you can see which products or sizes cause the most size-related returns and improve their guidance. Clear returns information also helps customers feel confident buying; our guide to delivery and returns pages covers that side. For the specifics of your legal obligations on cancellations and returns, check current guidance or take advice.

Keeping size guides accurate

Size guides go out of date quietly. A new supplier, a revised pattern or a change of fabric can make an old chart wrong. Build checks into your routine:

  • Measure samples of new products before they go live rather than relying solely on supplier charts.
  • Review fit notes when return reasons or reviews suggest a problem.
  • Keep size guide data in one place, such as a metafield or reusable block, so updates apply everywhere.
  • Check that variant names on products match the sizes listed in the guide. Our product variants guide covers variant setup.

Size guides and SEO

Sizing content can support search visibility. A product page that answers fit questions in text is more useful to shoppers and gives search engines more relevant content to understand. A standalone size guide page can also answer searches about your brand's sizing. Keep the information as crawlable text rather than images, and avoid duplicating huge identical charts across thousands of product pages if a shared guide will do. FAQs

Should I use the size chart my supplier gives me?

It can be a starting point, but check it against real samples. Supplier charts are sometimes generic or based on different sizing conventions.

Should size guides show centimetres or inches?

Many UK shoppers are comfortable with both. Centimetres with an inches option is a common approach.

Where should the size guide link go?

Right next to the size selector on the product page, opening without leaving the page.

Do I need a separate size guide for each product?

Not always. Category-level guides work for consistent ranges, with product-specific notes or measurements where the fit differs.
