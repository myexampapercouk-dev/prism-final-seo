Selling on Amazon vs Your Own Website: Which Is Right for a UK Brand?

Amazon gives instant access to shoppers but takes fees and control; your own website builds a brand and customer data. Compare both before you choose.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Amazon puts products in front of shoppers who are ready to buy, but you pay fees, compete on price and own little of the customer relationship.
  • Your own website costs more effort to attract traffic, but you control branding, pricing, customer data and repeat sales.
  • Many UK brands use both: a marketplace for reach and discovery, and their own site as the home of the brand and its best customers.

Selling on Amazon gives you immediate access to a huge number of shoppers who are already in a buying mood, while selling on your own website gives you control over your brand, prices and customer relationships. Amazon is usually faster to start and harder to own; your own site is slower to grow and easier to build long-term value in.

For most UK product businesses the question is less "which one?" and more "which first, and in what balance?". This guide compares selling on Amazon vs your own website on traffic, cost, control, data and risk, so you can make a decision that fits your products and margins. Marketplace fees, rules and seller programmes change, so always check Amazon's current seller information before committing.

The case for selling on Amazon

The biggest advantage is demand. Many shoppers start product searches on Amazon rather than Google, so a well-optimised listing can be seen by people ready to buy without you building traffic from nothing. Trust is also built in: buyers know how delivery, returns and payment work, which helps a new or unknown brand.

Fulfilment programmes can take care of storage, packing and delivery, which suits sellers without warehouse space, though they come with their own fees and requirements. Amazon is also a useful testing ground: you can see whether a product sells before investing heavily in your own store.

Visibility on Amazon depends on listing quality, reviews, price, availability and advertising. Our guide to Amazon SEO covers how titles, bullet points, backend keywords and A+ content affect where products appear. Similar ideas apply on eBay, covered in our eBay SEO guide.

The downsides of relying on Amazon

  • Fees add up. Referral fees, fulfilment fees, storage charges and advertising can take a large share of each sale. Work out your margin per unit with current fees before listing.
  • Price competition. Shoppers can compare similar products side by side, which pushes prices down.
  • Little customer relationship. You cannot freely market to Amazon buyers, so repeat business is harder to build.
  • Platform dependence. Policy changes, listing suppressions or account issues can cut off sales quickly.
  • Limited brand expression. Your product sits in a standard layout next to competitors, so building a distinctive brand is harder.

The case for your own website

Your own online shop is the one sales channel you control. You set the design, the product story, the prices, the bundles and the checkout experience. You collect customer details (with proper consent) and can build email flows, loyalty offers and remarketing audiences. Over time, repeat customers and organic traffic can make each sale cheaper to win.

A website also supports everything else you do. Press coverage, social posts, influencer mentions and Google Shopping ads all need somewhere to send people, and a branded site turns that attention into customers you can keep in touch with. Our guide to ecommerce email marketing shows how owning the customer relationship turns into repeat sales.

The downsides of your own website

  • You need to bring the traffic. A new shop has no visitors until you build SEO, run ads or grow an audience.
  • Upfront investment. Building a good store takes time and money, plus ongoing platform and app costs.
  • Trust takes work. Clear delivery and returns pages, reviews and secure payment options matter more when shoppers do not know you.
  • Operations are yours. Unless you use a fulfilment partner, you handle stock, packing, delivery and returns.

The fairest comparison is cost per sale after all fees and marketing, not headline fees alone.

PlatformSeller account planEcommerce platform subscription and apps

A spreadsheet with your own numbers per product is worth more than any general rule. Some products are profitable on Amazon and not on your site, and the reverse is equally common.

Using both: a common approach for UK brands

Many brands run both channels with different jobs. Amazon brings discovery and volume, especially for products people already search for there. The website is the home of the brand, with the full range, bundles, gift options and a direct relationship with loyal customers. Some brands keep certain products or bundles exclusive to their own site to give customers a reason to buy direct.

If you run both, keep stock in sync to avoid overselling, keep product information consistent, and check marketplace rules about pricing and how you contact buyers. Platforms such as Shopify offer sales channel integrations; our guide to Shopify sales channels explains how they work.

Legal and practical points for UK sellers

Whichever channel you use, some responsibilities stay with you. Products must meet UK safety and labelling rules for their category, and VAT registration depends on your turnover and circumstances, so check current HMRC guidance or speak to an accountant. Marketplaces may collect and account for VAT on certain sales, which affects your pricing, so read their current tax information carefully.

On your own website you also need clear terms, a privacy policy, delivery information and a returns process that reflects UK consumer law, including cancellation rights for most online purchases. Our guide to delivery and returns pages explains what shoppers expect to see. On Amazon, much of this is handled through the marketplace, but you must still meet its standards for dispatch times, cancellations and customer service to keep your account in good health.

How to decide which to start with

  1. Check where your product is searched. Look at whether shoppers search for products like yours on Amazon, Google or both.
  2. Calculate margin per channel. Include every fee, delivery cost and likely advertising spend.
  3. Consider your brand. If your story, design and customer experience are your edge, your own site matters more.
  4. Think about repeat purchases. Consumables and subscriptions reward owning the customer relationship.
  5. Plan for both over time. Even if you start on one, decide how the other will fit in later.

FAQs

Selling on Amazon vs your own website: which is better?

Amazon is usually better for fast access to ready-to-buy shoppers, while your own website is better for brand control, customer data and repeat sales. Many UK brands use both, with each channel doing a different job.

Is it cheaper to sell on Amazon or my own website?

It depends on your products and margins. Amazon has per-sale fees but brings traffic; your own site has lower per-sale fees but you pay to attract visitors. Compare total cost per sale for each channel.

Can I sell the same products on Amazon and my own website?

Yes, many brands do. Keep stock levels in sync, keep product details consistent and check current marketplace policies on pricing and customer contact.

Should a new product brand start on Amazon?

Amazon can be a useful way to test demand and gain early sales, but build your own site alongside it so you are not wholly dependent on one marketplace and can start building direct customer relationships.

Do I own my customers when I sell on Amazon?

Not in the way you do on your own site. Amazon limits how sellers can contact buyers and use their details, so building a direct marketing list from marketplace sales is restricted.

Our e-commerce builds typically take 5-12 weeks depending on the number of products, integrations and design work involved, with time needed afterwards to build traffic.
