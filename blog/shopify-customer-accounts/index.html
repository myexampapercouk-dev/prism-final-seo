Shopify Customer Accounts: New vs Classic and What to Set

Shopify customer accounts explained: how new and classic accounts differ, whether to require sign-in at checkout, and what UK store owners should set.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify offers two account types: newer customer accounts that sign in with a one-time code, and classic accounts that use a password.
  • For most stores, keep accounts optional at checkout so nobody is forced to sign up before paying.
  • Check how your theme, apps and any customer-only content behave before switching account types, and test on a copy first.

Customer accounts let shoppers sign in to your Shopify store to see past orders, track deliveries, manage addresses and reorder. They also give you a cleaner picture of repeat customers. Shopify offers two versions of accounts, and the settings around them affect checkout, returning customers and how your theme behaves.

This guide explains the difference between the newer customer accounts and classic accounts, the main options in the admin, and sensible choices for a UK store. For the wider design questions around accounts and saved items, see our guide to ecommerce customer accounts and wishlists.

Where the settings live

In the Shopify admin, go to Settings, then Customer accounts. This screen controls whether accounts are shown at all, which version you use, and some related options. Shopify updates this area from time to time, so labels may differ slightly from what is described here. Check the current Shopify help centre if something does not match.

New customer accounts

The newer version does not use passwords. A customer enters their email address and Shopify sends a one-time code to sign in. Accounts are hosted by Shopify on a separate account area, with a consistent layout for orders, addresses and profile details.

Points to understand:

  • No passwords to forget. Customers do not need to create or remember a password, which removes a common source of frustration and support emails.
  • Order history and status. Customers can see their orders and, depending on setup, track them and request returns.
  • Branding options. You can apply your logo and colours, but the layout is controlled by Shopify rather than your theme.
  • Extensions. Shopify allows extra features to be added to the account area through approved extensions, which developers can build or install.

Because the account pages are not part of your theme, any customisation you previously made to classic account templates will not carry over automatically.

Classic customer accounts

Classic accounts use an email address and password. The account pages are templates inside your theme, so a developer can change their layout and content more freely.

Classic accounts may suit stores that rely heavily on theme-based account customisation or older apps built around them. Shopify has been moving its focus to the newer version, so check the current position in the Shopify help centre before building anything new around classic accounts.

Should customers need an account to buy?

Shopify lets you choose how accounts appear at checkout. The main choices are along these lines:

  • Accounts off or hidden. Customers check out as guests only.
  • Accounts optional. Customers can sign in if they want, or check out as a guest.
  • Accounts required. Customers must sign in or create an account before checking out.

For most consumer stores, optional is the sensible default. Forcing sign-up adds a step between a shopper and payment, and many people simply want to buy and leave. Required accounts make more sense for trade or wholesale stores, members-only shops, or stores where prices and products depend on who the customer is.

Checkout friction is covered in more detail in our Shopify checkout settings guide.

What customers can do in their account

Depending on the version and your setup, customers can typically:

  • View past orders and order status.
  • Save and edit delivery addresses.
  • Update their name and contact details.
  • Reorder previous purchases or start a return, where enabled.
  • Manage marketing preferences, where your setup exposes them.

Think about which of these matter to your customers. A store selling consumables that people reorder often gets more value from accounts than a store selling one-off items.

Switching from classic to new accounts

If your store uses classic accounts and you are considering the newer version, plan the change rather than flipping a switch on a busy day.

  1. List what touches accounts. Theme templates, header sign-in links, loyalty schemes, wishlists, wholesale pricing, subscriptions and any apps that read customer login status.
  2. Check compatibility. Ask each app provider, or your developer, whether their feature works with new customer accounts.
  3. Review customer-only content. If parts of your theme show different content to logged-in customers, test that it still works.
  4. Test on a duplicate theme or development store. Sign in, place a test order, view history and check every link.
  5. Tell customers if needed. Existing customers sign in with a code instead of a password. A short note on the sign-in page or in an email can avoid confusion.

Our Shopify apps audit guide is a useful way to review what you have installed before making the change.

Customer data and privacy

Accounts store personal data such as names, addresses and order history. As a UK business you have responsibilities under data protection law for how you collect and use it. Make sure your privacy notice explains what you hold and why, only ask for information you actually need, and handle requests from customers to see or delete their data. This is general information, not legal advice, so check current ICO guidance or take advice for your situation.

Staff access matters too. Only give team members the access they need to customer records. Our Shopify staff accounts and permissions guide covers this.

Making the most of accounts in your theme

Whichever version you use, a few theme details make accounts more useful:

  • A clear sign-in link. Usually an account icon in the header, labelled for screen readers and easy to tap on mobile.
  • Reasons to sign in. Tell customers what they get: order tracking, faster checkout, easy reordering.
  • Consistent branding. Set your logo and colours so the account area does not feel like a different site.
  • Helpful order confirmation emails. Include a link to view the order, which works for guests and account holders.

Common mistakes

  • Requiring accounts on a consumer store without a clear reason.
  • Switching account versions without testing how apps and theme features respond.
  • Leaving the sign-in link hidden or broken after a theme change.
  • Collecting more personal data than needed at sign-up.
  • Not checking the account area on mobile, where many customers will view it.

Accounts for trade and wholesale customers

If you sell to businesses as well as consumers, accounts become more important. Trade buyers often need to see different prices, order in larger quantities, pay on agreed terms or reorder the same items regularly. Shopify offers business-to-business features on some plans, and these rely on customers signing in so the store knows who they are.

Before setting this up, decide how trade customers will be approved. A simple application form, reviewed by a member of staff, keeps control in your hands. Make sure the approval process is clear on the site, so trade buyers know what to expect and consumers are not confused by trade-only messages. Check which features are available on your current plan in the Shopify help centre before planning around them.

Measuring whether accounts are worth it

Accounts are only useful if customers use them. A few things are worth watching over time in your Shopify reports and analytics:

  • How many orders come from signed-in customers compared with guests.
  • Whether returning customers tend to sign in or check out as guests again.
  • Support questions about signing in, passwords or order tracking.
  • Whether customers use features such as reordering or returns from their account.

If very few people sign in, think about whether the benefits are clear enough on the site, or whether accounts simply do not suit how your customers buy. Neither answer is wrong. The aim is a setup that fits your store, rather than one copied from somewhere else.

FAQs

What is the difference between new and classic Shopify customer accounts?

New customer accounts use a one-time code sent by email instead of a password and are hosted by Shopify. Classic accounts use a password and are built from templates in your theme.

Should I make customers create an account to check out?

For most consumer stores, no. Optional accounts let people buy as guests while giving returning customers the choice to sign in. Required accounts suit trade, wholesale or members-only stores.

Will my apps work with new customer accounts?

It depends on the app. Check with each provider or your developer, and test on a duplicate theme or development store before switching.

Can I customise the look of new customer accounts?

You can apply branding such as your logo and colours, and extensions can add features. The overall layout is controlled by Shopify rather than your theme templates.

Do guest customers still get order updates?

Yes. Guests receive order confirmation and shipping emails, and can usually view the order status from the link in those emails.

What happens to existing customers if I switch versions?

Their customer records stay in Shopify. The main change is how they sign in, so let them know about the code-based sign-in to avoid confusion.
