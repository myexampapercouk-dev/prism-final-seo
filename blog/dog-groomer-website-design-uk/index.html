Dog Groomer Website Design: Bookings, Breeds and Nervous Owners

Website design for UK dog groomers and pet services: online booking, breed-based services, new client forms, mobile grooming areas and building owner trust.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • For most groomers, the website's core job is to make booking simple and reduce no-shows and back-and-forth messages.
  • Owners worry about how their dog will be treated, so photos of your space, clear handling policies and honest information about anxious dogs build trust.
  • Services and prices often depend on breed, size and coat condition, and the site should explain that rather than hide it.

Dog grooming is a personal service with a twist: the customer paying is not the one being groomed. Owners want to know their dog will be safe, calm and handled kindly, and they want booking to be easy. Many groomers manage everything through messages and phone calls, which works until the diary is full and the inbox is overflowing. A well-planned website can take on much of that admin and help new owners feel confident before they ever walk through the door.

This guide covers website design for UK dog groomers, including salon-based, home-based and mobile grooming businesses, and touches on related pet services such as dog walking and day care. It focuses on the site itself rather than wider promotion. For local visibility, see our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile.

Booking is the main event

For most groomers, online booking is the feature that changes day-to-day life. A good booking setup can:

  • Show available slots so owners do not need to message to ask.
  • Match services to appointment lengths, for example a full groom taking longer than a nail trim.
  • Send reminders, which can help reduce missed appointments.
  • Take deposits, if you choose to, under a clearly stated policy.

The tricky part with grooming is that appointment length depends on the dog. A small short-coated dog and a large double-coated breed are very different jobs. Some groomers handle this by letting existing clients book online while asking new clients to submit a form first. Others offer service options by size or coat type. Either approach works if it is explained clearly.

Our guide to online booking systems covers the main options and how they connect to a website. Platforms such as Squarespace, Wix and WordPress can all work with booking tools, so the choice often comes down to which booking system suits your diary and how you want to take payments.

New client forms

A new client form helps you prepare and spot potential issues before the first appointment. Useful information might include:

  • Owner's name and contact details.
  • Dog's name, breed or cross, age and approximate size.
  • Coat condition, including any matting.
  • Health conditions, allergies or mobility issues that may affect grooming.
  • Behaviour notes, such as nervousness, reactions to dryers or handling sensitivities.
  • Vaccination status, if you have a policy on it.
  • Vet details, if you ask for them.

Explain why you ask for each piece of information; it reassures owners that you take care seriously. Because you are collecting personal data, your privacy notice should explain how you use and store it. See our guide to website contact forms for keeping forms friendly and focused.

Services and pricing by breed and coat

Grooming prices often vary by size, breed, coat type and condition. Rather than hiding this, the website can explain it clearly:

  • List your main services, such as full groom, bath and brush, puppy introduction, hand stripping, nail trims and de-shedding treatments.
  • Explain what each service includes in plain terms.
  • Describe how size, coat and condition affect time and cost.
  • Be upfront about matted coats. Many groomers have policies on de-matting versus clipping short for welfare reasons, and explaining yours in advance avoids difficult conversations at collection.

If you display prices, keep them up to date and make clear that final prices may vary after you have seen the dog. Alternatively, link to a price guide or ask people to request a quote. Either way, clarity helps.

Reassuring nervous owners (and dogs)

Many owners worry about how their dog will cope, especially rescue dogs, elderly dogs or puppies. Your website is a chance to show how you approach this, as long as you only describe what you genuinely do:

  • Photos of your grooming space, so owners can picture where their dog will be.
  • An explanation of how you handle anxious dogs, such as breaks, quieter appointment times or shorter introductory sessions, if you offer these.
  • Your policy on whether owners can stay, drop off or wait nearby.
  • How long dogs typically stay with you and how you let owners know they are ready.
  • Whether you use cage drying or other drying methods, explained honestly.

Puppy introduction sessions are worth a dedicated page if you offer them. Owners of new puppies are often actively searching, and a clear page explaining what happens at a first visit can be a strong way to win long-term clients.

Photos: before and after, done well

Before and after photos are naturally appealing in grooming. A few tips:

  • Photograph against a consistent, uncluttered background.
  • Ask owners whether you can share photos of their dog online, and avoid including owners or anything identifying without permission.
  • Use a range of breeds and coat types to show your experience.
  • Compress images so the gallery loads quickly on phones. Our guide to website speed optimisation explains why this matters.

A curated gallery on your site complements social media rather than replacing it. You control how it looks, and it does not depend on any platform's algorithm.

Mobile groomers and service areas

If you run a mobile grooming van or groom in clients' homes, your website needs to answer "do you come to me?" immediately. A clear list of towns or postcodes covered, any extra charge for longer distances, and what you need on arrival (such as parking space or access to power and water, depending on your setup) all reduce wasted messages. A simple postcode check on the booking form can help too.

Policies that protect your diary

Cancellations and late arrivals can be costly for a small grooming business. Clear, friendly policies on the website help:

  • Cancellation and rescheduling notice periods.
  • What happens with late arrivals or late collections.
  • Deposits, if you take them, and when they are refunded.
  • Your approach if a dog becomes too stressed to finish a groom safely.

Make these visible before booking rather than buried at the bottom of a page. If you take payments online, link to your terms at the point of payment.

Licensing and other pet services

Some pet businesses need licences from their local council. In England, for example, dog day care and home boarding are licensable activities, while grooming on its own generally is not. Rules differ across the UK nations and can change. If you offer boarding, day care or similar services, check with your local council and only describe licences you actually hold. If you display a licence or star rating, show it accurately.

If you also offer dog walking, day care or pet sitting, give each its own page. Owners searching for a walker have different questions from those booking a groom, such as group sizes, insurance and how keys are handled.

Trust details

  • Your name, face and a little about your background with dogs.
  • Any grooming qualifications or first aid training you genuinely hold.
  • Insurance, described plainly.
  • Genuine reviews, with a link to where people can read them.
  • Your address or the area you work in, and opening hours.

Selling products alongside grooming

Some groomers sell shampoos, brushes, treats or accessories. A small online shop can work if you have the stock and time to fulfil orders. Squarespace, Wix and Shopify can all add a shop to a services site. If retail is a bigger part of your plans, our guide to Shopify for pet product brands covers it in more depth.

FAQs

Should dog groomers offer online booking?

For many groomers it saves a lot of messaging. Some limit online booking to existing clients and ask new clients to fill in a form first, because appointment length depends on the dog.

Should I show prices on my grooming website?

Showing prices or price ranges by size and coat helps owners, as long as you explain that final prices may vary after you see the dog.

What should a new client form include?

Typically the dog's breed, age, size, coat condition, health issues and behaviour notes, plus owner contact details and any vaccination information you require.

Do dog groomers need a licence?

Grooming alone generally does not need a licence in England, but related services such as day care and boarding can. Rules vary by nation, so check with your local council.

How should mobile groomers show their service area?

List towns or postcodes clearly, explain any travel charges and consider a postcode check in the booking form.
