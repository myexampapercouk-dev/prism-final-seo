Charity Website Design: Donations, Volunteers and Trust

Website design for UK charities: clear mission pages, donation journeys, Gift Aid, volunteering sign-ups, impact reporting and accessible design on a budget.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • A charity website serves several audiences at once, including donors, volunteers, service users and funders, so navigation should give each a clear route.
  • Donation pages should be short, secure and honest, with Gift Aid declarations worded in line with HMRC guidance.
  • Show your registered charity number where required and describe impact accurately, using figures you can evidence.

A charity's website has to work harder than most. It may need to raise money, recruit volunteers, help people find support, reassure funders and explain what the charity does, often on a small budget and maintained by staff or volunteers with plenty of other responsibilities. Good design helps each of those audiences find what they need quickly and gives the organisation a site it can actually keep up to date.

This guide focuses on the website itself. For fundraising campaigns, social media, email and advertising, see our guide to charity marketing in the UK. If you run a smaller community or faith group rather than a registered charity, much of this still applies, scaled down.

Know who the website is for

Most charity websites serve several groups:

  • People seeking help, who may be stressed, in crisis or using a phone with limited data.
  • Donors, from one-off givers to regular supporters and legacy pledgers.
  • Volunteers, looking for roles, time commitments and how to apply.
  • Funders and partners, checking governance, accounts and impact.
  • Fundraisers, taking on challenges or organising events for you.

Their needs differ, and sometimes conflict. A person looking for urgent help should not have to get past a donation pop-up to find a phone number. List these audiences, decide what each most needs, and design the navigation around them.

A structure that works for many charities

  • Get help or Our services, placed prominently if you support people directly.
  • About us, covering mission, history, trustees, staff and governance.
  • Donate, with clear routes for one-off, monthly and other giving.
  • Get involved, covering volunteering, fundraising events and campaigns.
  • Our impact, with reports, stories and annual reviews.
  • News, if you will keep it updated.
  • Contact.

Keep the top-level navigation short. A visible Donate button in the header is common, but if your primary purpose is supporting people, a clear Get help link deserves equal prominence.

Designing for people who need support

If your charity provides services, these pages may be the most important on the site. Write in plain, calm language. Explain who the service is for, what happens when someone gets in touch, and how to reach you, including opening hours. Where relevant, signpost emergency services and other sources of urgent help.

For sensitive topics, consider a quick exit button that takes users away from the site immediately, and be mindful that browsing history can be seen by others. Avoid collecting more personal information than you need in contact forms.

Donation pages

The donation journey should be short, clear and secure. Good practice includes:

  • Letting donors choose one-off or monthly giving on the same page.
  • Suggesting amounts, optionally with a short honest description of what a gift can help with, alongside a free amount field.
  • Asking only for the information you need to process the donation and claim Gift Aid.
  • Offering common payment methods and a mobile-friendly checkout.
  • Confirming the donation clearly afterwards, with a thank-you message and receipt.

Most charities use a payment or donation platform rather than building their own payment handling. These can often be embedded in sites built on WordPress, Squarespace or Wix. Check how the embedded form looks on a phone and whether it can carry your branding, so donors are not confused by a sudden change of style.

Descriptions such as "this amount could fund" should be realistic and based on your actual costs. Avoid implying a gift will be spent on one specific item if donations go into general funds.

Gift Aid declarations

Gift Aid can add significantly to eligible donations from UK taxpayers, so most charities collect declarations online. The wording of a Gift Aid declaration matters, and HMRC publishes guidance on what it should include. Use wording that follows that guidance, and keep records of declarations as required. If you are unsure, check with HMRC guidance or a charity finance adviser rather than copying another organisation's form.

Volunteering pages

Volunteers want to know what roles exist, how much time each needs, where they take place, what training is provided and whether any checks are involved. A page for each role, or a filterable list if you have many, helps people find the right fit. Keep the sign-up form simple and tell applicants what happens next and when they can expect to hear back.

If roles involve working with children or vulnerable adults, you may need to mention background checks. Describe these accurately and in line with your safeguarding policy.

Showing impact honestly

Donors and funders want to know their support makes a difference. Impact pages can include stories, annual reviews and key figures. Use numbers you can evidence from your own records, explain what they measure, and keep them dated so readers know how current they are.

Stories from people you have helped can be powerful, but they must be shared with informed consent, and you should consider anonymising details or changing names where there is any risk to the person. Do not combine details from different people into a composite story without saying so.

Trust, governance and legal details

People want to know a charity is legitimate before they give. In England and Wales, registered charities with an annual income above a certain threshold are generally required to state their registered status on websites and certain documents, and similar rules apply elsewhere in the UK. Check current requirements with the relevant regulator: the Charity Commission for England and Wales, the Office of the Scottish Charity Regulator or the Charity Commission for Northern Ireland. Including your charity number in the footer is common practice.

Other trust signals include trustee information, links to your published accounts, your safeguarding and complaints policies, and, if you are registered with the Fundraising Regulator, the relevant badge used according to its rules. Only display registrations you actually hold. Our guide to website trust signals covers the broader principles.

Data protection and consent

Charities often collect personal data through donations, volunteering and contact forms. This must be handled in line with UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act, and electronic marketing consent is also covered by the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations. Provide a clear privacy notice, ask for marketing consent in a way that meets these rules, and avoid pre-ticked boxes. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance; take professional advice where needed.

Accessibility

Charity websites are often used by people with disabilities, older people and people on low-cost devices. Accessibility is therefore central, not optional. Use readable text sizes and contrast, meaningful headings, alt text for images, keyboard-friendly navigation and accessible forms. Consider plain-language and easy-read versions of key information if your audience would benefit. See our guide to website accessibility for more.

Keeping it manageable

Many charity websites become outdated because nobody has time to maintain them. Choose a platform your team can update easily, such as WordPress, Squarespace or Wix, and keep the structure simple enough that a volunteer can add news or events without breaking anything. Remove pages that will not be kept current. A smaller site that is accurate is better than a large one full of old events and broken links.

Common mistakes on charity websites

  • Help information hidden below fundraising messages.
  • Long donation forms that ask for unnecessary details.
  • Impact figures with no source or date.
  • Missing charity number or governance information.
  • Out-of-date events and news pages.
  • Poor accessibility and slow mobile performance.

FAQs

Do I need to show my charity number on the website?

Many registered charities are required to state their registered status, depending on income and where they are registered. Check current rules with your regulator; showing it in the footer is common practice.

Can I take donations directly on the website?

Yes, usually through an embedded donation or payment platform. Make sure it is secure and works well on a phone.

How should I word a Gift Aid declaration?

Follow HMRC guidance on what a declaration must include, and keep proper records.

Should services or donations come first?

It depends on your purpose. If you support people directly, make help easy to find before asking for money.

Which platform is best for a small charity?

One your team can maintain. WordPress, Squarespace and Wix are all common choices.
