Yoga and Pilates Studio Website Design: Timetables, Classes and Memberships

Website design for UK yoga and Pilates studios: class timetables, booking and passes, beginner pages, teacher profiles, retreats and accessible design.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • For most yoga and Pilates studios, the timetable and booking journey is the heart of the website, so it should be quick to use on a phone.
  • Beginners are often unsure which class to choose, so plain explanations of class types, levels and what to expect can make a real difference.
  • Class passes, memberships and intro offers should be explained clearly, with terms visible before anyone pays.

A yoga or Pilates studio website has a slightly unusual job. It needs to serve regulars who just want to book tomorrow's class in a few taps, and it needs to welcome nervous beginners who are not sure what vinyasa means or whether reformer Pilates is right for them. Getting both right, without the site feeling cluttered, is what good studio web design is about.

This guide covers website design for UK yoga studios, mat and reformer Pilates studios, and teachers running classes from hired spaces. It focuses on the website itself. If you run a larger gym or fitness centre, our guide to gym website design may be a closer fit, and one-to-one coaches may find our guide to personal trainer website design useful. For getting found locally, see our guide to local SEO.

What a studio website needs to do

For most studios, the main jobs of the website are to:

  • Show the timetable clearly and let people book or join a waitlist.
  • Explain class types and levels in plain language.
  • Set out passes, memberships and intro offers.
  • Introduce teachers and the feel of the studio.
  • Promote workshops, courses, teacher training or retreats where relevant.
  • Make location, parking, changing facilities and accessibility details easy to find.

Everything else is secondary. A calm, well-structured site that does these jobs well will usually serve students better than one packed with features.

The timetable and booking journey

For regulars, the website is mostly a route to the timetable. That route should be short. A clear link in the main navigation, a timetable that works on a small screen, and the ability to book in a few steps are the priorities.

Many studios use dedicated class booking software, which can usually be embedded on a website or linked from it. If you already use one, the design should make it feel part of the site rather than a jarring jump to a different look. If you are choosing a system, think about:

  • How well the timetable displays on mobile.
  • Whether students can book, cancel and join waitlists themselves.
  • How passes, memberships and intro offers are sold and tracked.
  • Whether it handles limited-capacity classes, such as reformer sessions with a set number of machines.
  • How it handles online or hybrid classes, if you run them.

Our guide to online booking systems covers the general options. Whichever system you use, test the full journey on a phone, from finding a class to receiving confirmation, because small frictions add up.

Explaining class types and levels

Beginners often find studio timetables confusing. Class names like flow, yin, hatha, ashtanga, mat Pilates, reformer and barre mean little to someone new. A short class guide, with a page or section for each type, can help. For each class, explain:

  • What the class involves and how it feels, in everyday language.
  • Who it suits, and whether any experience is needed.
  • How long it lasts and what to bring or wear.
  • Any equipment involved, such as reformer machines.

Labelling classes on the timetable by level, such as beginner, all levels or more experienced, helps people choose without needing to ask. Separate class pages can also support people searching for a specific type of class in your area, such as reformer Pilates or hot yoga.

Be careful with health claims. It is fine to describe what a class involves, but avoid promising specific health outcomes, such as curing back pain or treating medical conditions. Advertising rules, enforced by the Advertising Standards Authority, apply to claims on websites, and health-related claims need to be supportable. Encouraging people with injuries, health conditions or pregnancies to check with a medical professional and speak to the teacher before class is a sensible approach.

A page for first-timers

A dedicated new students page can do a lot of work. It might cover:

  • Which classes are best to start with.
  • What to wear and bring, and whether mats are provided.
  • How early to arrive and what happens when you get there.
  • How intro offers work, if you have them.
  • A short note on etiquette, such as phones and late arrivals.

Linking this page from the homepage and the timetable means beginners can find it without hunting. A warm, encouraging tone suits this page, as many people feel nervous walking into their first class.

Passes, memberships and offers

Studios often have several ways to pay: drop-in classes, class packs, monthly memberships and intro offers. These can get confusing quickly. A clear pricing page, ideally with a simple comparison, helps people choose. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout principles.

Make the terms easy to find. Expiry dates on class packs, notice periods on memberships, cancellation windows for booked classes and late cancellation charges should be shown before anyone pays. Consumer law can affect what terms are fair, particularly for recurring memberships, so taking advice may be sensible if you are unsure. Clear terms upfront also tend to reduce awkward conversations later.

Teacher profiles and studio feel

Many students follow a particular teacher, so short teacher profiles are worth including. A photo, a few lines about their approach, the classes they teach and relevant training or qualifications can all help. Only list qualifications that teachers genuinely hold, and keep profiles updated when teachers join or leave.

Photos of the actual studio, natural light, the reformer room, changing areas, help people picture themselves there. Real photos usually feel more trustworthy than stock images. If you photograph classes, make sure the people in the photos are happy to appear on the website.

Workshops, courses, teacher training and retreats

Beyond weekly classes, many studios run workshops, beginner courses, teacher training or retreats. These often need more detail than a timetable entry: dates, schedule, what is included, prerequisites, payment options and cancellation terms. Giving each a dedicated page makes them easier to share and promote.

Retreats in particular can involve accommodation, travel and larger payments. If you organise them, be clear about exactly what is included, who is responsible for what, and the cancellation terms. Depending on how they are structured, package travel rules may apply, so it is worth checking your position before selling retreats through your website.

Online classes and on-demand content

Some studios offer live-streamed classes or an on-demand video library. If you do, make the online offer clear and separate from in-studio classes, so people do not book the wrong thing. On-demand libraries can be hosted through booking software, membership platforms or a members area on your own site. Our guide to using video on your website covers some of the practical considerations, such as hosting and page speed.

Accessibility and inclusive design

Yoga and Pilates attract a wide range of people, including older students, people recovering from injury and people with disabilities. Accessible design matters for all of them. Clear text, good contrast, descriptive link text and forms that work with screen readers are good practice for any site. Our guide to website accessibility covers the basics.

Physical access information is also important. Say whether the studio has step-free access, where the nearest parking is, and what changing facilities are available. If you offer adapted classes, chair yoga or classes for specific groups, make them easy to find.

Health information and privacy

Many studios ask new students to complete a health questionnaire. This is sensitive personal data under UK GDPR, so only ask what you genuinely need, explain how it will be used and stored, and make sure your privacy notice covers it. If your booking software collects this information, check how it handles it. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for small businesses.

Design and tone

Studios often want a calm, uncluttered look, and that suits the purpose. Plenty of white space, readable typography and a restrained colour palette can work well, as long as key actions such as booking remain obvious. Our guide to homepage design covers how to balance atmosphere with clear calls to action.

Choosing a platform

Most studio sites are content sites with an embedded booking system, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all work. If you plan to sell products such as mats or clothing, or run a substantial on-demand library, that may influence the choice. FAQs

Can I embed my class booking system on my website?

Most class booking systems offer a way to embed a timetable or link to a booking page. The design should make that step feel consistent with the rest of the site, and it should work well on mobile.

Should a yoga studio website list prices?

Generally yes. Clear prices for drop-ins, class packs and memberships help people decide, and terms such as expiry and notice periods should be visible before payment.

How can a studio website help beginners?

A new students page explaining which classes to start with, what to bring and what to expect, along with clear level labels on the timetable, can help beginners feel confident booking.

Can yoga studios make health claims on their website?

Describe classes accurately but avoid promising specific health outcomes. Advertising rules apply to website claims, and health claims need to be supportable.

Do reformer Pilates studios need different booking features?

Reformer classes usually have a fixed number of machines, so booking needs to handle limited capacity, waitlists and late cancellations reliably.
