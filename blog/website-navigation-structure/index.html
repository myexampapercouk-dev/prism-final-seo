Website Navigation and Structure: How to Organise Your Site

How to plan website navigation and site structure: grouping pages, menu labels, URL structure, footers, breadcrumbs and internal links for SEO.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Organise pages around how customers think about what you offer, not around your internal departments.
  • Use plain, specific menu labels and keep the main menu short; the footer and inner pages can carry the rest.
  • A clear structure helps visitors and search engines alike, so plan it before design, not after.

Website navigation is the set of menus and links people use to find their way around; site structure is the way your pages are grouped and connected underneath. Get the structure right and the navigation almost designs itself. Get it wrong and no amount of visual polish will stop visitors getting lost.

Start with an inventory

Before thinking about menus, list every page you have or plan to have. For an existing site, a crawl or a simple export from your content management system will do. Mark which pages matter most to customers and to the business, which are out of date, and which overlap. Many structural problems come from pages that should have been merged or removed long ago.

Group pages the way customers think

A common mistake is to mirror the organisation chart. Customers do not care which team handles what; they care about their problem. Useful ways to group pages include:

  • By service or product type, the most common approach.
  • By audience, where you serve distinct groups with different needs, such as homeowners and landlords.
  • By task, such as "Book", "Track an order" or "Get support", for sites with lots of returning users.

If you are unsure how people group things, a simple card-sorting exercise helps: write page names on cards and ask a few customers to sort them into piles that make sense to them.

Keep the hierarchy shallow

As a rule of thumb, important pages should be reachable within a few clicks of the homepage. A typical structure looks like this:

LevelExample (service business)Example (online shop)
TopHomepageHomepage
SectionServicesWomen's clothing
Sub-sectionBoiler installationCoats and jackets
DetailCombi boiler replacementIndividual product

Deep chains of sub-menus are hard to use, especially on phones, and pages buried far from the homepage tend to receive less attention from search engines too.

Writing menu labels

  • Use the words customers use. "Kitchen fitting" is clearer than "Interior solutions".
  • Be specific. "Services" is fine as a parent, but the items beneath it should be concrete.
  • Keep labels short so they fit on one line on smaller screens.
  • Put the most important items first and keep "Contact" easy to find, often at the end or as a button.

Main menu, footer and everything else

The main menu should hold the handful of sections most visitors need. Everything else has a home elsewhere:

  • Footer: contact details, policies, delivery and returns, careers, and secondary pages.
  • In-page links: links within body copy to related services, guides and next steps.
  • Breadcrumbs: a trail such as Home > Services > Boiler installation, which helps people orient themselves and gives search engines extra context.
  • Search: worth adding on larger sites and most online shops, as long as the results are good.

Mega menus, which open into a large panel of links, can suit shops with many categories, but they need careful design to stay usable on touch screens and with a keyboard.

URLs that reflect the structure

Readable URLs that follow your hierarchy, such as /services/boiler-installation/, are easier to understand and share. Pick a pattern and keep it consistent. If you change URLs on an existing site, set up 301 redirects from old addresses to new ones; our guide to redesigning without losing rankings explains why this matters.

Structure and SEO

Search engines use links to discover pages and to understand which ones are most important. A clear structure with sensible internal linking helps each page do its job: one page per main topic, linked from its parent section and from related pages. Watch out for orphan pages that nothing links to, and for several pages competing for the same subject. Our technical SEO audit checklist covers how to check for these.

Mobile navigation

On phones, menus usually collapse behind an icon. That makes clear labels even more important, because people see fewer options at once. Make tap targets large enough, keep the number of levels low, and consider keeping one key action, such as a call or basket button, visible at all times.

Structures for different kinds of site

Small service businesses

A small service site often needs only a handful of sections: home, services, about, perhaps examples of work, a blog or guides area, and contact. The key decision is whether each service gets its own page. Usually it should, because a dedicated page can explain the service properly, answer specific questions and give search engines a clear page to show for that topic. A single "Services" page listing everything briefly can still exist as a hub that links to each one.

Online shops

Shops live or die by their category structure. Organise categories around how customers browse, which may be by product type, by room, by occasion or by brand. Products can often sit in more than one category, which is useful for browsing but needs care so the same product does not appear under several different URLs. Keep the number of top-level categories manageable, and use sub-categories and filters for the detail rather than creating a separate menu item for every variation.

Content-heavy sites

If you publish lots of guides or articles, group them into topics rather than relying on a single chronological feed. Topic hubs that introduce a subject and link to the detailed articles beneath it make it easier for readers to explore and help show search engines how your content fits together.

Testing whether your structure works

You do not need specialist tools to get useful feedback. A simple approach is tree testing: give a few people a text-only version of your menu and ask them where they would go to complete specific tasks, such as "find out whether you cover my area" or "return an item". Where people hesitate or choose the wrong section, the labels or grouping need another look.

Your analytics can help too. Look at what people type into site search, which can reveal pages they expected to find in the menu, and at journeys where visitors bounce between sections without settling. Heatmap and session-recording tools can add detail, but be mindful of privacy and consent requirements if you use them.

Common structural problems

  • Duplicate or overlapping pages: two pages covering nearly the same thing, often written years apart.
  • Catch-all pages: one long page trying to cover many unrelated topics.
  • Orphaned content: pages that exist but are not linked from anywhere sensible.
  • Jargon labels: menu items named after internal products or campaigns that customers do not recognise.
  • Navigation that differs by page: menus that change between sections, so people lose their bearings.

Maintaining structure over time

Structure tends to decay as new pages are added without a plan. Agree where new content should live before it is published, review the menu at least once a year, and retire or merge pages that no longer serve a purpose, redirecting their URLs to the closest equivalent. Keeping a simple sitemap document, even a spreadsheet, makes these decisions much easier.

FAQs

How many items should a main menu have?

There is no fixed number, but fewer is usually better. If your menu is getting long, that is often a sign some items belong in sub-menus, the footer or elsewhere.

Should I use a hamburger menu on desktop?

On desktop there is usually room to show the main sections, and visible options tend to be easier to use than hidden ones. Hamburger menus are more common on mobile, where space is tight.

Do breadcrumbs help SEO?

They can help search engines understand how pages relate and may appear in search results. Their main value, though, is helping visitors see where they are.

Will changing my navigation affect rankings?

It can, because navigation changes how pages are linked. Changing menu links alone is usually lower risk than changing URLs, but plan any restructure carefully and monitor afterwards.

Should my blog be in the main menu?

If it is a regular source of useful content for customers, yes. If it is rarely updated, a footer link may be more appropriate.

What is the difference between a sitemap and site structure?

Site structure is how your pages are organised and linked. A sitemap is a list of those pages, either a planning document for people or an XML file that helps search engines discover URLs. A good sitemap reflects a good structure but cannot fix a poor one.

How do I know if my navigation is confusing?

Watch a few people try to complete common tasks, review site search terms, and look for pages with unexpectedly low traffic that should be popular.
