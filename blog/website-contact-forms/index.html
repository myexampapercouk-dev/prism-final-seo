Website Contact Forms That Convert: Design, Fields and Follow-Up

How to design website contact forms that convert: which fields to ask for, placement, mobile usability, spam protection, privacy and follow-up.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Ask only for what you need to respond well; every extra field gives people a reason to give up.
  • Tell visitors what happens after they press send, and make the button say what it does.
  • A form is only as good as the follow-up: route enquiries quickly and test the whole journey regularly.

A website contact form that converts is short, clearly placed, easy to complete on a phone, and honest about what happens next. Most underperforming forms fail for simple reasons: too many fields, vague buttons, confusing errors, or enquiries that land in an inbox nobody checks. Fixing those basics is often one of the quickest ways to get more from the traffic you already have.

Decide what the form is for

A general "get in touch" form, a quote request and a booking form do different jobs. Before designing, decide what a good enquiry looks like and what you need to know to respond usefully. That determines the fields, not the other way round.

Choosing fields

Each field should earn its place. A useful test: if you would not change your reply based on the answer, do not ask yet.

FieldKeep it?Notes
NameUsuallyA single name field is often enough
EmailYesUse the email input type so phones show the right keyboard
PhoneOnly if you will callConsider making it optional
MessageUsuallyA prompt like "What do you need help with?" gets better answers than "Message"
Budget or timescaleSometimesUseful for qualifying, but offer ranges rather than a blank box
Company, job title, addressRarely at firstAsk later in the conversation unless essential

If you genuinely need more information, consider a short multi-step form that starts with the easiest questions. People who have started are more likely to finish.

Placement and context

  • Put a form or a clear route to one on every page where someone might be ready to act, not only on the contact page.
  • Place it next to the content that answers last-minute doubts: what you do, rough pricing guidance, and what happens next.
  • Offer alternatives such as phone or email for people who prefer them.

Wording that helps

Replace "Submit" with a button that says what happens, such as "Request a quote" or "Send my enquiry". Add a line near the button setting expectations, for example when you aim to reply. Only make promises you can keep. Clear labels above fields work better than placeholder text that disappears when someone starts typing. Our website copywriting guide has more on microcopy.

Different forms for different jobs

General enquiry forms

These suit visitors who are not sure exactly what they need. Keep them short and use an open question that invites context, such as "Tell us a little about your project". A dropdown asking what the enquiry is about can help route messages to the right person, but keep the options few and clear, and include an "Other" choice.

Quote request forms

Quotes need more detail, so these forms can justify a few extra questions, such as the type of service, approximate size or quantity and preferred timescale. Use dropdowns, radio buttons and ranges where you can, because choosing is easier than typing. Explain why you are asking: "This helps us give you an accurate quote" makes people more willing to answer.

Booking and callback forms

If you offer consultations or calls, letting people pick a time directly removes back-and-forth emails. Where live booking is not practical, a callback form asking for a preferred time of day is a simple alternative.

Ecommerce contact forms

For online stores, most contact form messages are about existing orders. Asking for an order number up front, and linking to delivery and returns information near the form, helps customers find answers and helps your team respond quickly. Reducing hesitation

Some visitors are ready to enquire but hold back because they are unsure what happens next or worry about being pressured. Small additions near the form can help:

  • A brief explanation of the next step, such as a reply by email or a short call.
  • Answers to two or three common questions, especially about pricing approach and timescales.
  • A note on how you handle their details, with a link to your privacy policy.
  • Genuine reviews or examples of work, where you have permission to show them.

Only include what is true and current. Unrealistic promises about response times or results damage trust as soon as they are broken.

Measuring and improving

Treat your forms as something to improve over time rather than set and forget. Track how many people view a page with a form, how many start filling it in and how many finish. If many start but few finish, the form itself is likely the problem: too long, confusing or broken on certain devices. If few start, the issue may be the page, the offer or the placement.

Small tests can be revealing. Try removing a field, changing the button text or moving the form higher on the page, and compare results over a sensible period. Look at the quality of enquiries too, not only the number. A form that generates more messages but fewer genuine leads may not be an improvement.

Mobile and accessibility

Many enquiries start on a phone. Make fields large enough to tap, use the correct input types, avoid tiny checkboxes and make sure the form works with autofill. For accessibility, every field needs a proper label, errors must be explained in text (not only colour), and the form should be usable with a keyboard. See our website accessibility guide.

Errors and validation

Validate gently. Show errors next to the relevant field, explain how to fix them, and never clear what someone has already typed. Avoid overly strict rules, such as rejecting phone numbers with spaces.

Spam protection

Spam is a real nuisance, but heavy-handed protection can block genuine enquiries. Lighter options, such as hidden honeypot fields and server-side filtering, are often enough. If you use a challenge tool, choose one that is as unobtrusive as possible and check it does not create accessibility problems.

Privacy and consent

A contact form collects personal data, so UK data protection rules apply. In general, you should explain how you will use the information, link to your privacy policy, and keep marketing consent separate from the enquiry itself, for example with an unticked opt-in box. This is general information rather than legal advice; check your own obligations, and take advice if you are unsure.

After the button is pressed

  • Show a clear confirmation message, or a thank-you page that explains what happens next.
  • Consider an automatic confirmation email so people know it arrived.
  • Send enquiries somewhere they will be seen quickly, such as a shared inbox or CRM, rather than one person's email.
  • Track form submissions as conversions in your analytics so you can see which pages and channels produce enquiries.
  • Test the form regularly. Forms break silently after updates, and a broken form can cost enquiries for weeks before anyone notices.

FAQs

How many fields should a contact form have?

As few as you need to respond well. For many businesses that is name, email and a message, with optional extras only where they change your reply.

Are multi-step forms better?

They can help when you genuinely need more information, because starting with easy questions builds momentum. For simple enquiries, a single short form is usually best.

Should I include a phone number field?

Only if you plan to call. If you do, consider making it optional and saying why you are asking.

Do I need a consent checkbox on my contact form?

Not always for responding to an enquiry, but you should explain how data is used and keep any marketing consent separate. Check your own obligations under UK data protection law.

Should my contact form be on every page?

Not necessarily the full form, but every page where someone might be ready to act should offer a clear route to it, such as a button in the header or at the end of the content. Service and landing pages often benefit from having the form on the page itself.

What should the thank-you page say?

Confirm the message was received, explain what happens next and roughly when, and offer something useful while they wait, such as a relevant guide or your phone number for urgent queries.

Why am I not getting enquiries from my form?

Start by testing that it works and that notifications arrive. Then look at traffic to the page, the number of fields, the button wording and how easy it is to use on a phone.
