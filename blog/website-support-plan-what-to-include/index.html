Website Support and Maintenance Plans: What Should Be Included, and What Happens Without One

What a website support and maintenance plan should cover, from updates and backups to response terms and reporting, and the risks of running a site without one.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A useful support plan names who does what: updates, backups, security checks, fixes and reporting, along with how requests are handled and what sits outside the plan.
  • Without a plan, problems tend to build quietly through missed updates, untested backups and broken forms, and they are often found only when something has already gone wrong.
  • Read the terms before you compare prices, because two plans with the same name can cover very different amounts of work and responsibility.

A website support and maintenance plan is an agreement that makes someone responsible for keeping your site updated, backed up, secure and working, and for dealing with problems when they appear. A good plan should state which tasks are included, how often they happen, how you ask for help, how quickly requests are acknowledged, what is excluded and how you will know the work was done. Without one, those jobs can fall between people, and small faults may stay hidden until they affect customers or enquiries.

This guide is for UK business owners and marketing managers reviewing a plan they have been offered, or deciding whether they need one. It concentrates on the contents of the agreement. For a wider view of routine tasks and how to compare costs, see our guide to website maintenance in the UK.

Maintenance and support are not quite the same

People often use the words together, but it helps to separate them when reading a proposal.

  • Maintenance is planned, preventive work, such as updates, backups, checks and tidying up.
  • Support is reactive help, such as fixing a fault, answering a question or making a small change you have asked for.

Some plans include plenty of one and little of the other. A plan that runs updates but offers no route for urgent help may leave you stranded when a page breaks. A plan that promises friendly support but says nothing about preventive work may simply wait for things to go wrong. Ask how each is handled, and do not assume that one implies the other.

What a website support plan should include

Platform, theme and extension updates

The plan should explain what gets updated and how. On a self-hosted platform such as WordPress, that may mean the core software, the theme and any plugins. On a hosted platform such as Shopify, the platform itself is looked after for you, but themes and apps can still need attention. Our guides to WordPress automatic updates and Shopify theme updates explain the differences.

Ask whether updates are tested first, whether a backup is taken beforehand and what happens if an update breaks something. Fixing the fallout from an update should ideally be covered, or at least clearly priced.

Backups you can actually restore

A backup is only useful if it can be restored. A good plan should state what is backed up, how often, where copies are kept, how long they are retained and whether restores are tested. It should also say who performs a restore and whether that is included. Our guide to building a website backup and recovery plan covers the questions to ask.

Security monitoring and response

No provider can honestly promise that a site will never be attacked. What a plan can do is describe the precautions taken and the response if something happens. Look for details such as access control, malware checks, removing unused accounts and extensions, and what is done if a compromise is suspected. If you run WordPress, our WordPress security guide gives useful background.

If an incident involves personal data, you may have legal duties of your own, including possibly reporting to the regulator. Make sure the plan says how quickly you would be told, and take professional advice on what applies to you.

Uptime and functional checks

A site can be online yet still broken. Monitoring whether it responds is useful, but someone should also check the things that make money or generate leads: contact forms, booking tools, basket and checkout, and confirmation emails. Ask which journeys are tested, and how often.

Performance and technical health

Sites tend to gather weight over time through larger images, extra scripts and old extensions. A plan may include periodic reviews of speed, broken links, crawl errors and similar issues. It does not have to replace a full technical SEO audit, but it should at least notice when something has clearly deteriorated.

Small content and design changes

Some plans include an allowance of time for small edits. If yours does, check what counts as a small change, whether unused time carries over and how larger pieces of work are quoted. Vague wording here can cause friction later on.

Hosting, domains and renewals

Clarify whether hosting, domain renewal and security certificates fall within the plan or are your responsibility. A lapsed domain can take down your site and your email together, so somebody must own the renewal dates. Our guide to business domain and email set-up explains how these pieces fit together.

Reporting

You should be able to see what was done without having to ask. A short, regular report might list updates applied, backups completed, issues found, fixes made and anything that needs a decision from you. It is less about volume than about evidence that the work is happening.

The terms that matter as much as the tasks

A task list tells you what is intended. The terms tell you what happens under pressure. Check these points before you sign:

  • Response and resolution: an acknowledgement is not a fix. See whether the plan separates the two, and whether urgent faults are treated differently from routine requests.
  • Support hours: find out when requests are monitored, and what happens at weekends or during holidays if that matters to your business.
  • How requests are made: a named route, such as email or a ticket system, avoids requests getting lost.
  • Exclusions: new features, redesigns, third-party faults and problems caused by your own edits are often treated separately, so look for them explicitly.
  • Extra work: ask how out-of-scope work is quoted, and whether it needs your approval before it starts.
  • Length and notice: check the minimum term, how to cancel and any price review arrangements.
  • Exit: ask what you receive when the plan ends, including access, files, backups and documentation.

Ownership deserves special attention. You should know who holds the accounts for your domain, hosting, platform and analytics, and how to get access if the relationship ends. Our guide to website ownership and handover goes through this. If the contract has data protection or liability wording you are unsure about, a solicitor can advise you.

What happens without a plan

Some sites run for a long while with little attention, and not every small brochure site needs an elaborate plan. But if nobody is clearly responsible, these are the risks that can build up.

Security exposure grows

Out-of-date software can contain known weaknesses. Old administrator accounts and forgotten plugins add further ways in. A compromised site might show spam, redirect visitors or leak data, and cleaning it up tends to be more disruptive than prevention.

Updates become harder

Updates skipped for a long time can stack up, and applying many at once carries a greater risk of conflict. What could have been a routine job can become a small project, particularly if the site also needs a newer server software version.

Faults go unnoticed

Forms are a good example. A contact form can stop sending messages without any visible sign on the page, so a business may simply believe enquiries have gone quiet. The same applies to payment methods, booking tools and tracking. Our guide to website contact forms includes things worth testing.

Recovery is slower

If there is no recent backup, or nobody knows where it is or how to restore it, a serious fault may mean lost content or orders and a long period of disruption. Knowledge can also leave with a person: if the one person who understood the site moves on, you may struggle to get back in.

Search visibility can suffer

Slow pages, broken links, server errors and accidental changes that block search engines can all harm how a site performs in search. A problem like this may go unseen for some time, which is why someone should keep an eye on search data and technical health.

Compliance can drift

Your privacy notice, cookie settings and accessibility may also need review as a site changes. New tracking tools or forms, for instance, may need your notices and consent settings updating. Our guide to website terms and privacy policies explains the basics, though it is not legal advice.

How much cover does your site need?

The right level depends on what a failure would cost you and on how complicated your site is.

Type of siteLikely priorities
Simple brochure siteUpdates, backups, form checks, renewals and a clear route for help
Lead generation siteThe above, plus closer checks on forms, tracking, landing pages and speed
Online shopCheckout and payment testing, stock and order integrations, faster responses around busy periods
Site with custom featuresDocumentation, testing on a staging copy and developers who understand the build

Testing changes away from the live site can be valuable for anything beyond a basic site. Our guide to website staging and testing explains how that works.

Questions to ask a provider

  • Exactly which tasks are included, and how often are they done?
  • Do you test updates before they go live, and who fixes any problems they cause?
  • Where are backups stored, how long are they kept and when was a restore last tested?
  • Which user journeys do you check, and how?
  • How do I report an urgent fault outside normal hours, if at all?
  • What is not covered, and how would that work be priced?
  • What would I receive if I decided to leave?

FAQs

Do I need a support plan if my site is on a hosted platform?

You may need less technical upkeep, because the platform handles its own software and hosting. Themes, apps, content, integrations and tracking can still need attention, so a lighter arrangement or a clear in-house routine may suit you.

Can I handle website maintenance myself?

Often, yes, if the site is simple and you have the time, access and confidence. What matters is that it is done reliably, with tested backups and a recovery plan, and that someone can step in if a fault is beyond you.

Does my web host already do this?

Possibly in part. Hosts may provide server upkeep and some backups, but that does not necessarily cover your site software, testing, forms or content changes. Check your hosting terms rather than assuming.

Will a support plan guarantee my site never goes down?

No plan can honestly promise that. A good one reduces risk, helps problems get noticed sooner and sets out how they will be handled. Treat any absolute promise with caution.

How do I compare plans fairly?

Set the task lists, terms and exclusions side by side before you look at price. A cheaper plan may be the right fit for a simple site, but only if it covers the failures that would genuinely hurt your business.

Next steps

List who currently owns each job on your site: updates, backups, security, form testing, renewals and urgent fixes. Any blank space or uncertain answer shows you where to start.
