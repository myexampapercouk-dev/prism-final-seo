Website Content Audit: How to Decide What to Keep, Improve or Remove

How to run a website content audit: building an inventory, judging each page, and deciding whether to keep, update, merge or remove it for visitors and SEO.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A content audit is a structured review of every page to decide whether it should be kept, improved, merged or removed.
  • Judge pages on usefulness, accuracy and purpose as well as traffic, since some low-traffic pages still matter.
  • Merging and removing pages needs care with redirects so visitors and search engines are not sent to dead ends.

Websites accumulate content. Blog posts written years ago, service pages for things the business no longer offers, three slightly different pages about the same topic, product pages for discontinued lines. Each one seemed sensible when it was published, but over time the site becomes harder to navigate, harder to maintain and less clear about what the business actually does.

A content audit is a structured way to step back and review everything. This guide explains what a content audit involves, how to gather the information you need, how to judge each page and what to do with the results.

What is a content audit?

A content audit is a page-by-page review of the content on a website. For each page, you record some basic facts, judge its quality and usefulness, and decide on an action: keep it as it is, improve it, merge it with something else or remove it.

It is different from a technical SEO audit, which looks at how a site is built and crawled, such as indexing, redirects and site speed. The two overlap and are often useful together, but a content audit focuses on the words, images and purpose of each page.

When a content audit is worth doing

  • Before a redesign or migration, so you do not move outdated or unwanted pages to a new site.
  • When the business has changed, such as new services, a new focus or a rebrand.
  • When organic traffic has plateaued or fallen and you want to understand why.
  • When the blog has grown large and older posts have not been looked at in a long time.
  • Before planning new content, so you build on what exists rather than duplicating it.

There is no fixed rule for how often to audit. Smaller sites may manage with a light review once a year, while larger or faster-changing sites may benefit from rolling reviews.

Step 1: Build a content inventory

Start with a list of every page you want to review. A spreadsheet works well. Ways to gather the list include:

  • exporting pages and posts from your content management system, such as WordPress or Shopify,
  • using your XML sitemap as a starting list,
  • crawling the site with a site audit tool to catch pages not in the sitemap, and
  • checking Google Search Console for pages Google knows about.

Comparing these sources is useful in itself. Pages that appear in one list but not another can reveal orphaned content, old pages still indexed or pages missing from your sitemap.

For an online store with many products, you may choose to audit products at category or template level rather than one by one, and focus individual review on your most important or problematic pages.

Use a consistent time period, such as the last twelve months, so seasonal swings do not distort the picture. If your analytics are not set up properly, our GA4 setup guide is a good place to start.

Step 3: Judge each page

Numbers are only part of the picture. A page with little traffic might still be essential, such as a delivery information page or a privacy policy, and a page with steady traffic might be attracting the wrong audience. For each page, ask:

  • Purpose: does this page have a clear job, and does it still match what the business offers?
  • Accuracy: are prices, services, contact details, dates and policies still correct?
  • Usefulness: does it genuinely answer the question a visitor would arrive with?
  • Overlap: is there another page covering the same topic for the same audience?
  • Quality: is it well written, well structured and in keeping with your tone of voice?
  • Links: does it link sensibly to related pages, and do other pages link to it?

A simple scoring system can help keep judgements consistent, especially if several people are involved. Even a rough rating of strong, adequate or weak for each question makes patterns easier to see.

Step 4: Decide on an action

Every page should end up with one clear action. The common options are:

Keep

The page is accurate, useful and performing its role. No change is needed beyond routine checks.

Update or improve

The page has value but needs work: refreshing out-of-date information, expanding thin sections, improving headings, adding internal links or rewriting unclear copy. Pages with search impressions but few clicks are often good candidates, as improving titles and content may help them perform better. Our guide to website copywriting may help with rewrites.

Merge

Two or more pages cover the same topic and compete with each other. Combine the best parts into one stronger page and redirect the others to it. This is common on blogs where similar posts were written at different times.

Remove

The page is out of date, irrelevant or of very low quality, and is not worth improving. Remove it, and decide whether to redirect it to a relevant alternative or let it return a 404 or 410 status. Our guide to 404 page design explains when each response makes sense.

Keep but exclude from search

Some pages are useful to visitors but not to searchers, such as certain thank-you pages or account pages. These can stay on the site while being kept out of search results with appropriate settings.

Handling removals and merges carefully

Removing or merging pages is where audits can go wrong. Before deleting anything:

  • check whether the page has links from other websites or meaningful traffic,
  • choose the most relevant destination for any redirect, rather than defaulting to the homepage,
  • update internal links across the site so they point straight to the new destination, and
  • keep a record of every change so you can review the effect later.

Many of the same principles apply as in a site migration; our SEO migration checklist covers redirect mapping in more detail.

Finding gaps as well as problems

A content audit is also a chance to see what is missing. As you review, note questions visitors ask that no page answers, services or products without a proper page, and topics where competitors offer more useful content. Search Console queries can reveal topics people associate with your site that you have not covered well.

These gaps feed naturally into a content strategy, and keyword research can help prioritise them.

Prioritising the work

An audit can produce a long list of actions. To keep it manageable, prioritise by likely impact and effort:

  1. fix inaccurate information first, especially prices, policies and contact details,
  2. improve important commercial pages such as services, categories and top products,
  3. merge competing pages on topics that matter to the business,
  4. refresh valuable older posts, and
  5. remove clearly redundant content.

Work through the list in batches rather than trying to change everything at once. It is easier to manage and to see which changes made a difference.

Content audits and AI search

AI-generated answers in search, such as Google's AI Overviews, draw on pages that answer questions clearly. Clear structure, accurate facts and one strong page per topic rather than several weak ones can make content easier for both people and these systems to understand. Our guide to AI Overviews and SEO explores this further.

Keeping content healthy after the audit

An audit is most useful when it leads to better habits. Simple steps include:

  • adding a "last reviewed" date to your inventory and revisiting important pages periodically,
  • checking whether a similar page already exists before publishing something new,
  • updating pages when services, prices or policies change, and
  • including content checks in routine website maintenance.

Common mistakes

  • Deleting pages based on traffic alone without checking links or purpose.
  • Redirecting removed pages to the homepage by default.
  • Forgetting to update internal links after merges.
  • Producing a spreadsheet of findings that is never acted on.
  • Trying to fix everything at once with no record of what changed.

FAQs

What is the difference between a content audit and an SEO audit?

A content audit reviews the usefulness and accuracy of each page. A technical SEO audit looks at how the site is built, crawled and indexed. They complement each other.

Should I delete old blog posts?

Not automatically. Old posts with useful information can often be updated. Remove only those that are outdated, irrelevant or very weak, and check for links and traffic first.

How long does a content audit take?

It depends on the size of the site and the depth of review. A small site can be reviewed relatively quickly, while large sites may need sampling or a phased approach.

What tools do I need?

A spreadsheet, Google Search Console, GA4 and a site crawler will cover most needs.

Can merging pages improve rankings?

Combining overlapping pages into one stronger page can help search engines understand which page to show, though results are never guaranteed.
