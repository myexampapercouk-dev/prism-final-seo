Website Design for Interior Designers in the UK: Portfolios, Services and Enquiries

Website design guide for UK interior designers: portfolio and project pages, services and process, enquiries, image rights, local search and platform choice.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • An interior design website is largely judged on its portfolio, so project pages need strong images and enough context to explain the work.
  • Clearly described services, a plain explanation of how you work and a well-designed enquiry form help the right clients get in touch.
  • Image permissions, client privacy and honest descriptions of credentials and projects need care on every page.

Interior design is a visual, personal and often high-value service. Prospective clients want to see your taste, understand how you work and feel confident you can handle their home or commercial space. For most interior designers, the website is where that impression is formed long before a first meeting, and a weak site can undersell excellent work.

This guide is for UK interior designers, from independent designers working on residential projects to studios handling commercial, hospitality or rental property work. It covers the portfolio, project pages, services, your process, enquiries, images and privacy, local search, selling products if you do, and choosing a platform. If your work is closer to architecture, our architect marketing guide covers that profession.

Start with who you design for

Interior designers serve very different clients. A designer focusing on full renovations of period homes needs a different site from one offering e-design consultations, styling for property developers or fit-outs for offices and restaurants. Before thinking about layout, be clear about:

  • The type of clients and projects you want more of.
  • Whether you work locally, regionally or across the UK.
  • The services you offer, from a single room to full project management.
  • What makes your approach distinct.

These answers shape everything from the home page message to which projects you feature first.

The portfolio is the heart of the site

Most visitors will spend more time in your portfolio than anywhere else. It should showcase your best work and make browsing a pleasure on any device.

Curate rather than include everything

A smaller selection of strong, well-photographed projects is usually more convincing than a long list of mixed quality. Feature the work that best represents the projects you want to attract.

Organise for your visitors

If you work across different sectors or styles, consider letting visitors filter projects, for example by residential and commercial, or by type of room. Keep it simple; too many categories can make a portfolio harder to browse.

Use professional photography

Interiors are notoriously hard to photograph well. Professional photography of finished projects is often worth the investment, as the images are used across your website, social media and any press opportunities. Make sure you have the right to use them; more on that below.

Project pages that tell the story

A gallery of beautiful rooms shows taste, but a project page with context shows how you think. For each featured project, consider including:

  • The brief: what the client wanted and any constraints, such as budget, period features or how the space needed to function.
  • Your approach: the key design decisions and why you made them.
  • The scope: which rooms, and whether you handled design only or full project management.
  • Before and after images, if you have them and permission to use them.
  • A client comment, if genuine and given with permission.

Keep any outcomes you describe factual. It is fine to explain what the space now does differently; avoid claims you cannot support. Written well, project pages can also help with search, as they naturally include the language clients use when looking for a designer.

Explaining your services and process

Many people have never hired an interior designer and are unsure what is involved. Clear service and process pages reduce that uncertainty and filter out poor-fit enquiries.

Services

Describe the ways clients can work with you. Depending on your business, this might include a design consultation, a room design package, full home design, project management, sourcing and procurement, styling, or remote e-design. For each, explain who it suits and what is involved. Only describe inclusions you actually provide. Our service page design guide explains how to structure these pages.

Process

A simple outline of how a project typically runs, from first conversation to finished space, helps clients picture working with you. Describe your actual process rather than an idealised one, and be honest that timelines depend on the project, suppliers and trades involved.

Fees

Interior designers charge in different ways, including fixed fees, hourly rates and fees based on project scope. Some show starting points on their site, while others prefer to discuss fees after understanding the project. Either can work. If you do publish fees, keep them accurate and up to date. Our pricing page design guide covers the options.

Turning interest into enquiries

An enquiry form for an interior designer can do a lot of useful work if it asks the right questions. Consider asking about:

  • The type of property and location.
  • Which rooms or spaces are involved.
  • The service the person is interested in.
  • Rough timing.
  • An indicative budget range, if you find this helps.

Balance useful detail with ease of completion; a very long form can put people off. Make the contact route visible across the site, not just on the contact page, and say how quickly people can expect a reply only if you can keep to it. Our contact forms guide explains how to design forms that people complete.

If you offer initial consultations, an online booking tool can save back-and-forth emails. Our online booking systems guide covers the options.

Images, permissions and client privacy

Interior design websites rely heavily on images, which raises a few practical points.

  • Photographers' rights. If a photographer took the images, check your agreement covers using them on your website and elsewhere. Copyright often stays with the photographer unless agreed otherwise.
  • Client permission. Get clients' agreement before publishing photographs of their homes. Some will be happy to be featured; others will prefer anonymity.
  • Location and security. Avoid revealing addresses or details that could identify a private home, and think about whether images show valuables or security features.
  • Supplier and brand images. Do not use other companies' images without permission.

Large images also slow sites down if they are not optimised. Interior design sites are especially vulnerable to this. Our image optimisation guide explains how to keep image quality high without hurting page speed.

Credentials and claims

Interior design is not a protected title in the UK in the same way as architect, which is regulated by the Architects Registration Board. If you hold qualifications or memberships of professional bodies, list them accurately. Do not imply qualifications, memberships, awards or press coverage you do not have, and be careful about describing services that may need other professionals, such as structural changes or building regulations matters, which may require an architect, structural engineer or approved contractor.

Local search and getting found

Many interior designers serve a particular area, and clients often search with a location in mind. A well-maintained Google Business Profile, a site that clearly states the areas you serve, and project pages that mention general locations where appropriate can all help. Our local SEO guide and Google Business Profile guide explain the basics.

A journal or blog with genuinely useful content, such as how to plan a kitchen layout or how to choose lighting for a period home, can also support visibility and show expertise. Our content strategy guide covers how to plan it.

Selling products or offering e-design

Some interior designers also sell homeware, offer curated product edits or provide remote e-design packages paid for online. If you do, your site needs ecommerce or payment features, along with clear terms, delivery and returns information. Shopify suits designers who want a substantial shop, while Squarespace and Wix offer simpler selling tools. Our guide to Shopify for furniture and homeware brands covers product-led businesses in more depth.

Choosing a platform

Squarespace and Wix are popular with designers who want a visually polished site they can update themselves. WordPress offers more flexibility for larger studios, extensive journals or custom features. A custom design on any of these can give a more distinctive result than a lightly edited template. Our comparisons of Squarespace vs WordPress and Wix vs WordPress, and our article on website builders vs custom websites, explain the trade-offs.

Whichever platform you choose, pay attention to typography, colour and white space, as visitors will read your website as an example of your design sense. Our colour and typography guide has more.

FAQs

What should an interior designer's website include?

A strong portfolio with project pages, clear service descriptions, an explanation of your process, an about page, a well-designed enquiry form and a privacy notice.

Should interior designers show their fees online?

Some show starting points and some discuss fees after learning about the project. Either approach can work if your information is accurate and consistent.

Do I need permission to show clients' homes on my website?

It is good practice to get clients' agreement first and avoid revealing addresses or identifying details. Also check you have the photographer's permission to use images.

Which platform is best for an interior design website?

Squarespace and Wix suit designers who want to manage a visual site themselves. WordPress suits larger studios or those needing more custom features.

How can interior designers get found locally?

A complete Google Business Profile, clear service area information, well-written project pages and genuinely useful content can all support local visibility.
