Ecommerce Discount Code Strategy: Using Offers Without Eroding Margin

A practical discount code strategy for UK online shops: when to offer codes, which types to use, protecting margin and keeping pricing claims within UK rules.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Give every discount a clear purpose, such as a first order, a clearance or a loyalty reward.
  • Limit codes by audience, products, dates and usage so they cannot leak and run indefinitely.
  • Keep price and discount claims accurate, and check current UK pricing guidance before big sales.

A discount code strategy is a plan for when, why and how your online shop offers money off. Discounts can bring in first-time customers, clear old stock and reward loyal buyers. Used without a plan, they can train shoppers to wait for a sale, appear on voucher websites you never chose, and quietly erode the margin your business depends on.

This guide covers the types of discount available on major ecommerce platforms, how to decide when to use them, how to stop codes leaking and what to keep in mind about UK pricing rules.

Start with the purpose of each discount

Every discount should answer a simple question: what behaviour are we trying to encourage? Common purposes include:

  • Acquiring new customers who are unsure about buying from a shop they do not know.
  • Increasing order value by encouraging shoppers to add another item.
  • Clearing stock that is seasonal, discontinued or overstocked.
  • Rewarding loyalty from repeat customers or subscribers.
  • Recovering lapsed customers who have not bought for a while.
  • Supporting a campaign such as a seasonal sale or product launch.

When the purpose is clear, choosing the type, audience and limits becomes much easier. When it is not, discounts tend to become a reflex whenever sales feel slow.

Types of discount to consider

Shopify, WooCommerce and BigCommerce support similar discount types, sometimes through extensions or apps.

Percentage off

A percentage off the basket or specific products. Simple to understand, but the cost to you rises with order value, so check what it means on your most expensive items.

Fixed amount off

A set amount off, often with a minimum spend. This gives you more predictable costs and works well for encouraging larger orders.

Free delivery

Many shoppers respond strongly to free delivery. It can be offered with a minimum spend or as a time-limited promotion. Make sure the delivery cost you absorb is understood. Our delivery and returns pages guide covers communicating delivery clearly.

Buy X get Y and bundles

Offers such as buy one get one half price, or a discount for buying a set, can increase order value and move slower products alongside popular ones. Our guide to product variants and bundles explains how to set these up cleanly.

Gift with purchase

A free sample or small item with orders over a certain value can feel generous without reducing the visible price of your products.

Automatic discounts

Discounts can apply automatically without a code. This avoids frustration when shoppers forget or mistype a code, and keeps sale prices visible on product pages.

Choosing the right audience

A discount that goes to everyone costs the most, because it includes people who would have paid full price. Targeting reduces that cost.

  • New subscribers. A welcome offer for joining your email list. Our ecommerce email marketing guide covers welcome flows.
  • Customer segments. Platforms let you limit codes to specific customer groups, such as repeat buyers or wholesale accounts.
  • Specific products or collections. Apply offers only to stock you want to move.
  • Partners and influencers. Unique codes per partner help you understand which relationships produce orders. Make sure partners disclose paid promotions in line with Advertising Standards Authority guidance.

Protecting your margin

Before launching an offer, work out what it costs per order once product cost, delivery, payment processing and returns are considered. A discount that looks small on the price tag can remove much of your profit on a low-margin item.

Practical safeguards include:

  • Setting minimum spends on fixed amount and free delivery offers.
  • Excluding low-margin products or already reduced items.
  • Deciding whether discounts can be combined, and setting your platform accordingly.
  • Limiting usage to once per customer where appropriate.
  • Setting start and end dates so offers do not run indefinitely.

Stopping codes from leaking

Public codes often end up on voucher and coupon websites. Shoppers then leave your site mid-checkout to search for a code, and some who would have paid full price use one instead.

  • Use unique codes for email campaigns and customer service gestures. Most platforms can generate batches of single-use codes.
  • Set usage limits so a leaked code stops working after a set number of uses.
  • Expire codes promptly once a campaign ends.
  • Consider the discount field at checkout. A prominent empty box can prompt shoppers to go looking. Some shops make it less prominent, while others use automatic discounts instead.

Avoiding discount dependency

If customers see a sale every few weeks, many will simply wait. Over time your full price stops meaning anything. Signs of dependency include sales dipping between promotions and a rising share of orders using codes.

To avoid this, plan promotions in a calendar with clear gaps, focus on value beyond price such as product quality, service and content, and use non-price incentives like early access, free samples or loyalty perks. Our guide to seasonal sales preparation explains how to plan bigger events carefully.

UK rules on pricing and promotions

This is general information rather than legal advice. If you are unsure, take advice from a qualified professional.

  • Reference prices. If you show a was price, it should be a genuine price you charged. The Competition and Markets Authority and Trading Standards have published guidance on pricing practices, and rules under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 have strengthened enforcement.
  • Urgency claims. Countdown timers and limited-time claims should be true. If an offer is said to end on a date, it should end.
  • Clear terms. State any minimum spend, exclusions and expiry dates clearly.
  • Advertising. Promotional claims in ads and emails fall under the CAP Code, which the Advertising Standards Authority enforces.
  • Marketing consent. Sending discount codes by email or text is marketing, so UK GDPR and PECR apply. See ICO guidance.

Displaying discounts on your site

How you show an offer affects whether it helps or confuses. Keep sale prices consistent between product pages, collection pages and the basket. Make it obvious when a discount has been applied. If a code fails, show a clear message explaining why, such as minimum spend not met. Our guide to category page design covers showing sale items in listings.

Building a promotions calendar

A simple promotions calendar stops discounts from becoming reactive. List the moments that matter to your shop across the year, such as seasonal events, product launches, end-of-season clearances and any dates that are meaningful to your customers. Then decide which moments genuinely need a price offer and which could be supported with new content, early access or a limited product instead.

Leave clear gaps between promotions so your full price keeps its meaning. Note who on your team needs to prepare stock, update the website, write emails and check that codes expire on time. Writing this down in advance makes it much easier to brief anyone helping with the shop and to review afterwards what worked.

Testing before launch

Before any offer goes live, test it as a customer would. Add qualifying and non-qualifying products to the basket, try the code on mobile, check it combines or refuses to combine as intended, and confirm the confirmation email shows the correct totals. A few minutes of testing can prevent a code that fails at checkout or, worse, one that applies far more widely than planned.

Measuring whether discounts work

Look beyond the number of orders placed with a code. Useful questions include:

  • Did the offer bring in new customers, or mainly existing ones?
  • Did those new customers buy again at full price?
  • What happened to average order value and margin during the offer?
  • Did sales drop before or after the promotion as shoppers waited or stocked up?

Your platform's reports and Google Analytics can answer most of these if codes are named consistently.

FAQs

Should I offer a discount for joining my email list?

A welcome offer can encourage sign-ups and first orders. Consider whether a smaller incentive, free delivery or a non-price benefit would work for your audience before defaulting to a large percentage.

Are automatic discounts better than codes?

Automatic discounts reduce friction and stop shoppers hunting for codes, which suits public sales. Codes are better for targeting specific audiences or tracking partners.

How do I stop my codes appearing on voucher sites?

Use unique single-use codes for targeted offers, set usage limits and expiry dates, and avoid long-running public codes.

Can I show a was price on sale items?

You can, but the reference price should be genuine and not misleading. Check current CMA and Trading Standards guidance, and take advice if you are unsure.

Can discounts be combined?

Most platforms let you choose whether discounts stack. Decide deliberately, because stacking can cut much deeper into margin than any single offer.
