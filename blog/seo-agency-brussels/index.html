Why SEO in Brussels is different

Brussels is officially bilingual, and Belgium as a whole has three official languages: Dutch, French and German. On top of that, the city hosts European institutions, international associations, consultancies and many multinational offices, which makes English common in business. A single search for an accountant, a consultant or a dental clinic can happen in any of three languages, and each version of the query has its own competitors and its own results.

That has practical consequences. Keyword research has to be done separately for each language, because the terms people use are not direct translations of each other. Each language version needs its own URL, title, description and headings. And search engines need clear signals about which version to show to whom, so that a French speaker does not land on a Dutch page.

Brussels also has a very local search pattern. People often search for services near a particular commune, such as Ixelles, Etterbeek, Uccle or Schaerbeek, rather than for Brussels as a whole. Local pages and a well-maintained Google Business Profile can make a real difference for businesses that serve customers in person.

Getting multilingual SEO right

The most common problem we see on Belgian websites is a weak language setup. Sometimes only one language is indexed. Sometimes French and Dutch pages share the same title. Sometimes a translation plugin generates pages that search engines treat as low quality. Each of these limits how well the site can rank.

A sound setup gives each language its own section, such as /fr/, /nl/ and /en/, with matching pages linked to each other through language tags. Navigation, forms, cookie banners and error messages should all appear in the visitor's language. Our guide to multilingual website design covers the build side, and if your site needs a rebuild, our post on web design for Brussels businesses explains how we approach it.

Content quality matters as much as structure. A page written well in French and then translated literally into Dutch rarely performs as well in Dutch. We provide briefs and keyword targets for each language, and your team or translator writes or adapts the copy so it reads naturally.

Keyword research and content

Good keyword research shows the real demand behind each service: what people search for, how they phrase it and which questions they ask before buying. For Brussels businesses, we look at each language separately and then decide which pages each language needs. Not every page has to exist in all three languages if demand in one of them is very low.

Our keyword research guide explains the method. For professional services and B2B firms, which are common in Brussels, content that answers specific questions clearly tends to perform best, both in classic search results and in AI search answers.

Technical and local SEO

Technical SEO makes sure search engines can crawl, understand and index your pages. On multilingual sites, that includes checking language tags, canonical tags, redirects and sitemaps for each language. Our technical SEO audit checklist sets out what we review.

For businesses that serve customers in person, local SEO is essential. That means a complete Google Business Profile with the right categories, photos and opening hours, consistent contact details across the web, and local pages where they genuinely help. Our guide to Google Business Profile covers the setup, and the principles apply in Belgium too.

Compliance basics for Belgian websites

These notes are general information only. Please follow official guidance from the relevant authorities and take advice from your own legal adviser.

  • GDPR. Contact forms, newsletters and analytics all collect personal data. You need a clear privacy notice in each language, a lawful basis and sensible retention. Belgium's data protection authority publishes guidance.
  • Cookie consent. Under the ePrivacy rules as applied in Belgian law, non-essential cookies such as analytics and advertising tags generally need prior consent, and rejecting should be as easy as accepting. Our post on cookie banner design covers the practical side.
  • Accessibility. The European Accessibility Act has applied since June 2025 to many consumer products and services, including e-commerce. Microenterprises providing services are generally exempt, but accessible pages help every visitor. The expected standard is EN 301 549, which points to WCAG at level AA. See our overview of the European Accessibility Act for websites.
