Chiropractor and Osteopath Website Design: Bookings, Trust and Careful Claims

Website design for UK chiropractors and osteopaths: treatment pages, online booking, first appointment guides, registration details and careful health claims.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Chiropractic and osteopathy websites need to explain treatment clearly while staying careful about health claims, which are subject to advertising rules and professional standards.
  • A clear guide to the first appointment, covering what happens, how long it takes and what it costs, can help nervous new patients take the step of booking.
  • Registration with the relevant statutory regulator is a meaningful trust signal, so it is worth showing accurately and linking to the official register where appropriate.

Someone looking for a chiropractor or osteopath is often in discomfort, short on patience and unsure what either profession actually does. They want to know whether you can help, what will happen at the first appointment, and how soon they can be seen. A practice website that answers those questions clearly, without overpromising, can make booking feel like an easy next step.

This guide covers website design for UK chiropractic and osteopathic practices, from single-practitioner clinics to multi-room practices. It focuses on the website itself. Physiotherapy practices may find our guide to physiotherapy clinic website design more relevant, and counsellors or talking therapists may prefer our guide to therapist website design. For wider promotion of health services, see our guide to healthcare marketing.

What the website needs to do

For most practices, the main jobs of the site are to:

  • Explain what chiropractic or osteopathy involves, in plain language.
  • Describe the conditions and situations you commonly see, carefully.
  • Set out what happens at the first appointment and what it costs.
  • Let people book online or request an appointment easily.
  • Introduce practitioners and show their registration.
  • Make location, parking, opening hours and accessibility details easy to find.

Getting these right usually matters far more than visual flourishes.

Careful claims: the most important design consideration

Health claims are the area where chiropractic and osteopathy websites most often run into trouble. Advertising on your own website is covered by the CAP Code, which the Advertising Standards Authority enforces. Claims that a treatment can help with a particular condition generally need to be backed by suitable evidence, and the ASA has published guidance relating to both professions over the years. Professional standards set by the General Chiropractic Council and the General Osteopathic Council also expect registrants to advertise in a way that is accurate and not misleading.

The details can change, and interpretation depends on exact wording, so practices should check the current guidance from the ASA, CAP and their own regulator and professional body, and take advice if unsure. As general principles for website content:

  • Avoid words such as cure, guarantee, fix or permanent relief.
  • Be cautious about listing specific medical conditions as things you treat, unless you are confident the claim is supportable under current guidance.
  • Describe what you do, such as assessment, hands-on treatment, exercise advice, rather than promising outcomes.
  • Do not discourage anyone from seeking medical advice, and say when people should see a GP or seek urgent care instead.
  • Be careful with testimonials, which are treated as claims by the practice if they imply treatment results.

Building this into the design from the start, for example by planning service pages around the patient journey rather than long condition lists, is much easier than rewriting a site after a complaint.

Explaining the profession

Many visitors are not sure of the difference between a chiropractor, an osteopath and a physiotherapist. A short, neutral page explaining your profession, what training practitioners have and how treatment generally works can help. Keep it factual and avoid comparing yourself unfavourably with other professions. If your practice offers more than one discipline, explain how patients can decide which to book, or invite them to call for advice.

Treatment and service pages

Rather than building pages around conditions, many practices find it clearer to organise around services and patient groups. Examples might include:

  • Initial consultation and assessment.
  • Follow-up treatment sessions.
  • Sports massage or soft tissue work, if offered.
  • Appointments for specific groups, such as pregnancy or older adults, if you genuinely offer tailored care and the wording is carefully checked.
  • Workplace or ergonomic advice, if relevant.

For each, explain who it is for, what happens, how long it takes and the price or where to find it. Our guide to service page design covers structure and layout principles that apply here.

The first appointment

A first appointment page is one of the most useful things a practice can publish. People who are in discomfort or nervous about hands-on treatment want to know exactly what to expect. You might cover:

  • How long the first appointment usually lasts.
  • The case history and assessment process.
  • Whether treatment usually starts in the first session.
  • What to wear, and how modesty and comfort are handled.
  • Whether someone can bring a chaperone.
  • What happens if treatment is not considered suitable.
  • The cost and accepted payment methods.

Explaining consent, and that patients can ask questions or stop at any point, can be reassuring. A calm, plain tone suits this page well.

Online booking

Many patients want to book outside clinic hours, often when discomfort is on their mind. Practice management systems commonly include an online booking feature that can be embedded on a website or linked from it. If yours does, make it prominent and test it thoroughly on a phone. Let patients choose the right appointment type, such as a new patient assessment rather than a follow-up, so diaries are not disrupted. Our guide to online booking systems covers the general options.

Keep a phone number visible too, as some patients prefer to talk first, and a click-to-call button on mobile can be helpful.

Practitioner profiles and registration

In the UK, the titles chiropractor and osteopath are protected by law, and practitioners must be registered with the General Chiropractic Council or the General Osteopathic Council respectively. Showing this accurately is a meaningful trust signal. For each practitioner, a profile might include:

  • A photo and short introduction.
  • Their registration with the relevant regulator, stated accurately.
  • Their qualifications and any areas of particular interest, phrased carefully.
  • The days they work.

Linking to the regulator's public register lets patients check for themselves. Membership of professional associations can also be mentioned where genuine. Our guide to website trust signals covers other ways to build confidence, such as real photos of the clinic and clear contact details.

Prices, insurance and policies

Patients often want to know costs upfront. A clear fees page listing the price of initial and follow-up appointments, along with your cancellation policy, can reduce phone calls. If patients may be able to claim through private health insurance, explain that cover varies and that they should check with their insurer before booking. Avoid suggesting insurers will definitely pay unless you have confirmed arrangements.

Reviews and testimonials

Reviews can help, but in health they need care. Testimonials that describe recovery from a specific condition may be treated as a claim made by the practice, so the same rules apply as for your own wording. General comments about the experience, such as friendliness, clarity of explanation or ease of booking, are less likely to raise concerns. Only use genuine reviews, with permission, and do not edit them in ways that change their meaning.

Privacy and health data

Booking and intake forms often collect health information, which is special category data under UK GDPR. Collect only what you genuinely need, explain how it will be used and stored, and make sure your privacy notice covers it. If your practice management system handles intake forms, check how it stores data. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for health organisations.

Accessibility and practical details

Patients may have restricted movement, so physical access information matters. State whether there is step-free access, parking nearby and a ground floor treatment room. Online accessibility matters too: clear text, good contrast and forms that work with assistive technology. Our guide to website accessibility covers the basics.

Choosing a platform

Most practice websites are service sites with a booking feature, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all be suitable. The choice often depends on how the site needs to connect with your practice management system and who will keep it updated. Our guide to website builders versus custom websites covers the trade-offs, and our guide to website design costs explains what affects budgets.

FAQs

Can a chiropractor or osteopath list conditions they treat on their website?

Claims about treating specific conditions generally need to be supported by suitable evidence under advertising rules. Check current guidance from the ASA, CAP and your regulator before listing conditions, and take advice if unsure.

Should practitioners show their registration on the website?

Yes, stating registration with the General Chiropractic Council or General Osteopathic Council accurately, and linking to the public register, helps patients check credentials.

Is online booking worth it for a small practice?

For many practices it is, as patients often want to book outside hours. Check whether your practice management system offers booking that can link to your website.

Can I use patient testimonials?

Testimonials can be treated as claims by the practice, so those describing treatment results need the same care as your own wording. Comments about the overall experience are usually safer.

What should a first appointment page include?

How long it lasts, what the assessment involves, whether treatment starts on the day, what to wear, chaperone options and the cost.
