Window and Door Installer Website Design: Products, Surveys and Compliance

Website design for UK window and door installers: product pages for uPVC, aluminium and timber, survey booking, compliance certificates and quote forms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Window and door sites sit between a product catalogue and a trade website, so structure pages around both product types and the installation service.
  • Replacement windows in England and Wales generally need building regulations compliance, often shown through a competent person scheme, so explain this accurately and only claim memberships you hold.
  • Quote forms work best when they ask for the number and type of openings rather than exact measurements the customer cannot reliably take.

Replacing windows or a front door is a bigger decision than it looks from the outside. It is usually a substantial spend, the result is visible to the whole street, and customers have to choose between materials, styles, colours, glazing options and hardware before they even get to choosing an installer. A window and door installer's website therefore has a slightly unusual job: part product guide, part trade website, and part reassurance that the firm will handle surveys, fitting and paperwork properly.

This guide covers the website itself. For broader promotion, such as reviews, local listings and advertising, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK. If you also build conservatories or extensions, our guide to construction company website design covers larger building projects, which this article does not.

Organise around products and the service

Visitors arrive with very different starting points. Some know they want a composite front door. Some just know their windows are draughty. Some are replacing a single broken unit. A structure that works well for many installers is:

  • Windows, with pages for the styles you fit, such as casement, tilt and turn, sash and bay.
  • Doors, with pages for front doors, back doors, French doors, bifold doors and sliding patio doors, as relevant.
  • Materials, explaining uPVC, aluminium and timber in plain terms, including where each tends to suit.
  • Repairs and replacements, if you replace misted glass units, handles, locks or hinges.
  • How it works, covering survey, quote, manufacture, fitting and paperwork.

Product pages should help someone choose, not just list options. A bifold door page, for example, might explain configurations, threshold options, how they open and what to consider about space inside and outside. Write in your own words rather than reusing manufacturer brochure copy, which is often duplicated across many sites and may be under copyright.

Showing colours, finishes and hardware

Customers care a great deal about how their windows and doors will look. Clear photographs of finished installations, and of colour and finish options you offer, help more than lengthy descriptions. If you supply products from a particular manufacturer or system, check what imagery and naming you are permitted to use before featuring it.

Some installers add a simple door style or colour selector to their site. This can be useful, but it is not essential, and a poorly built one can be slow and frustrating on a phone. Good photography organised by style and colour is often enough, and it is much easier to maintain. If you want a visual tool, make sure it is clear that it is an illustration and that actual colours should be checked against physical samples.

Explaining compliance accurately

This is where a window and door installer's website needs real care. In England and Wales, replacement windows and external doors are generally covered by building regulations, which set requirements such as thermal performance and, in some cases, means of escape and safety glazing. Installers commonly show compliance by being registered with a competent person scheme, which lets them self-certify the work and arrange for a compliance certificate to be issued. FENSA and CERTASS are examples of such schemes. Otherwise, the work may need to go through local authority building control.

On your website you can explain, in general terms, that you will handle compliance and that customers should receive certification, which they may need when selling the property. Keep the following in mind:

  • Only name schemes you are actually registered with, and follow each scheme's rules for displaying logos.
  • Do not summarise the regulations in detail, since requirements change. Link to or mention official guidance instead.
  • Rules differ in Scotland and Northern Ireland, so if you work there, check what applies and word your pages accordingly.
  • Listed buildings and conservation areas can have additional restrictions. A short page saying you will advise customers to check with their local planning authority is safer than promising anything specific.

Energy performance is another area to handle carefully. You can describe the general benefits of modern double or triple glazing, but avoid promising specific savings on energy bills, which depend on the property, the existing windows and how the home is heated. If you quote energy ratings or U-values, make sure they come from the actual products you fit.

Quote forms that customers can complete

Most homeowners cannot measure windows accurately, and asking them to do so produces unreliable numbers. A better approach is to ask for information they can easily give:

  • Windows, doors or both.
  • Rough number of openings, for example "about eight windows and a front door".
  • Preferred material, or "not sure".
  • Type of property, such as terraced, semi-detached or flat.
  • Postcode.
  • Optional photos of the front and back of the property.

Then explain that a survey will confirm exact measurements before anything is ordered. This is honest, and it reduces the barrier to enquiry. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design in more detail. If you offer survey appointments at set times, an online request system may suit; see online booking systems.

A short note about flats is worth adding if you take that work. Leaseholders may need permission from their freeholder or managing agent before replacing windows, and saying so early can prevent problems later.

Repairs and smaller jobs

Many installers also handle repairs: misted double-glazed units, broken handles, failing locks, dropped doors and worn hinges. These smaller jobs can be a useful source of work and sometimes lead to larger replacements later. If you offer them, give repairs their own clear section rather than burying them on a services list. People searching for a misted window replacement are not looking for a full house quote, and they will leave a site that only talks about complete installations.

If you offer emergency boarding or glazing after a break-in, say so clearly with hours and areas, and say equally clearly if you do not.

Explaining the timeline

Unlike many trades, window and door installation usually involves a gap between survey and fitting while products are made to measure. Customers who do not expect this can feel frustrated. A simple page explaining the stages, and why lead times vary, manages expectations without committing to specific dates you may not be able to hit.

It is also helpful to describe what fitting day looks like in general terms: how you protect the home, how old frames are removed, how waste is taken away, and how you leave things. Only describe what you actually do.

Guarantees and paperwork

Customers want to know what happens if something goes wrong. If you offer a guarantee on your installation, say what it covers and for how long, and keep it distinct from manufacturer guarantees on frames, glass units or hardware. If you are part of an insurance-backed guarantee arrangement, describe it accurately and only if you hold it.

Deposits are worth addressing too. If you take deposits for made-to-measure products, explain how deposits work in general terms. Consumer cancellation rights for contracts agreed in the home or at a distance can be complex, particularly for bespoke goods, so take advice on your terms rather than summarising the law on your website.

Trust signals for a visible purchase

Because the finished work is on show, photos of real installations are your strongest trust signal. Add, where true, details such as how long you have been trading, your public liability insurance, the areas you cover and who carries out the fitting. Our guide to website trust signals covers this in more depth, and our guide to a testimonials page explains how to present genuine customer feedback well.

Avoid high-pressure tactics such as countdown timers on "limited" offers that are not truly limited. They can undermine trust, and misleading pricing and urgency claims may fall foul of consumer protection rules.

Platform choice

Most installers do not sell online, so a content-led site on WordPress, Squarespace or Wix usually fits better than an ecommerce platform. The exception is firms that sell doors, handles or hardware for customers to collect or fit themselves, where Shopify or WooCommerce might make sense. Either way, aim for fast loading on mobile, since many people browse these sites on their phones while standing outside their own house.

FAQs

Should I mention FENSA or CERTASS on my website?

Only if you are registered with that scheme, and follow its rules on displaying logos. If you are not, explain how compliance is handled for your installations instead.

Should customers measure their own windows for a quote?

Usually not. Ask for the rough number and type of openings and photos, and confirm exact measurements at survey.

Can I promise energy savings?

It is safer not to promise specific savings, since they depend on the property. Describe the general benefits and use accurate ratings for the products you fit.

Should repairs have their own page?

If you offer them, yes. People searching for misted glass or lock repairs want a different answer from people replacing every window.

Do I need a door colour selector?

It is optional. Clear photos organised by style and colour often do the job and are easier to maintain.
