Website Design for Consultants in the UK: Winning Trust Before the First Call

Website design guide for UK consultants: positioning, service pages, proof without overclaiming, enquiry routes, thought leadership, SEO and platforms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A consultant's website should make it obvious who you help, what problem you solve and what working with you looks like.
  • Specific, honest proof such as approach, credentials and permitted case studies builds more trust than broad claims.
  • A simple, low-pressure enquiry route and a few genuinely useful articles often matter more than a large, complex site.

Independent consultants and small consultancies sell something that cannot be seen before it is bought: expertise, judgement and the ability to get results inside someone else's organisation. That makes the website unusually important. A prospective client will often look you up after a referral, a LinkedIn conversation or a search, and decide within a few minutes whether you look credible enough to contact.

This guide is for UK consultants, whether you work in management, operations, HR, IT, marketing, sustainability or a specialist niche, who want a website that supports how they actually win work. It covers positioning, the pages that matter, how to show proof without overclaiming, enquiry routes, content, search and platform choice.

Start with positioning, not design

The most common weakness on consultant websites is vagueness. Phrases such as "helping businesses grow" or "delivering transformational change" could describe almost anyone. Before thinking about layout or colours, try to answer three questions in plain language:

  • Who do you help? A sector, a company size, a job title or a situation, such as founders preparing for investment or councils procuring new systems.
  • What problem do you solve? Describe it the way a client would describe it, not in your own technical shorthand.
  • What changes after working with you? The outcome a client is hoping for, stated carefully and without promising results you cannot guarantee.

A narrower, clearer message can feel risky, but it usually helps the right people recognise themselves. If you genuinely serve several audiences, you can give each one its own page while keeping the home page focused on what ties them together.

The pages a consultant's website needs

Most consultants do not need a large site. A focused set of pages, each doing a clear job, tends to work better than dozens of thin ones.

Home page

Your home page should state who you help and how in the first screen, then give visitors a route to the next step. A short summary of services, a sense of your approach, some proof and a clear call to action are usually enough. Our homepage design guide goes into structure in more detail.

Services or ways of working

Many consultants offer a mix of engagement types: a diagnostic or audit, a defined project, ongoing advisory support, workshops or interim roles. Explaining these as distinct offers helps buyers understand what they could actually commission. For each one, cover who it suits, what is typically involved, roughly how long it takes and what the client receives. Our guide to service page design covers this in depth.

About page

For a consultant, the about page is often one of the most visited pages. Buyers want to know whose judgement they are paying for. Include your background, relevant experience, qualifications or professional memberships you genuinely hold, and why you work the way you do. A good, natural photograph helps. The about us page guide has practical tips.

Approach or process

A page explaining how an engagement usually runs can reduce uncertainty. It does not need to be elaborate. A few stages, such as an initial conversation, scoping, delivery and review, described honestly, give buyers a sense of what to expect.

Insights or articles

Useful writing shows how you think. It also gives you something to share and gives search engines more to understand. More on this below.

Contact page

Make it easy to get in touch, with more than one route where practical. More on enquiries below.

Showing proof without overclaiming

Proof is where consultant websites often go wrong in one of two directions: either there is none at all, or there are bold claims that a cautious buyer will not believe. Aim for specific, verifiable and honest.

  • Case studies with permission. Consulting work is often confidential. Only publish client names, details or outcomes where you have clear permission. An anonymised case study describing the situation, your approach and what changed can still be valuable.
  • Testimonials that are real. Use genuine quotes, attributed in a way the client has agreed to. Never write or embellish testimonials. Our testimonials page guide covers how to collect and present them.
  • Credentials. Professional qualifications, chartered status or memberships are useful if you hold them. Do not imply accreditation you do not have.
  • Your thinking. Articles, talks or frameworks you have developed show expertise in a way that does not rely on naming clients.

Be careful with numbers. Claims about savings, revenue growth or percentage improvements should be accurate, attributable and, ideally, agreed with the client. The Advertising Standards Authority's rules apply to marketing claims on your own website, so it is worth checking their guidance if you are unsure. Our article on website trust signals has more ideas.

Pricing: to show or not to show

Consulting fees often depend on scope, so many consultants do not publish prices. That is a reasonable choice, but complete silence can deter some buyers. Middle-ground options include:

  • Publishing a starting point for a defined offer, such as a fixed-scope diagnostic.
  • Explaining how you price, whether by day rate, project or retainer, without giving figures.
  • Describing what affects cost so buyers can judge whether you are likely to fit their budget.

Whatever you choose, be consistent with how you actually quote. Our pricing page design guide explores the options.

Designing a low-pressure enquiry route

Consulting buyers are often early in their thinking when they first visit. A single "Book a call" button may feel like too big a step for some. Consider offering a few routes:

  • A short contact form. Name, email, organisation and a free-text box describing the challenge. Keep it brief. See our guide to website contact forms.
  • An introductory call booking. A scheduling tool for a short, no-obligation conversation suits buyers who are ready to talk. Our online booking systems guide covers options.
  • Email and phone. Some senior buyers simply prefer to email directly.

Say what happens after someone gets in touch, such as when you usually reply. If you collect personal data through forms, you will generally need a privacy notice; the ICO's guidance for small businesses is a sensible starting point.

Content that demonstrates expertise

For many consultants, a modest amount of genuinely useful writing does more than any design flourish. Good topics often come from questions clients ask before they hire you:

  • How to tell whether you need outside help with a particular problem.
  • Common mistakes organisations make in your area.
  • Explanations of regulations, frameworks or methods relevant to your clients, carefully hedged where they touch on legal points.
  • How to prepare for a project like the ones you run.

Quality matters more than frequency. A handful of thorough, well-structured articles that you can send to prospects can be more useful than frequent short posts. Our content strategy guide covers planning.

Search visibility for consultants

Many consultants win most work through referrals and networks, so search may be a secondary channel. Even so, people who hear your name will often search for it, and some buyers search for specialist help directly. Basic steps include:

  • Clear, descriptive page titles and headings that use the words buyers use.
  • A dedicated page for each main service or audience.
  • Consistent business details if you have a Google Business Profile and are eligible for one.
  • Fast, mobile-friendly pages with a sensible structure.

Our guide to SEO for professional services goes further, and B2B SEO is relevant if you sell to organisations.

Design choices that suit consulting

Consultant sites rarely need elaborate visuals. What helps most is clarity:

  • Readable typography with generous spacing, because buyers will read your words carefully.
  • Restrained colour that reflects your brand without distracting from content.
  • Real photography of you rather than generic stock images of handshakes and boardrooms.
  • Clear navigation so visitors can find services, about and contact quickly.
  • Accessibility considered from the start, which also tends to improve clarity for everyone. See our website accessibility guide.

Choosing a platform

The right platform depends on how much you want to manage yourself and how much content you plan to publish.

  • Squarespace or Wix can suit a small, design-led site you edit yourself.
  • WordPress suits consultants who plan to publish regularly or want more flexibility over structure and SEO.
  • A custom build may make sense for a consultancy with several consultants, complex service lines or specific integration needs.

Our comparison of website builders and custom websites explains the trade-offs, and Squarespace vs WordPress compares two common choices.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Describing services in jargon that only other consultants understand.
  • Hiding who you are behind a corporate "we" when buyers want to know the person.
  • Publishing client logos or outcomes without permission.
  • Making it hard to find contact details.
  • Launching a blog and abandoning it, leaving old posts that suggest the business is inactive.

FAQs

What should a consultant's website include?

A clear home page, service pages for each type of engagement, an about page, a short explanation of your approach, genuine proof, some useful articles and an easy way to get in touch.

Should consultants publish their day rate?

It is a personal choice. Some publish a starting price for a defined offer or explain how they price without figures, which can help buyers judge fit.

Can I mention clients I have worked with?

Only with permission. Consulting work is often confidential, so check agreements and ask before naming clients, using logos or sharing results.

Do consultants need a blog?

Not necessarily, but a few thorough articles that answer client questions can show expertise and give you useful material to share with prospects.

Which platform is best for a consultant website?

Squarespace or Wix suit a small site you manage yourself. WordPress suits more content. A custom build may suit larger consultancies with specific needs.
