Product Variants and Bundles: How to Structure Them in Your Online Store

How to set up product variants and bundles in an online store: when to use options or separate products, naming, images, stock, pricing and SEO.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Use variants for versions of the same product and separate products when items differ meaningfully or are searched for in their own right.
  • Every variant needs clear names, accurate stock and, ideally, its own image so customers know exactly what they are buying.
  • Bundles should solve a real customer need, with transparent pricing and stock that stays in sync with the individual items.

Few parts of an online store cause more confusion behind the scenes than product variants and bundles. A T-shirt in five sizes and four colours, a candle available in three scents, a starter kit made up of items you also sell separately: each raises questions about how products should be structured, how stock is tracked and how customers choose what they want.

Get the structure right and shopping feels effortless. Get it wrong and you end up with duplicate pages, mismatched stock, confused customers and a catalogue that is hard to manage. This guide explains how to think about variants and bundles, with practical advice that applies whether you use Shopify, WooCommerce or another platform.

What are product variants?

Variants are different versions of the same product, distinguished by one or more options. Common options include:

  • size,
  • colour or finish,
  • material,
  • scent or flavour, and
  • pack size or quantity.

Each combination of options, such as a medium blue T-shirt, is a variant. Variants usually share a product page, a main description and reviews, but can have their own price, stock level, SKU, barcode and image.

Variants or separate products?

The biggest structural decision is whether something should be a variant of an existing product or a product in its own right. There is no universal rule, but these questions help:

  • Would a customer think of them as the same product? Different sizes of one shirt, yes. A shirt and a matching jacket, no.
  • Is the description mostly the same? If you would need to rewrite most of the copy, they are probably separate products.
  • Do people search for them separately? If customers search for a specific colour or edition by name, a dedicated page may help it rank.
  • Are prices very different? Large price gaps can suggest separate products with distinct positioning.
  • Would one page become unwieldy? Dozens of options on one page can overwhelm customers.

Some stores take a hybrid approach: separate product pages for each colour, each with size variants, and links between colours using swatches. This can work well for fashion, where colour often drives search and browsing, while size is a simple choice made on the page.

Platform limits to be aware of

Every platform handles variants slightly differently. Shopify, for example, has limits on the number of options and variants per product, which have changed over time; check the current limits for your plan. WooCommerce uses variable products with attributes, and very large numbers of variations can affect performance in the admin and on the page.

If your product has many combinations, such as custom furniture with fabrics, sizes and leg finishes, a standard variant setup may not be the best fit. Product option apps, extensions or a custom configurator may suit better, but these add complexity, so weigh up the trade-offs.

Designing the variant selector

How customers pick variants on the product page has a big influence on usability. Good practice includes:

  • Visual swatches for colour and finish rather than text-only dropdowns, so customers can see the choice.
  • Buttons for sizes where there are only a few options, which are quicker to use than dropdowns, especially on mobile.
  • Clear out-of-stock states, such as greyed-out options, with a back-in-stock notification where possible.
  • Images that update when a variant is selected, so the customer sees the exact version they are buying.
  • Price changes shown instantly if variants are priced differently.
  • Size guides close to the size selector, not buried elsewhere.

Avoid preselecting a variant in a way that lets customers add the wrong one to their basket without noticing, particularly for sizes. Either require a choice or make the selected option very obvious.

Naming and data hygiene

Consistent naming makes your catalogue easier to manage and easier for customers to understand. Decide on conventions and stick to them:

  • Use the same option names across products, such as always "Colour" rather than mixing "Color", "Colour" and "Shade".
  • Use customer-friendly values. "Forest green" is more helpful than an internal code.
  • Give each variant a unique SKU so stock, orders and reporting stay accurate.
  • Add barcodes or GTINs where you have them, which matters for Google Shopping feeds.

Messy variant data causes problems that spread: filters stop working properly on category pages, feeds get rejected and stock reports become unreliable.

Variants and SEO

Variants usually share one URL, which keeps ranking signals consolidated on a single strong page. That is usually what you want. Some platforms also create variant-specific URLs through parameters; make sure canonical tags point to the main product page unless you have a deliberate reason to index variants separately.

If a particular variant has genuine search demand of its own, a separate product page with unique content can make sense. Avoid creating dozens of near-identical pages with the same description, which can dilute relevance and create thin content.

Product structured data can describe variants, and Google supports markup for product variant groups. Your platform or theme may handle some of this automatically, but it is worth checking that prices and availability are reported accurately.

What are product bundles?

A bundle is a group of products sold together, often at a combined price. Common types include:

  • Fixed bundles: a set selection, such as a starter kit or gift set.
  • Build-your-own bundles: the customer chooses items from a range to fill a box or set.
  • Multi-buys: several of the same item, such as a pack of three.
  • Frequently bought together: suggested combinations on the product page or in the basket.

When bundles make sense

Bundles work best when they solve a real problem for the customer. Good reasons to bundle include:

  • helping new customers get started with everything they need,
  • making gifting easier,
  • offering better value for customers who would buy several items anyway, and
  • introducing customers to products they might not have tried.

Bundles created purely to clear slow-moving stock, padded with items nobody wants, tend to disappoint. Customers can usually tell.

Bundle pricing and presentation

Be transparent about what is included and what the customer saves, if anything. Show:

  • each item in the bundle, with images and a short description,
  • the bundle price and, where accurate, what the items would cost separately, and
  • any choices the customer needs to make, such as sizes or colours for items within the bundle.

Make sure any comparison prices are genuine. Claiming a saving against prices that were never actually charged is misleading and can breach consumer protection rules, so check current guidance on pricing practices if you are unsure.

Stock management for bundles

This is where bundles often go wrong. If a bundle is set up as an entirely separate product with its own stock count, it can show as available when one of its components has sold out, or vice versa. Look for a setup where bundle availability is calculated from the stock of its individual items, so sales of either reduce the shared inventory.

Many bundle apps and extensions handle this, but test thoroughly. Place test orders and check that stock, fulfilment information and reporting all behave as expected.

Fulfilment considerations

Think about how bundles will be picked and packed. Will they be pre-assembled and stored as a single unit, or assembled at the time of order? Pre-assembled bundles are quicker to ship but tie up stock. Assembling to order is more flexible but needs clear picking instructions so nothing is missed.

Returns also need thought. Decide whether customers can return part of a bundle and how refunds will be calculated, and explain this on your returns page.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Creating separate products for every size, cluttering search and category pages.
  • Inconsistent option names that break filters.
  • Variants without images, leaving customers unsure what they will receive.
  • Bundles with stock counts that do not reflect component availability.
  • Misleading "was" prices on bundles.
  • Duplicate content across near-identical product pages.

FAQs

Should each colour have its own product page?

It depends. If colours are searched for separately or need different content, separate pages can help. Otherwise, colour variants on one page keep things simpler.

Do product variants affect SEO?

Variants on a single page usually help by consolidating signals. Problems arise when variant URLs are indexed as duplicates, so check your canonical tags.

How do I keep bundle stock accurate?

Use a setup where bundle availability is based on the stock of each component item, then test it with real orders before launch.

Can customers build their own bundles?

Yes, on most major platforms with the right app, extension or custom development. Keep the selection process simple and show progress clearly.

Is there a limit to how many variants a product can have?

Most platforms have practical or formal limits. Check the current limits for your platform and plan, and consider other approaches for highly configurable products.
