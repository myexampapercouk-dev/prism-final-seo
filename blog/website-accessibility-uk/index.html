Website Accessibility in the UK: A Practical Guide to WCAG for Businesses

What website accessibility means for UK businesses, how WCAG works in plain English, the common problems to fix first and how to build it into a new site.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Accessibility means people can use your site whatever their sight, hearing, movement or way of reading.
  • WCAG is the widely used technical standard; most businesses aim for its AA level.
  • Many common problems, such as poor contrast, missing alt text and unlabelled forms, are straightforward to fix.

An accessible website is one that people can use whether they see the screen clearly or not, use a mouse or a keyboard, hear audio or rely on captions, or read quickly or slowly. For a business, that is simply more people able to find what they need, buy or get in touch.

Prism Works Studio is a UK web design, ecommerce and digital marketing agency working remotely with businesses across the country. This guide explains accessibility in practical terms for owners and marketing teams. It is general information, not legal advice.

Who accessibility helps

It is easy to picture accessibility as something for a small group of screen reader users. In practice it helps a much wider range of visitors:

  • People with low vision or colour blindness who need good contrast and text that can be enlarged
  • Blind visitors using screen readers that read the page aloud
  • People who navigate by keyboard or switch devices rather than a mouse
  • Deaf and hard of hearing visitors who need captions or transcripts for video
  • People with dyslexia, cognitive differences or anxiety who benefit from clear layouts and plain language
  • Anyone with a temporary injury, a cracked phone screen or bright sunlight on their display

What WCAG is

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, usually shortened to WCAG, are the standard most organisations use to judge whether a site is accessible. They are organised around four principles: content should be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. Each guideline has success criteria graded at levels A, AA and AAA.

LevelWhat it means in practice
AThe basics. Missing these can block some people entirely
AAThe level most organisations aim for, covering contrast, focus visibility, consistent navigation and more
AAAThe most demanding level, usually applied to specific content rather than a whole site

WCAG is updated from time to time, so check which version is current when you write a brief.

The legal picture, hedged

In the UK, the Equality Act 2010 places duties on service providers not to discriminate against disabled people, and this is widely understood to extend to websites and apps. Public sector bodies have separate, more specific accessibility regulations. How the law applies to a particular private business depends on its circumstances, so if you need certainty, speak to a qualified legal adviser. Nothing in this guide is legal advice.

Whatever the legal position, accessibility is good practice and makes commercial sense, because a site more people can use is a site more people can buy from.

Common problems worth fixing first

Many sites share the same handful of issues. Fixing these often makes the biggest difference for the least effort:

  • Low colour contrast, such as pale grey text on white or text over busy images
  • Missing or unhelpful alt text on meaningful images, and alt text on purely decorative ones that should be skipped
  • Unlabelled form fields where placeholder text disappears as soon as someone types
  • Invisible keyboard focus, so keyboard users cannot see where they are on the page
  • Vague link text like "click here" or "read more" repeated many times
  • Skipped or misused headings, which make pages hard to navigate with a screen reader
  • Pop-ups and menus that trap focus or cannot be closed with a keyboard
  • Video without captions and audio without transcripts

How to check your own site

A sensible first pass does not need special training. Try these yourself:

  1. Put the mouse away and use only the Tab, Shift+Tab and Enter keys to move through your main pages and complete a form or checkout.
  2. Zoom the browser to 200% and see whether text overlaps or content disappears.
  3. Look at your images and ask whether the alt text would make sense if read aloud.
  4. Read your headings on their own and check they outline the page logically.
  5. Run an automated accessibility checker built into your browser's developer tools.

Automated tools only catch part of the picture. They can tell you an image lacks alt text, but not whether the alt text is any good. A manual review, ideally including testing with assistive technology, gives a fuller view.

Building accessibility into a new site

Accessibility is far cheaper to include from the start than to retrofit. If you are planning a new build or redesign, put it in the brief:

  • State the WCAG level you are aiming for
  • Choose brand colours that pass contrast checks for text and buttons, or agree accessible variants
  • Ask that templates use proper headings, labels and focus styles by default
  • Make sure your team knows how to add alt text and structure content when they publish
  • Agree how accessibility will be tested before launch

Ecommerce has extra pressure points

Online shops have more interactive parts, and each one is a chance for something to break for keyboard or screen reader users. Product filters, size selectors, image galleries, basket drawers and checkout forms all deserve testing. Apps and plugins added later can introduce problems even on a theme that started out accessible, so check again after installing anything new. Accessibility and SEO overlap

Several accessibility basics also help search engines understand a page: descriptive headings, meaningful link text, text alternatives for images and a logical structure. Accessibility is not an SEO trick, but the two often pull in the same direction.

Writing accessible content

Much of accessibility sits with the people who publish content, not just the developers who build templates. A few habits make a real difference:

  • Use headings in order to structure a page, rather than making text bold and large to look like a heading
  • Write link text that makes sense out of context, such as "download the price list" instead of "click here"
  • Keep sentences and paragraphs reasonably short, and explain jargon
  • Avoid putting important information only in images of text, such as a promotional banner with the offer details baked in
  • Do not rely on colour alone to convey meaning, for example marking required fields only in red
  • Provide captions for video and transcripts for podcasts or audio

PDFs deserve a mention. Menus, brochures and forms are often uploaded as PDFs that screen readers struggle with. Where possible, put the key information on a normal web page instead.

Writing an accessibility statement

An accessibility statement tells visitors what standard you are working towards, any known problems you have not yet fixed and how to contact you if they have difficulty using the site. It should be honest rather than promotional. Claiming full compliance when you have not tested for it can do more harm than good, so describe what you have actually checked.

Prioritising fixes on an existing site

If an audit produces a long list, start with issues that completely block people from key tasks, such as a checkout that cannot be completed with a keyboard or a contact form with no labels. Then move to problems on your most visited pages, and finally to lower-impact issues across the rest of the site. Fixing shared templates, such as headers, footers and product page layouts, often resolves the same problem across hundreds of pages at once.

Keeping it accessible

A site can launch in good shape and slowly drift as new pages, images and plugins are added. Include accessibility checks in routine updates and in your website maintenance plan, and publish an accessibility statement explaining your approach and how people can report problems.

FAQs

Is website accessibility a legal requirement in the UK?

The Equality Act 2010 is generally understood to apply to websites, and public sector bodies have specific regulations. How it applies to your business depends on your circumstances. This is not legal advice, so speak to a legal adviser if you need a definitive answer.

What WCAG level should a business aim for?

Most organisations aim for level AA, which covers the most significant barriers without the demands of AAA across every page.

Can an accessibility overlay or widget make my site compliant?

Be cautious. A toolbar added on top of a site does not fix underlying problems in the code and content. Fixing the site itself is the more reliable approach.

Are automated accessibility checkers enough?

No. They are useful for catching some issues quickly, but many problems need a person to judge, such as whether alt text or link wording actually makes sense.

Does accessibility mean a plain-looking website?

Not at all. Accessible sites can be visually distinctive. It mainly means making considered choices about contrast, type, layout and interaction.
