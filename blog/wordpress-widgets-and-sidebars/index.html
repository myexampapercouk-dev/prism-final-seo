WordPress Widgets and Sidebars: How to Use Them Well

How WordPress widgets and sidebars work in classic and block themes, what to put in them, how to keep them tidy and when a sidebar is better left out entirely.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Widgets are small content blocks shown in theme areas such as sidebars and footers, and how you manage them depends on whether your theme is classic or block-based.
  • Every widget should earn its place by helping visitors take a useful next step, not just filling space.
  • Sidebars can clutter mobile layouts and slow pages, so review them regularly and remove anything that does not serve a purpose.

Widgets and sidebars have been part of WordPress for a long time. They are the small blocks of content that appear beside or below your main content: a search box, a list of recent posts, a newsletter sign-up, opening hours or a short "about us" note. Used well, they help visitors find their way around. Used carelessly, they create clutter that distracts from what the page is actually for.

The way widgets work has also changed as WordPress has moved towards blocks and full site editing. This guide explains what widgets and sidebars are, how they work in different types of theme, what is worth including and how to keep them tidy.

What are widgets and sidebars?

A widget is a self-contained piece of content or functionality that can be placed in a defined area of your theme. Examples include a search form, a list of categories, a calendar, a custom text block or an image.

A sidebar, in WordPress terms, is any area of a theme that can hold widgets. The name comes from the traditional column that sits beside the main content, but these areas are often called widget areas and can appear in footers, headers or below posts too. Which areas exist depends entirely on your theme.

Classic themes versus block themes

How you manage widgets depends on the kind of theme your site uses.

Classic themes

Classic themes define widget areas in their code. You manage the contents through Appearance, then Widgets, in the dashboard, or through the Customizer. In recent versions of WordPress, the widgets screen uses a block-based editor, so you can add blocks such as paragraphs, images and lists into widget areas, alongside traditional widgets. Some sites have the older widget screen restored, which looks more like a list of draggable boxes.

Block themes

Block themes use the Site Editor rather than a widgets screen. There is no separate concept of widget areas: instead, you edit templates and template parts, such as the header, footer or a sidebar column, and add blocks directly. The ideas are similar, but the tools are different. If your site uses a block theme and you cannot find Appearance, then Widgets, this is usually why.

If you are not sure which type you have, check under Appearance. A Site Editor option usually indicates a block theme, while Widgets and Customize options usually indicate a classic theme. Our WordPress block editor guide explains the block approach in more detail.

Useful things to put in a sidebar or widget area

A good rule is that each item should help a visitor take a sensible next step. Some widely useful options include:

  • Search. On content-heavy sites, a search box helps people find specific articles quickly.
  • Categories or topics. A short list of main blog categories helps readers explore related content. Keep it short; a list of dozens of categories is hard to scan.
  • Recent or featured posts. A handful of links to recent or important articles can keep readers on the site.
  • A clear call to action. A short box inviting visitors to get in touch, book a call or view your services can work well on blog posts. Our guide to call-to-action buttons covers wording and placement.
  • Contact details or opening hours. Particularly useful in footers for local businesses.
  • A newsletter sign-up. If you send a newsletter, a simple sign-up form can be placed in a sidebar or footer. Make sure it is clear what people are signing up for.

Things that often add clutter

Older WordPress sites in particular can accumulate widgets that no longer serve anyone. Candidates for removal include:

  • Archive lists showing every month since the site began.
  • Tag clouds with dozens of tags of varying sizes.
  • Meta widgets with login and feed links, which most visitors do not need.
  • Calendars that show when posts were published.
  • Social media feeds that duplicate content and may load extra scripts.
  • Multiple competing calls to action stacked on top of each other.

None of these is wrong in every case, but each should be justified. If you would not miss it, it can probably go.

Sidebars on mobile

On smaller screens, a sidebar column usually drops below the main content. That means visitors on phones may have to scroll past a long stack of widgets to reach the footer, or may never see them at all. Check how your sidebar behaves on a phone and ask whether it still helps there.

Some themes let you hide certain widget areas on mobile, and in block themes you can design templates with mobile layout in mind. Either way, it is worth reviewing the mobile experience separately rather than assuming the desktop layout translates well.

Do you need a sidebar at all?

Sidebars are not compulsory. Many modern designs use full-width layouts for key pages and keep sidebars, if any, for blog posts. Consider the purpose of each template:

  • Homepage and service pages. These often work better without a sidebar, so that visitors focus on the main message and calls to action.
  • Blog posts. A sidebar can help readers find related content or get in touch, although an in-content box or a related posts section at the end can do the same job.
  • Shop pages. On ecommerce sites, a sidebar may hold product filters, which can be genuinely useful on category pages.
  • Landing pages. Pages built for a specific campaign usually benefit from removing distractions, including sidebars.

Widgets, speed and security

Each widget can add markup, scripts or requests to external services. A few heavy widgets, such as embedded feeds or third-party embeds, can slow pages noticeably. If speed is a concern, review what each widget loads. Our guide to website speed optimisation covers the wider picture.

Many widgets come from plugins. Removing a widget from a sidebar does not remove the plugin behind it, so if you no longer need a plugin's widgets, consider whether the plugin itself is still needed. Our WordPress plugin audit guide explains how to review plugins safely.

Keeping widgets tidy

Review them regularly

Every so often, visit a few pages on desktop and mobile and look at your sidebars and footers with fresh eyes. Check that links work, information is current and nothing looks out of place.

Check after theme changes

Widget areas belong to themes. If you switch theme, widgets may move to different areas, end up in an inactive section or disappear from view. After any theme change, check widget content carefully. Moving from a classic to a block theme usually means rebuilding these areas in the Site Editor.

Use consistent content

If your opening hours, phone number or address appear in a footer widget, make sure they match your contact page and other listings. Inconsistent details confuse visitors.

Consider reusable content

If the same content appears in several places, keeping one source of truth reduces the risk of versions drifting apart. In block-based editing, synced patterns can help with this.

Customising widget areas safely

Adding new widget areas to a classic theme, or changing where existing ones appear, usually involves editing theme files. Making those changes directly in a parent theme means they could be overwritten when the theme updates. Using a child theme keeps customisations separate. Our guide to WordPress child themes explains how this works.

In block themes, much more can be done through the Site Editor without code, by editing template parts and adding columns or groups where you need them.

FAQs

Why can I not find the Widgets menu in WordPress?

If your site uses a block theme, there is no Widgets menu, because widget areas are replaced by templates and template parts edited in the Site Editor. With a classic theme, the menu sits under Appearance, though some themes have no widget areas at all.

Can I use blocks in widget areas?

Yes. In recent versions of WordPress, the widgets screen in classic themes is block-based, so you can add standard blocks such as paragraphs, images and buttons to widget areas.

Do sidebars help SEO?

A sidebar is not an SEO feature in itself. Sensible internal links in a sidebar can help visitors and search engines discover related content, but a cluttered sidebar full of links adds little. Focus on usefulness to visitors first.

Will changing my theme delete my widgets?

Changing theme does not usually delete widget content outright, but widgets may be moved to an inactive area or placed in different locations because the new theme has different widget areas. Check and rearrange them after any theme change, ideally on a staging copy first.

Should my homepage have a sidebar?

Often not. Homepages tend to work better with a focused, full-width layout that guides visitors towards your main services or products. Sidebars are more commonly useful on blog posts and archive pages.
