Web Design in Swansea: Bilingual Websites for Coastal Tourism, Activity Providers and Local Businesses

Web design for Swansea and Gower businesses: bilingual Welsh and English sites, coastal tourism bookings, activity providers and seasonal planning.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Decide early how Welsh and English will work together on the site, because it shapes structure, navigation and search.
  • Coastal tourism and activity sites need live availability, clear conditions policies and pages that work on a phone outdoors.
  • Plan content around the seasons: peak bookings, shoulder-season offers and quieter-month local trade.

Web design in Swansea has to account for two things that shape many local businesses: the coast and the language. The city sits on a long bay, with the Mumbles and the Gower peninsula close by, so tourism, hospitality and outdoor activity providers make up a large part of the local economy. At the same time, Swansea is in Wales, where Welsh and English sit side by side, and many businesses want or need to present themselves in both. Prism Works Studio works with Swansea and Gower businesses remotely, handling planning, design and build over video calls and shared drafts.

Our Cardiff guide covers capital-city professional services, the public sector and the wider Welsh business scene. This guide focuses on what is specific to Swansea's coastal visitor economy and on the practical side of building a bilingual site that a small team can maintain.

Planning a bilingual Welsh and English website

Whether you need Welsh content depends on your organisation. Public bodies in Wales have duties under the Welsh language standards, and some organisations that receive public funding may have related expectations. Most private businesses are not legally required to publish in Welsh, but many choose to, because customers value it and it reflects the place. If you are unsure what applies to you, check with the Welsh Language Commissioner's guidance or take advice.

Once you decide to go bilingual, the structural choices matter more than the design:

  • Separate language versions or mixed pages. Separate versions, for example under /cy/ and /en/ paths, are generally easier for visitors and search engines to understand than pages that mix both languages throughout.
  • A clear, consistent language switcher. It should appear in the same place on every page and take visitors to the equivalent page in the other language, not back to the home page.
  • Language tags for search engines. Correct language markup, including hreflang, helps Google show the right version to the right searcher.
  • Equal treatment. If you offer both languages, avoid a Welsh version that is noticeably thinner or out of date. Visitors notice.
  • Proper translation. Use a qualified translator rather than machine translation for published content, and agree who checks updates.

Platform choice affects how easy all this is. WordPress can handle multilingual sites with the right setup, and Shopify supports multiple languages for online shops. Squarespace and Wix also offer multilingual options, with varying flexibility. The important question is whether your team can add and update content in both languages without help.

Keeping bilingual content manageable

The real cost of a bilingual site is ongoing, not upfront. Every new blog post, offer or opening-hours change has to happen twice. Small businesses can keep this manageable by:

  • Keeping the core site focused, with fewer, stronger pages rather than lots of thin ones.
  • Agreeing a simple workflow: who writes, who translates and who publishes.
  • Using structured fields for things like prices, times and dates, so they are entered once and shown in both languages where the platform allows.
  • Deciding which content is bilingual and which, such as a short-lived social post, can stay in one language.

Coastal tourism: bookings first

Visitors planning a trip to Swansea Bay or Gower are often comparing options on a phone, sometimes while already in the area. Accommodation, cafés, surf schools, coasteering and kayaking providers, boat trips, campsites and attractions all benefit from making the next step obvious.

Useful elements include:

  • Live availability and online booking. Visitors are more likely to book when they can see dates and times rather than having to send an enquiry. Our guide to online booking systems covers the options.
  • Clear location information. Exact meeting points, parking, public transport and accessibility, which matters on a peninsula with narrow roads and limited parking.
  • What to expect and what to bring. Particularly for outdoor activities: age limits, ability levels, kit provided and what to wear.
  • Photography of the real place. Your own beaches, coastline and venue, rather than generic seaside images.

Accommodation providers can find more in our guides to holiday let websites and hotel and B&B websites, and winning more direct bookings.

Weather, tides and cancellation policies

Coastal activity businesses depend on conditions. A surf lesson, boat trip or coasteering session may move or be cancelled because of wind, swell or tides. Websites that address this clearly reduce anxious enquiries and disputes.

Explain your conditions policy in plain language: how and when you decide whether a session goes ahead, how customers will be told, and what happens to their booking. Put it near the booking button as well as in your terms. If you post daily updates on whether sessions are running, a simple status notice on the home page can save a lot of phone calls. Consumer law sets out rights around cancellations and refunds, so it is sensible to take advice on how your policy is worded.

Safety information also belongs on activity sites. Describe the qualifications and safety standards you work to accurately, and only mention accreditations or memberships you actually hold.

Seasonality and the quieter months

Swansea's visitor trade rises and falls through the year. A website can help smooth that out:

  • Peak season. Booking and availability front and centre, with clear information on busy times.
  • Shoulder seasons. Content about what makes spring and autumn worth visiting, such as quieter beaches, walking and wildlife, alongside midweek or short-break offers.
  • Winter. Gift vouchers, local events, private hire and local customers. Our guide to gift cards and vouchers covers selling them online.

Plan this as a calendar so the website changes before each season begins, not halfway through it.

Local businesses beyond tourism

Swansea also has plenty of businesses serving local people rather than visitors: trades, retailers, health and wellbeing practices, professional firms and food producers. For them, local search visibility usually matters most. A complete Google Business Profile, consistent contact details, clear service area pages and genuine reviews all help. Bilingual business profiles and website content can be a point of difference locally. Our local SEO guide explains the basics.

Accessibility for all visitors

Tourism sites serve a wide range of people, including older visitors and those with disabilities who need to know whether a venue, path or activity will work for them. Honest access information, readable text, good contrast and forms that work with assistive technology all help. Our website accessibility guide covers the essentials.

Food, drink and local produce

Swansea and Gower also have food and drink businesses that trade with both visitors and locals: cafés, bakeries, farm shops, seafood sellers, breweries and producers selling at markets. Many could reach customers beyond the summer season by selling online or taking pre-orders for collection. Clear product pages, accurate allergen information, delivery or collection options and a simple checkout are the foundations. Our food and drink ecommerce guide covers the practical and legal points of selling food online, which apply in both languages if you publish bilingually.

Photography and maps for a spread-out area

Gower's beaches, bays and villages can be confusing for first-time visitors. Embedding a map, giving accurate postcodes and written directions to the exact spot, and describing the walk from the nearest car park can prevent missed bookings. Use your own photographs of the actual meeting point or entrance, so visitors recognise it when they arrive.

FAQs

Does my Swansea business have to have a Welsh language website?

Most private businesses are not legally required to, but public bodies and some publicly funded organisations have duties. Check the guidance that applies to you or take advice.

Should Welsh and English be on the same page?

Separate language versions with a clear switcher are usually easier for visitors and search engines than pages mixing both languages throughout.

Can we use machine translation for the Welsh version?

It is generally better to use a qualified translator for published content, as poor translation can undermine the reason for offering Welsh at all.

How should an activity provider explain weather cancellations?

Set out how decisions are made, how customers are told and what happens to bookings, and show it near the booking button. Take advice on wording.

Which platform suits a bilingual tourism site?

WordPress, Shopify, Squarespace and Wix all offer multilingual options. The best choice depends on your booking needs and how easily your team can update both languages.

Do you have an office in Swansea?

No. Prism Works Studio works with Swansea businesses remotely, using video calls and shared drafts throughout the project.
