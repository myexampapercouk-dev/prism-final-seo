Ecommerce Packaging and Unboxing: Protecting Orders and Making a Good Impression

How online shops can plan packaging that protects products, controls costs, considers sustainability and creates a memorable unboxing experience for customers.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Packaging has to protect the product first; presentation and branding come after that.
  • Size, weight and materials affect delivery costs, damage rates and how customers feel about your brand's environmental impact.
  • The unboxing moment connects back to your website through inserts, care information and easy routes to reviews, reorders and returns.

For an online shop, the parcel is usually the first physical contact a customer has with the brand. Everything before that point happens on a screen: the product pages, the checkout, the confirmation emails. When the box arrives, the experience becomes tangible, and the packaging plays a large part in how that moment feels.

Packaging also has practical consequences. It affects whether products arrive intact, how much delivery costs, how easy returns are and how customers judge your approach to waste. This guide covers how to think about ecommerce packaging, from protection and cost through to branding, sustainability and the link back to your website.

Start with protection

Before thinking about tissue paper and stickers, the packaging needs to get the product to the customer undamaged. A beautifully presented parcel containing a broken item creates a worse impression than a plain box that arrives safely.

Consider:

  • Fragility. Glass, ceramics, electronics and items with delicate edges need cushioning and a box that limits movement.
  • Weight. Heavier items need stronger boxes and secure sealing.
  • Shape. Awkward shapes may need inserts or custom sizing to stop them moving in transit.
  • Liquids and perishables. These may need leak protection, insulation or specific carrier services, and some items have carrier restrictions.

It can help to send test parcels to yourself or colleagues using your chosen carrier and see how they arrive. Tracking which products are reported damaged over time also shows where packaging needs improving.

Right-sizing and delivery costs

Carriers generally price parcels by weight and size, and some apply dimensional pricing where large, light parcels cost more than their weight alone suggests. Oversized boxes can push parcels into a more expensive band, need more void fill and look wasteful to customers.

Keeping a small range of box and mailer sizes that fit your most common orders can help control costs without making packing too complicated. Flat mailers, padded envelopes and boxes that fit through a letterbox can suit smaller items and may reduce failed deliveries, depending on the carrier service.

Delivery costs affect how you present delivery charges and thresholds on your site. Our guide to delivery and returns pages covers communicating these clearly.

Choosing packaging materials

Common options for online shops include:

  • Corrugated cardboard boxes for most general items, available in different strengths.
  • Cardboard mailers that fold and lock without much tape, often used for clothing, books and gifts.
  • Paper mailers and padded paper envelopes for flat or soft items.
  • Plastic mailing bags, which are light and weatherproof, often used for clothing.
  • Void fill and cushioning, such as paper, cardboard inserts, moulded pulp or air pillows.

Each has trade-offs between protection, weight, cost, storage space and recyclability. What suits one product range may not suit another.

Sustainability and packaging rules

Many customers pay attention to packaging waste, and excessive packaging or materials that are hard to recycle can attract complaints. Practical steps include using fewer layers, choosing materials that are widely recyclable at home, avoiding mixed materials that are difficult to separate and right-sizing boxes to reduce void fill.

If you describe packaging as recyclable, compostable or plastic-free, make sure the claim is accurate and specific. UK consumer protection rules and guidance on environmental claims apply to how you describe your products and packaging, and vague green claims can be misleading. The Competition and Markets Authority has published guidance on environmental claims, and it is worth checking it or taking advice before making such statements.

There are also UK packaging regulations, including extended producer responsibility requirements and plastic packaging tax, that may apply depending on the size of your business and the packaging you handle. Thresholds and obligations can change, so check current government guidance or speak to an adviser to understand whether and how they affect you.

Branding and presentation

Once protection and cost are covered, packaging can reinforce your brand. Options range from simple to elaborate:

  • Branded tape or stickers on plain boxes, a relatively low-cost way to add recognition.
  • Printed boxes or mailers with your logo or pattern, usually with minimum order quantities.
  • Tissue paper, ribbons or wrapping inside the box for a more gift-like feel.
  • Interior printing on the inside of a box, which shows only when it is opened.

Branded packaging should reflect the same visual identity as your website, using consistent colours, logo use and tone. Our guide to brand identity design explains how to set this up so it carries across every touchpoint.

Consider whether branding on the outside of a parcel suits your products. For some items, such as gifts or personal products, customers may prefer discreet outer packaging. You could keep the outer box plain and put the brand presentation inside.

Designing the unboxing experience

The unboxing experience is the sequence a customer goes through when opening a parcel: what they see first, what they touch, what they read and how the product is revealed. It does not need to be extravagant. A tidy, thoughtful arrangement can feel more considered than a box packed with extras.

Elements to think about include:

  • First view. What is visible when the lid opens? A neatly folded wrap, a card or the product itself?
  • Order of reveal. Is there a natural sequence, or does the customer have to dig through void fill?
  • Ease of opening. Tear strips or easy-open tabs can make the moment smoother. Packaging that needs scissors and strength can frustrate.
  • Ease of repacking. If customers might return items, packaging that can be resealed or reused for returns helps.

The unboxing experience also needs to be repeatable. Whatever you design, it has to be packed consistently, at volume, in busy periods. Elaborate presentation that takes several minutes per order may not be sustainable as you grow.

Inserts: linking the parcel back to your website

Printed inserts are a simple way to connect the physical parcel to your online store. Options include:

  • A thank-you note, printed or handwritten, which can add a personal touch.
  • Care or usage instructions, which help customers get the most from the product and may reduce returns.
  • Returns information explaining how to start a return and pointing to your returns page.
  • A prompt to leave a review, perhaps with a short web address or QR code. Our product reviews guide covers collecting reviews fairly.
  • A discount or reorder prompt, which can encourage a second purchase.

Keep inserts focused. A parcel stuffed with leaflets can feel cluttered and wasteful. One well-designed card often works better than several.

If you use QR codes or short links, make sure they go to mobile-friendly pages that load quickly and relate directly to the insert's message.

Connecting packaging with post-purchase emails

Packaging works alongside the emails customers receive after ordering. Order confirmation, dispatch and delivery emails set expectations about when the parcel will arrive and what it will look like. If packaging is discreet or unusual, such as a letterbox-sized parcel, mentioning this in the dispatch email can help.

A follow-up email a few days after delivery can pick up the threads from the insert: care tips, an invitation to review or suggestions for related products. Our guides to transactional emails and ecommerce email marketing cover this in more detail.

Gift options

Many shops offer gift wrapping, gift messages or the option to remove prices from the packing slip. These can be added as checkout options or product add-ons on platforms such as Shopify and WooCommerce. If you offer them, show clearly on the product or basket page what the customer will receive, and make sure your packing team can see the instructions on each order.

If gift wrapping changes the packaging size or weight, factor this into your delivery pricing.

Showing packaging on your website

Packaging can be part of how you sell. Photos or short videos showing how products arrive can help customers buying gifts, reassure people about fragile items and communicate your approach to sustainability. Product photography that includes packaging, where relevant, can sit alongside standard product shots. Our product photography guide covers planning shots.

A short section on your delivery page or FAQ page about how orders are packed can answer common questions before customers ask.

Packaging and returns

Returns are part of selling online, and packaging affects how easy they are. Packaging that can be reused, resealable mailers and a clear returns insert can make returns smoother for customers and reduce damage to items coming back. Your returns policy should explain what condition items need to be in, and you should check current consumer rights rules on returns for distance sales, as customers have certain statutory rights regardless of your own policy.

Reviewing your packaging over time

Packaging decisions are worth revisiting. Useful signals include damage reports, customer comments and reviews mentioning packaging, delivery cost changes, packing time per order and returns reasons. Small changes, such as a different box size for a best-selling product, can make a noticeable difference.

FAQs

Does branded packaging matter for a small online shop?

It can help with recognition, but it is not essential. Plain, well-sized packaging with a branded sticker or insert can work well while you grow.

How can I reduce packaging costs?

Right-size boxes to your common orders, reduce void fill, compare suppliers and check how carriers price by size and weight.

What should I put inside an ecommerce parcel?

The product, packing slip and, optionally, a short insert with thanks, care information, returns details or a review prompt. Keep it focused.

Can I call my packaging eco-friendly?

Only if the claim is accurate, specific and can be backed up. Check UK guidance on environmental claims or take advice before making them.

Do packaging regulations apply to my business?

Some UK packaging rules apply depending on business size and packaging volume. Check current government guidance or speak to an adviser.
