Aesthetics Clinic Website Design: Consultations, Treatments and Compliance

Website design for UK aesthetics clinics: treatment pages, consultation booking, practitioner credentials, before and after images and advertising rules.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Prescription-only medicines such as botulinum toxin generally cannot be advertised to the public in the UK, which shapes how an aesthetics clinic website can describe some treatments.
  • Consultation-led booking, clear practitioner credentials and honest information about risks and recovery tend to build more trust than bold promises.
  • Rules in this area are detailed and change over time, so clinics should check current guidance from the ASA, CAP, MHRA and their own regulator, and take advice where needed.

An aesthetics clinic website has to do two things that can pull in different directions. It needs to present treatments attractively enough that people want to book a consultation, and it needs to stay within some of the stricter advertising rules any UK business faces. A site that gets this balance right looks polished, reads as trustworthy, and avoids the kind of claims that can lead to complaints or regulatory action.

This guide covers website design for UK aesthetics and cosmetic clinics, including medical aesthetics practices, skin clinics and practitioners offering non-surgical treatments. It focuses on the website itself. Beauty salons offering non-medical treatments may find our guide to beauty salon marketing more relevant, and skincare product brands our guide to Shopify for beauty and skincare brands. This guide is not legal advice.

What an aesthetics clinic website needs to do

For most clinics, the website's main jobs are to:

  • Explain the treatments and concerns the clinic can help with, accurately and responsibly.
  • Encourage people to book a consultation rather than a treatment outright.
  • Show who the practitioners are and what qualifications they hold.
  • Give honest information about suitability, risks, recovery and aftercare.
  • Make prices or price guidance, location and contact details easy to find.

Each of these is shaped by the advertising rules, so it helps to understand them before designing pages.

The rules that shape the website

Several overlapping sets of rules can apply. Clinics should check the current versions directly, but in broad terms:

  • Prescription-only medicines. UK law generally prohibits advertising prescription-only medicines to the public. Botulinum toxin products fall into this category. The MHRA and the ASA have both published guidance on how this applies to aesthetics websites and social media, including the use of related terms and imagery. In practice, many clinics talk about consultations for concerns such as lines and wrinkles, rather than promoting a named prescription medicine.
  • The CAP Code. Advertising on your own website is covered by the CAP Code, enforced by the Advertising Standards Authority. Claims need to be supportable, must not mislead, and should not exaggerate results or trivialise risks. There are specific rules about targeting cosmetic interventions at under-18s.
  • Professional regulators. Doctors, nurses, dentists and pharmacists offering aesthetic treatments are also bound by their own regulators' standards on advertising and consent.
  • Changing licensing rules. Licensing for some non-surgical cosmetic procedures has been under development in parts of the UK, so clinics should keep an eye on changes that may affect what they need to display or how they operate.

Because interpretation depends on precise wording and context, it can be worth having website copy reviewed by someone with relevant expertise before launch.

Treatment and concern pages

Given the rules around prescription-only medicines, many clinics organise their website around concerns and consultations rather than named products. For example, a page about lines and wrinkles might explain that the clinic offers a consultation to discuss suitable options, without promoting a specific medicine. Treatments that are not prescription-only, such as many skin treatments, may be described more directly, but still need careful, accurate wording.

For each page, consider including:

  • A plain explanation of the concern or treatment area.
  • What happens at a consultation.
  • General information about suitability, and who may not be suitable.
  • Honest information about potential side effects, risks and recovery time.
  • Price information, or how pricing is decided after consultation.

Avoid phrases such as guaranteed results, risk-free, painless or permanent unless you are certain they are accurate and supportable. Time-limited offers and discounts on medical treatments can also raise concerns, because they may pressure people into decisions. Our guide to service page design covers general page structure.

Consultation-led booking

For most medical aesthetic treatments, a consultation before treatment is expected, and in many cases a prescriber needs to assess the patient. The website can reflect this by making consultations the main call to action. That might be:

  • An online booking system for consultations.
  • A consultation request form asking for basic details and the area of concern.
  • Clear information about whether consultations are charged and whether the fee is redeemable.

Avoid designs that let people buy a medical treatment outright before any consultation. A cooling-off period between consultation and treatment is something many practitioners consider good practice, and explaining it on the site can reinforce trust. Our guide to online booking systems covers the general options.

Practitioner credentials

People choosing an aesthetics clinic increasingly look for evidence of who will be treating them. Clear practitioner profiles can include:

  • Name, photo and role.
  • Professional registration, such as with the GMC, NMC, GDC or GPhC, stated accurately.
  • Relevant training and qualifications.
  • Who prescribes, where prescription-only medicines are involved.

Only list registrations, training and memberships that are genuine and current. Linking to public registers allows people to check for themselves. If your clinic is registered with the Care Quality Commission or the equivalent body in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, because it provides regulated activities, stating that accurately can help. Our guide to website trust signals covers other ways to build confidence.

Before and after images

Before and after photos are popular but need particular care. The ASA has ruled against images that were edited, misleadingly lit, or not representative of typical results. Good practice includes:

  • Using genuine photos of your own patients, with written consent for website use.
  • Keeping lighting, angle and setting consistent between before and after shots.
  • Not retouching or filtering images.
  • Making clear that results vary from person to person.
  • Being cautious about images linked to prescription-only medicines, given the advertising restrictions.

Gallery pages should load quickly on mobile, so images need optimising. Our guide to website image optimisation covers the practical side.

Reviews and testimonials

Testimonials on your website can be treated as claims made by the clinic, so reviews that describe results need the same care as your own copy. Reviews that mention a prescription-only medicine by name may also cause problems. Comments about the consultation, the practitioner's care and the clinic environment are generally less risky. Only publish genuine reviews with permission.

Prices and terms

Price transparency helps people plan, and many clinics show starting prices or price ranges for treatments, with final prices confirmed at consultation. Be careful that price presentation does not itself promote a prescription-only medicine. Clear terms on deposits, cancellations and consultation fees, shown before payment, reduce misunderstandings. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout.

Age restrictions and suitability

Some cosmetic procedures, including botulinum toxin and dermal fillers for cosmetic purposes, cannot legally be given to under-18s in England, and the advertising rules restrict targeting cosmetic interventions at this age group. Stating your age policy clearly, and avoiding imagery or language likely to appeal particularly to under-18s, is sensible.

Privacy and health data

Consultation forms often collect health information and photos, which are sensitive personal data under UK GDPR. Collect only what you need, explain how it will be stored and used, and make sure your privacy notice covers it. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for health organisations.

Design and tone

Aesthetics clinics often want an elegant, premium look, which can work well. Clean layouts, restrained colour palettes and good photography of the clinic help. Tone matters as much as visuals: calm, informative copy that respects the reader's decision tends to build more trust than pressure. Our guide to website colour and typography covers visual principles.

Choosing a platform

Most clinic websites are service sites with a booking feature, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all be suitable. If you also sell skincare products, that may influence the choice. FAQs

Can an aesthetics clinic mention botulinum toxin on its website?

Botulinum toxin is a prescription-only medicine, which generally cannot be advertised to the public in the UK. Check current MHRA and ASA guidance, as many clinics focus on consultations for concerns instead.

Are before and after photos allowed?

They can be used if genuine, unedited, consented and representative, with a note that results vary. Extra care is needed where images relate to prescription-only medicines.

Should aesthetics clinics show prices?

Many show starting prices or ranges with final prices confirmed at consultation. Make sure the presentation does not promote a prescription-only medicine.

Should treatments be bookable online without a consultation?

For medical aesthetic treatments, a consultation-led booking process is generally more appropriate, and prescription-only treatments need a prescriber's assessment.

Who regulates aesthetics clinic advertising?

The ASA enforces the CAP Code, the MHRA oversees medicines advertising, and practitioners' own professional regulators set further standards.
