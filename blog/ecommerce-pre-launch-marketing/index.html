Ecommerce Pre-Launch Marketing: Building Interest Before Your Store Opens

How to build interest before a new online store launches, from a coming soon page and email list to SEO groundwork, social content and launch-day planning.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Pre-launch marketing gives a new store an audience on day one rather than starting from nothing.
  • A coming soon page with an email sign-up is often the simplest foundation to build on.
  • SEO, analytics and tracking groundwork done before launch saves rework and lets you measure results from the start.

Opening a new online store can feel like opening a shop on a street nobody walks down. The website is ready, the products are photographed and the checkout works, but there is no one there to buy. Pre-launch marketing is about changing that, so that when the store opens there are already people who know about it and want to visit.

This guide covers practical steps to build interest before launch, the groundwork worth putting in place early and how to plan launch day itself. It is aimed at new brands and businesses moving into ecommerce for the first time.

Why marketing before launch matters

New websites usually take time to build visibility in search engines, and paid advertising works better when you have some data and an audience to build on. Starting marketing before launch means you can:

  • gather an audience of interested people to tell on launch day,
  • test messages, product names and imagery before committing,
  • learn what potential customers care about and adjust accordingly, and
  • give search engines time to discover your site.

It also helps you check that there is genuine interest in what you plan to sell, which is valuable information in its own right.

Start with a clear picture of your customer

Pre-launch marketing works best when it is aimed at specific people. Before creating content or ads, write down who your ideal customer is, what problem your products solve for them, where they spend time online and what would persuade them to try a new brand. This shapes everything else, from the wording on your coming soon page to which social platforms you focus on.

If your brand identity is still taking shape, our guide to brand identity design covers the elements to settle early, and our tone of voice guide helps keep your messaging consistent from the first post.

Create a coming soon page

A coming soon page is a simple holding page on your domain that explains what is on the way and invites visitors to sign up for updates. It gives you somewhere to send people from social media, conversations and any early advertising. A good coming soon page includes:

  • your brand name and logo,
  • a short, clear description of what you will sell and why it is different,
  • one or two strong product images or a glimpse of the range,
  • an email sign-up form with a clear reason to join, and
  • links to your social profiles.

Shopify includes a password page feature that can act as a coming soon page while the store is being built, and WordPress and WooCommerce sites can use a holding page in a similar way. Make sure the page loads quickly and works well on mobile, since many visitors will arrive from social apps.

Build an email list

An email list is one of the most valuable things you can build before launch, because it is an audience you can contact directly. Give people a reason to sign up, such as:

  • being first to know when the store opens,
  • early access to products or limited items,
  • a launch offer for subscribers, or
  • behind-the-scenes updates as the brand comes together.

Only promise what you will actually deliver, and be clear about what people are signing up for. UK rules on electronic marketing generally require appropriate consent before sending marketing emails, so check the guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office and set up your sign-up form with that in mind. Our guide to ecommerce email marketing covers building and using a list in more depth.

Keep subscribers warm

Do not collect sign-ups and then go silent until launch. A short welcome email followed by occasional updates, such as a look at the products, the story behind the brand or a countdown, helps people remember why they signed up.

Lay the SEO groundwork early

Search visibility for a new site tends to build gradually, so starting early helps. Before launch, it is worth:

  • researching the terms customers use to search for products like yours; our keyword research guide explains how,
  • planning a sensible site structure with clear categories,
  • writing original product descriptions rather than copying supplier text; see our guide to ecommerce product descriptions,
  • setting up Google Search Console so you can submit your sitemap once the store is live, and
  • planning a few helpful pages or articles that answer questions your customers ask.

If the store is hidden behind a password or holding page, search engines will not see the full site until it opens. Plan to remove the password and submit your sitemap promptly at launch. Our ecommerce SEO checklist covers the wider set of tasks.

Set up analytics and tracking before launch

It is much easier to set up measurement before launch than to piece together data afterwards. Make sure GA4 is installed and recording ecommerce events, that any advertising platforms you intend to use are connected and that your cookie consent setup is working. Our GA4 setup guide explains the essentials, and our guide to cookie banner design covers consent. Test a few purchases on a staging or test setup to check that orders are recorded correctly.

Use social media to build anticipation

Social platforms let you show the brand coming together and start conversations before there is anything to buy. Ideas include:

  • behind-the-scenes content showing product development, packaging or photography,
  • sneak peeks of products, revealed gradually,
  • asking followers for opinions on colours, names or designs,
  • explaining the story and values behind the brand, and
  • a countdown in the days before launch.

Focus on the platforms where your customers actually spend time rather than trying to be everywhere. Every post should give people an easy route to your coming soon page and email sign-up.

Consider early paid advertising

Small-scale paid social or search advertising before launch can help grow your email list and test which messages and images resonate. Keep budgets modest and goals clear: at this stage you are usually paying for sign-ups and learning rather than sales. Any figures you see from a small test should be treated as early indications rather than firm predictions. Our guides to Meta advertising and TikTok advertising cover those platforms, and our paid media service page explains how we can help.

Reach out to people who can spread the word

Telling the right people about a new brand can help it reach beyond your own followers. Possibilities include contacting relevant publications and bloggers with a genuine story, approaching creators in your niche whose audience matches yours and letting friends, family and existing contacts know so they can share. If you work with creators on paid or gifted content, UK advertising rules require that ads are clearly identified; the Advertising Standards Authority and the Competition and Markets Authority publish guidance on this. Our guide to PR for small businesses covers approaching media.

Prepare the store itself

Marketing a store that is not ready can backfire. Before launch, make sure the essentials are in place:

  • clear delivery and returns information,
  • working order confirmation and shipping emails; see our guide to transactional emails,
  • contact details and an about page that builds trust,
  • a tested checkout on mobile and desktop, and
  • enough stock, or a clear plan for pre-orders.

Our guide to setting up a Shopify store and our staging and testing guide cover these checks in more detail.

Plan launch day

Launch day goes more smoothly with a simple plan. Decide in advance:

  1. the exact time the store will open,
  2. when the launch email will go to subscribers,
  3. which social posts will go out and when,
  4. who will watch for orders, questions and technical problems, and
  5. how you will handle anything going wrong, such as a payment issue or stock running out.

If you are offering a launch discount, make sure it is set up and tested before the store opens, and that the terms are clear. Promotional pricing in the UK is subject to consumer protection rules, so check that any offers are genuine and accurately described.

After launch

Launch is a beginning rather than an end. In the following weeks, follow up with subscribers who did not buy, ask early customers for reviews, look at your analytics to see where visitors came from and what they did, and keep publishing content. Our guide to product reviews covers gathering feedback, and our guide to ecommerce conversion rate optimisation explains how to improve the store once real visitors arrive.

Common mistakes

  • Waiting until launch day to start telling anyone about the store.
  • Collecting email sign-ups and never contacting subscribers until launch.
  • Forgetting to remove the password page or submit the sitemap at launch.
  • Launching without analytics or conversion tracking in place.
  • Promising offers or dates that the business cannot meet.

FAQs

How early should I start marketing a new online store?

There is no fixed rule, but starting several weeks before launch gives time to build an email list, create content and set up tracking.

Do I need a coming soon page?

It is not essential, but a simple page with an email sign-up gives you somewhere to send early interest and helps you gather an audience.

Can a new store rank in Google straight away?

It is unlikely. Search visibility usually builds over time, which is why groundwork before launch and ongoing content afterwards both help.

Should I offer a launch discount?

It can encourage early orders, but it is not essential. If you offer one, make sure the terms are clear and the offer is genuine.

Is paid advertising worth it before launch?

A small budget can help grow your list and test messages. Treat early results as learning rather than firm predictions of future performance.
