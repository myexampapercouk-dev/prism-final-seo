Keyword Cannibalisation: How to Find and Fix Pages Competing for the Same Search

Keyword cannibalisation is when several of your pages target the same search. Learn how UK small businesses can find it, decide what to do and fix it safely.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Cannibalisation means two or more of your own pages chase the same search intent, so neither performs as well as one strong page would.
  • Search Console shows it best: look for one query where the ranking URL keeps swapping between pages.
  • Fix it by merging and redirecting, retargeting one page to a different intent, or clarifying with internal links and canonicals, never by deleting pages blindly.

Keyword cannibalisation happens when two or more pages on your own website target the same search, so search engines have to guess which one to show and often show neither well. The fix is usually to pick one page as the clear answer for that search, then merge, retarget or redirect the others so every page has its own job.

This guide is written for UK small business owners and in-house marketers who manage their own site. It explains what cannibalisation looks like, how to find it with free tools, how to choose the right fix and how to stop it coming back as you publish more content.

What keyword cannibalisation actually means

Every page on a website should answer one main question for one kind of searcher. Problems start when a site grows without a plan. A plumber in Leeds might publish a service page for boiler repairs, then a blog post called boiler repair in Leeds, then a seasonal post about winter boiler breakdowns. All three are trying to rank for almost the same search.

Search engines do not usually penalise this. The issue is dilution. Links, internal links, engagement and relevance are split across several similar pages, and the search engine may rotate between them. You end up with three average pages instead of one page that deserves the top spot.

It is worth being precise, because not every overlap is a problem. Two pages can share words and still serve different intents. A page about boiler installation costs and a page about boiler repair serve different needs, even though both mention boilers. Cannibalisation is about the same intent, not the same words.

Signs your pages may be competing

  • Rankings for an important search jump up and down, and the URL shown changes each time.
  • A blog post ranks for a commercial search where you would rather show your service page.
  • Two or more pages have very similar title tags and opening paragraphs.
  • You have published several posts over the years on the same subject with slightly different titles.
  • Location or service pages were created from a template and differ only by a town name.
  • A page that once performed well dropped after you published a newer, similar page.

None of these signs proves cannibalisation on its own, but two or three together are a strong reason to look closer.

How to find keyword cannibalisation

You do not need paid software to find most cases. Free tools and a spreadsheet go a long way.

Using Search Console

Google Search Console is the most reliable source because it shows real searches and the pages Google actually chose. If you have not set it up yet, our Search Console setup guide walks through it. Then:

  1. Open the Performance report and set the date range to the last three to six months.
  2. Filter by a query you care about, such as your main service plus your town.
  3. Switch to the Pages tab. If several URLs have impressions for that single query, note them.
  4. Compare the dates. If clicks move from one URL to another over time, Google is swapping between them.

Repeat this for your ten to twenty most valuable searches. Our keyword research guide explains how to build that list if you do not have one.

Using a site search and your own content list

Type site:yourdomain.co.uk followed by the topic into Google. The results show which of your pages Google associates with that topic. Then export a list of every page and post title from your website and sort it alphabetically or by topic. Duplicated themes usually stand out quickly. A wider website content audit is the natural next step if the list turns up a lot of overlap.

Choosing the right fix

Once you have a group of competing pages, decide which page should own the search. Usually it is the page with the best mix of relevance, links, traffic and conversions. For commercial searches, it should normally be a service or product page rather than a blog post. Then choose a fix for the others.

Merging and redirecting

Merging is the most common fix. Take the best paragraphs, examples and FAQs from the weaker page and add them to the stronger one, rewriting so it reads as one piece. Then add a permanent 301 redirect from the old URL to the main page. Our guide to website redirects covers how to set these up and test them. Update any internal links that pointed to the old URL so visitors and crawlers go straight to the right page.

Retargeting a page

Sometimes both pages are worth keeping. A post titled how much does a boiler repair cost can stay if it focuses on cost factors and links to the main service page for bookings. Change its title tag, headings and introduction so it clearly answers a different question. Our guide to title tags and meta descriptions helps make that difference obvious in the results.

Using canonicals and internal links

Canonical tags tell search engines which version of near-duplicate pages is the main one. They suit cases like printer-friendly pages, filtered product lists or very similar product variants. Our canonical tags guide explains when they are appropriate. Internal links matter just as much: if most of your links about a topic point at the page you want to rank, you send a clear signal. The internal linking guide shows how to plan this.

Mistakes to avoid when fixing it

  • Deleting pages without redirects. This throws away any links and creates broken pages for visitors.
  • Using noindex as a shortcut. Noindex removes a page from results but does not pass its value anywhere. Merging is usually better.
  • Treating every shared word as a conflict. Pages that serve different intents should stay separate.
  • Making changes all at once. Fix one group, wait a few weeks and check Search Console before moving on, so you can see what worked.
  • Forgetting the sitemap. Remove redirected URLs from your XML sitemap so you are not asking search engines to crawl old addresses.

How to prevent cannibalisation in future

The best prevention is a simple keyword map: a spreadsheet listing each important search and the one page that owns it. Before anyone writes a new page or post, they check the map. If the topic is already owned, they either improve the existing page or pick a distinct angle that targets a different question.

A few habits help:

  • Keep one service page per service, and use blog posts for questions, comparisons and guides that link back to it.
  • Give location pages genuinely local content rather than swapping town names in a template.
  • Review older posts once or twice a year and update rather than republish.
  • Write title tags that make each page's purpose obvious at a glance.

Small sites rarely have severe cannibalisation, but it creeps in as soon as someone starts blogging regularly. Catching it early keeps your strongest pages strong.

When to get help

If you have a handful of pages, you can fix cannibalisation yourself with Search Console and an afternoon of editing. On larger sites, ecommerce stores with many filtered category pages, or sites that have been through a redesign, overlaps can be harder to untangle. That is where a technical review is useful. FAQs

What is keyword cannibalisation?

Keyword cannibalisation is when two or more pages on the same website target the same search intent. Search engines have to choose between them, so ranking signals are split and none of the pages performs as well as a single, focused page would.

Does keyword cannibalisation cause a Google penalty?

No. It is not a penalty. It is a dilution problem that can hold rankings back or make them unstable. Fixing it is about clarity, not about recovering from a punishment.

How do I check for keyword cannibalisation for free?

Use the Performance report in Google Search Console. Filter by a single query and look at the Pages tab. If several of your URLs share impressions or swap over time, they may be competing.

Should I delete pages that compete with each other?

Usually not. Merge the useful content into the stronger page and add a 301 redirect from the weaker one. Deleting without a redirect wastes any links and leaves visitors on a broken page.

Can a blog post and a service page target the same keyword?

They should not target the same intent. Let the service page own the commercial search and use the blog post to answer a related question, linking back to the service page.

How long does it take to see results after fixing cannibalisation?

Search engines need to recrawl the pages and process redirects, which can take a few weeks. The full effect on rankings often takes longer, and SEO results generally show over three to six months.
