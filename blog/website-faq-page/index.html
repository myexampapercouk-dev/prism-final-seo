Website FAQ Page: How to Plan, Write and Structure Useful FAQs

A UK guide to website FAQ pages: when you need one, finding real questions, writing clear answers, structuring the page, FAQ schema and keeping it up to date.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A useful FAQ page answers the questions real customers ask, not questions the business wishes they would ask.
  • Short, direct answers with links to fuller information work better than long, defensive explanations.
  • FAQs belong wherever the question arises, so a central FAQ page should support, not replace, answers on key pages.

An FAQ page sounds simple: list the questions people ask and answer them. In practice, many FAQ pages are either neglected afterthoughts or dumping grounds for information that did not fit anywhere else. A well-planned FAQ page can save customers time, reduce repetitive enquiries and help visitors feel confident about getting in touch or buying.

This guide explains when an FAQ page is worth having, how to find the right questions, how to write answers people can use, and how to structure and maintain the page over time.

Do you need an FAQ page?

Not every website needs a separate FAQ page. If your site is small and each page already answers the obvious questions, a standalone FAQ page may add little. An FAQ page tends to be most useful when:

  • Customers regularly ask the same practical questions about delivery, booking, payment, eligibility or process.
  • You offer several services or products and questions apply across them.
  • Your service involves steps or terms that people often misunderstand.
  • Your team spends noticeable time answering the same enquiries.

A useful test is whether the FAQ page would genuinely help someone, or whether it exists only because other sites have one. If the answers would be better placed on your service, product or contact pages, put them there first.

Finding the real questions

The best FAQ pages are built from questions customers actually ask. Good sources include:

  • Emails, phone calls and messages from prospective customers.
  • Questions raised during sales conversations or consultations.
  • Common reasons for complaints, cancellations or returns.
  • Searches made on your own website.
  • Search queries in Google Search Console that bring people to your site.
  • Questions colleagues are asked repeatedly.

Ask the people who deal with customers day to day. They usually know the top questions without needing to check. Write questions in the words customers use rather than internal jargon. If customers ask "how long does delivery take?", use that rather than "what are your fulfilment timescales?".

Avoid inventing questions to promote yourself, such as "why are you the best choice?". Visitors recognise these as marketing, and they make the rest of the page feel less trustworthy.

Writing answers people can use

A good FAQ answer gets to the point. Start with the direct answer, then add any necessary detail. For example, if the question is "do you deliver to Northern Ireland?", begin with "yes" or "no" before explaining any conditions.

Some principles for clear answers:

  • Answer first, explain second. Put the key fact in the first sentence.
  • Keep it short. Most answers should be a few sentences. If an answer needs several paragraphs, it may deserve its own page, with a short summary and a link in the FAQ.
  • Be specific. Where you can give a clear answer, do so. Vague answers create follow-up questions.
  • Link to detail. Point to the relevant service page, policy or guide for people who want more.
  • Use plain English. Avoid legal or technical language unless it is necessary, and explain it when it is.
  • Stay consistent. Answers must match what your terms, policies and other pages say.

Be careful with legal and policy answers

FAQ pages often touch on refunds, cancellations, guarantees, data protection or regulated services. A friendly summary is helpful, but it should not contradict or oversimplify your formal terms. Where an answer relates to legal rights or obligations, keep it accurate, link to the full policy and avoid promises the business cannot keep. If you are unsure how to phrase something, it may be sensible to check with a legal adviser. A short summary plus a link to the authoritative document is usually safer than restating the whole policy.

Structuring the FAQ page

A list of fifty unsorted questions is hard to use. Grouping and ordering make a large difference.

Group questions by topic

Organise questions into clear sections, such as ordering, delivery, returns, accounts and payments for a shop, or services, process, costs and getting started for a service business. Use headings so visitors can jump to the relevant section.

Put the most common questions first

Within each group, order questions by how often they are asked or how important they are. You might also include a short "most asked" section at the top.

Consider navigation for longer pages

For longer FAQ pages, a short list of section links at the top helps visitors move around. On-page search or filtering can help when there are many questions.

Accordions: useful, with care

Expandable accordions keep long FAQ pages tidy by showing only the questions until a visitor clicks. They work well for scanning, but make sure they are accessible: they should work with a keyboard, announce their state to screen readers and not hide content from people who need it. Our website accessibility guide explains the principles.

FAQs on other pages

A central FAQ page is not the only place for questions. Short FAQ sections on service pages, product pages and landing pages can answer questions at the moment they arise, which may help visitors decide without leaving the page. Keep these focused on the topic of the page, and avoid repeating the whole central FAQ on every page.

For service businesses, our service page design guide discusses how FAQs fit into a service page. For shops, practical questions about delivery and returns are often best answered close to the buy button, with a link to fuller information.

FAQ pages and search

FAQ content can help with search visibility because it answers specific questions in clear language. Search engines and AI assistants increasingly look for concise, well-structured answers, and a well-written FAQ can provide them. Our AI Overviews guide covers how this kind of content is used.

FAQ schema

FAQPage structured data marks up questions and answers so search engines can understand them. Google has changed how and where it displays FAQ rich results over time, and for most sites they are no longer shown in the way they once were. Structured data can still help search engines interpret your content, but it is best treated as a technical nicety rather than a reason to create an FAQ page. Mark up only questions and answers that are visible on the page. Our schema markup guide explains structured data in more detail.

Avoid thin or duplicated FAQs

Copying the same FAQ block across many pages, or writing one-line answers that say nothing, adds little value. Each answer should help the reader.

Signposting the next step

Someone reading your FAQ page is often close to a decision. Make it easy to act: include a clear route to contact you if their question is not answered, and links to relevant services, products or booking pages. A simple line such as "Cannot find your answer? Get in touch" with a link to your contact page can help. Our website contact forms guide covers making that next step straightforward.

Keeping FAQs up to date

Out-of-date FAQs can cause real problems, such as wrong delivery times, old prices or discontinued services. Build a simple review habit:

  • Review the page whenever policies, services or processes change.
  • Set a regular reminder to check every answer for accuracy.
  • Add new questions when the same enquiry starts appearing repeatedly.
  • Remove questions that are no longer relevant.
  • Assign someone to own the page, so it does not drift.

If you track on-site searches or contact form topics, these can show whether the FAQ is answering what people need.

Common FAQ page mistakes

  • Inventing promotional questions that nobody asks.
  • Long, unstructured lists with no grouping.
  • Answers that contradict terms or other pages.
  • Vague answers that create more questions.
  • Hiding important information only in the FAQ instead of on key pages.
  • Letting answers go out of date.
  • Inaccessible accordions that do not work with a keyboard.

A simple FAQ page checklist

  • Questions gathered from real customer contact.
  • Questions written in customers' words.
  • Direct answers in the first sentence.
  • Questions grouped by topic with clear headings.
  • Links to fuller policies, services and guides.
  • Accessible layout and accordions.
  • Clear route to contact for unanswered questions.
  • An owner and a review schedule.

FAQs

How many questions should an FAQ page have?

As many as are genuinely useful. A small business might need only a handful; a larger shop may need more, grouped by topic. Quality matters more than quantity.

Should FAQs go on a separate page or on each page?

Often both. A central page covers general questions, while short, focused FAQ sections on key pages answer questions where they arise.

Does FAQ schema still help?

It can help search engines understand your content, but FAQ rich results are now shown far less widely. Use it for visible questions only, and do not rely on it for visibility.

Can an FAQ page reduce enquiries?

It may reduce repetitive questions if it answers what people genuinely ask, though some customers will always prefer to get in touch directly.

Who should write the FAQ answers?

Ideally someone who deals with customers, with a check from whoever owns the relevant policy or service, so answers are both clear and accurate.
