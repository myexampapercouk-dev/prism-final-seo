Ecommerce Category Page Design: Layout, Filters and SEO

How to design ecommerce category pages that help shoppers find products: layout, filters, sorting, product cards, intro copy and SEO considerations.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Category pages are where most shoppers narrow down their choice, so they deserve as much care as product pages.
  • Useful filters, honest sorting and consistent product cards do more for conversion than decoration.
  • Category pages can rank well for broad searches, but filter combinations need managing so they do not create endless duplicate URLs.

An ecommerce category page (called a collection on Shopify) lists a group of related products and helps shoppers narrow them down. It sits between broad browsing and a specific product, which makes it one of the most important templates on any online shop. It is also often the page that ranks for broad, commercial searches such as "women's waterproof jackets".

What shoppers need from a category page

  • Confirmation they are in the right place.
  • A quick sense of the range and price spread.
  • Ways to cut the list down to what suits them.
  • Enough information on each product card to decide what to click.

Design choices should be judged against those four needs.

The top of the page

Start with a clear heading that matches the category name in your navigation. A short introduction of a sentence or two can help shoppers and search engines understand the range, but keep it brief so products appear quickly, particularly on mobile. Large banner images that push products below the fold can look attractive yet slow shoppers down; if you use one, keep it slim.

Sub-category links near the top, shown as text links or small image tiles, help people jump to a narrower range without using filters.

Filters

Good filtering is often the difference between a usable and an unusable category page. Choose filters based on how your customers actually decide:

Store typeFilters that often matter
ClothingSize, colour, fit, price, material
FurnitureDimensions, material, colour, delivery time
ElectronicsBrand, key specifications, compatibility, price
Food and drinkDietary needs, flavour, pack size, price
  • Show how many products each option will return, and hide or grey out options that return none.
  • Let people combine several filters and see which are applied, with an easy way to clear them.
  • On mobile, put filters behind a clearly labelled button and keep the product count visible.
  • Filters rely on clean product data. Inconsistent sizes or colours in your catalogue will produce messy filters.

Sorting

Offer the sort options shoppers expect, such as price low to high, price high to low and newest. A default such as "featured" or "best selling" is fine, but make sure it genuinely reflects what is popular or relevant rather than simply what you want to clear.

Product cards

Each card should make comparison easy. Typically that means a consistent image, product name, price, and any key variant information such as available colours. Optional extras include review ratings where you have genuine reviews, stock messages and a quick-add button for simple products. Keep image ratios consistent so the grid looks tidy, and make sure sale prices and badges are clear without being shouty.

Pagination and loading more

Large categories need a way to show more products. Numbered pagination, a "load more" button and infinite scroll each have trade-offs. Infinite scroll can make footers unreachable and makes it harder to return to a position, while pagination and "load more" give shoppers more control. Whatever you choose, make sure products beyond the first page can still be reached through normal links so search engines can find them.

Category pages and SEO

  • Target broad terms. Category pages often suit broader searches than individual products. Match the heading and title tag to how people search.
  • Add useful copy. A short intro at the top and, where helpful, more detailed buying guidance lower down. Write for shoppers, not just keywords.
  • Manage filter URLs. Filters can create huge numbers of near-duplicate URLs. Decide which combinations deserve their own indexable pages and use canonical tags or other controls for the rest.
  • Link sensibly. Link to related categories and popular sub-categories.

Empty and thin categories

Categories with only one or two products, or none, give a poor impression. Merge small categories, hide empty ones, or populate them before linking to them. If a seasonal category is temporarily empty, explain when products are expected and suggest alternatives rather than showing a blank page.

Measuring performance

In your analytics, look at how many category visitors click through to a product, which filters are used, and whether some categories have unusually high exit rates. Site search terms can reveal categories or filters shoppers expect but cannot find.

Merchandising the category

The order in which products appear is a quiet but powerful decision. Shoppers tend to look closely at the first rows and pay less attention further down. Options include ranking by sales, by margin, by newness, or by a manual order you control. Many shops use a mix: automatic sorting by default with a few products pinned to the top for seasonal reasons. Whatever you choose, keep out-of-stock items from dominating the first rows, as a grid full of unavailable products is discouraging. Some shops move them to the end of the list or hide them, while others keep them visible with a clear stock message so shoppers can sign up for alerts.

Category pages on mobile

Most category browsing happens on phones for many retailers, so the mobile layout deserves particular attention. Two products per row is common on small screens, giving enough room for an image, name and price. Keep filter and sort controls together in a bar near the top, and consider making that bar stick as people scroll so they can refine their search without scrolling back up. When filters are applied, update the results without jumping the shopper back to the top of the page unexpectedly, and make it obvious how many results remain.

Planning your category structure

Good page design cannot fully rescue a confusing category structure. Before designing the template, check that categories are organised around how customers shop rather than how stock is managed in the warehouse. A few questions help:

  • Would a customer know which category to look in for a given product?
  • Are there categories with very few products that could be merged?
  • Are there very large categories that would be easier to browse if split?
  • Do customers search for things, such as "gifts for runners", that could justify a curated category?

Promotional and seasonal content

Category pages are a natural place for timely messages such as a sale, free delivery threshold or seasonal range. Keep these compact so they do not push products down, and remove them promptly when they end. If you run regular seasonal categories, such as Christmas gifts, keeping the same URL from year to year and updating its content tends to work better than creating a new page each time, because the page can build up visibility over time.

Speed matters

Category pages load many images at once, so they can be slow if images are not optimised. Use appropriately sized thumbnails rather than full product images, lazy-load images further down the page, and keep third-party apps that inject content into the grid to a minimum.

FAQs

How many products should a category page show?

Enough to give a sense of choice without making the page slow. Many shops show a few rows on load and let shoppers load more. Test what works for your range.

Should category pages have text on them?

A short introduction usually helps both shoppers and search engines. Longer buying guidance can go below the products so it does not get in the way.

Should every filter combination be indexed?

Usually not. Index combinations that match real searches and have enough products, and keep the rest out of the index to avoid duplicate pages.

Is infinite scroll bad for SEO?

Not inherently, but it needs a crawlable fallback, such as paginated URLs, so search engines can reach every product.

What is the difference between a category and a collection?

Mostly naming. Shopify calls them collections; many other platforms call them categories. Both group related products on one page.

Should out-of-stock products appear on category pages?

It depends on how quickly they return. Products coming back soon can stay visible with a clear stock message and an alert option, ideally lower down the grid. Discontinued items are usually better removed, with their URLs redirected to a close alternative.

How often should I review category pages?

Check key categories whenever your range changes significantly, and review filters, sorting and intro copy at least a few times a year. Seasonal categories need attention before each peak.
