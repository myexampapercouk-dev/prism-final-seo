How to Choose a Web Designer in the UK: The Questions Worth Asking

How to choose a web designer in the UK: what to prepare, how to judge portfolios and proposals, the questions to ask and the warning signs to look out for.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Prepare a short brief before you approach anyone, so you can compare proposals on a like-for-like basis.
  • Judge designers on relevant work, clarity of process, who owns what at the end and what happens after launch, not on visuals alone.
  • Ask specific questions about scope, content, platform, SEO, ownership and support, and get the answers in writing.

Choosing a web designer is a decision you live with for a long time. The person or team you pick shapes how your business looks online, how easily customers can get in touch or buy, and how simple the site is to manage once it is live. Yet many businesses choose based on a nice portfolio and a price, then discover later that important things were never discussed. This guide sets out how to approach the decision, what to prepare, what to look for and the questions worth asking before you sign anything.

It applies whether you are speaking to freelancers, small studios or larger agencies, and whether you need a brochure site or an online shop.

Start with your own brief

Before you contact anyone, write down what you need. It does not need to be long or technical. A one or two page brief helps designers give you accurate proposals and makes it much easier to compare them.

  • Your business and audience. What you do, who your customers are and what they need from your site.
  • The main goal. Enquiries, bookings, online sales, credibility, recruitment or something else.
  • Pages and features. A rough list, such as services, about, contact, blog, booking or shop.
  • Content. Whether you have copy and photography ready or need help creating them.
  • Platform preferences. Any existing platform or a preference for WordPress, Shopify, WooCommerce, Squarespace or Wix.
  • Budget range and timing. Even a rough range helps designers suggest an appropriate approach.
  • Sites you like. Examples of what you like and dislike, and why.

Freelancer, studio or agency?

Web designers come in different shapes. A freelancer may offer a direct relationship and focused attention. A studio or agency may bring a wider range of skills, such as strategy, copywriting, SEO and development, under one roof. Neither is automatically better. What matters is whether the people involved have the skills your project needs and the capacity to deliver and support it. Ask any provider who will actually do the work and what happens if they are unavailable.

How to judge a portfolio

A portfolio shows taste and capability, but it needs to be read carefully.

  • Look at live sites, not just screenshots. Visit them on your phone. Are they quick, easy to use and clear?
  • Look for relevance. Has the designer built sites with similar features, such as ecommerce, bookings or lots of content?
  • Look for variety. Do the sites look tailored to each business, or do they all look the same?
  • Ask what they did. Did they design, build, write copy, or all three? Portfolios do not always make roles clear.
  • Ask what happened next. A site that looks good but cannot be updated by its owner may not be a success.

The questions to ask a web designer

About scope and process

  • What exactly is included in your proposal, and what is not?
  • What are the stages of the project, and what do you need from us at each stage?
  • How many design concepts and revision rounds are included?
  • How do you handle changes or extra requests during the project?
  • Who will we be dealing with day to day?

About content

  • Who writes the copy? If we write it, will you advise on structure and length?
  • Who sources photography and images, and who holds the rights to them?
  • Who loads content into the site?

Content is easy to overlook in a proposal and can affect both cost and schedule. Our guide to website copywriting explains what good web copy involves.

About the platform

  • Which platform do you recommend for us, and why?
  • Will we be able to edit pages, add blog posts or update products ourselves?
  • Will you use a theme, a page builder or custom code? What does that mean for future changes?
  • What ongoing costs come with the platform, such as hosting, subscriptions, apps or plugins?

If you are weighing platforms, our comparisons of Shopify vs WooCommerce and Squarespace vs WordPress may help.

About search and performance

  • How do you handle basic on-page SEO, such as titles, headings and descriptions?
  • If we are replacing a site, how will you protect existing search visibility and set up redirects?
  • How do you approach site speed and mobile usability?
  • How do you consider accessibility?

Our guides to website speed optimisation and website accessibility explain why these matter.

About ownership

  • Who will own the domain, hosting account and platform account?
  • Will we have full admin access from the start?
  • Who owns the design, code and content once the project is paid for?
  • What happens if we want to move to another provider later?

Ownership is one of the most important and least discussed topics. Agree it in writing before the project starts; our guide to business domain and email setup covers the domain side.

About after launch

  • What support is included after launch, and for how long?
  • Do you offer ongoing maintenance, and what does it cover?
  • Will you provide training or guidance on managing the site?
  • How do you handle security updates and backups?

Our website maintenance guide covers what ongoing care can involve.

Ask who will look after search once the site launches. A web designer and an SEO agency do different jobs, and redirects, page structure and content need both.

Comparing proposals fairly

Proposals can look very different, which makes price comparisons misleading. A lower price may leave out copywriting, content loading, SEO setup or post-launch support. A higher price may include strategy work or custom features you do not need. To compare fairly, line up each proposal against your brief.

Making sense of the first conversation

The initial call or meeting is a chance for both sides to test fit. Pay attention to how the designer listens. Do they ask about your customers, your goals and how you currently win business, or do they move straight to colours and layouts? Do they explain options in plain English, or rely on jargon? Do they push back sensibly when an idea might not serve your goals? A good designer should be curious about your business and honest about trade-offs, including telling you when something you want may add cost or time without much benefit.

It is also worth noticing how organised the follow-up is. A clear written summary or proposal that reflects what you discussed is a positive sign. If the proposal looks generic or misses points you raised, consider whether the same will happen during the project.

Warning signs to watch for

  • No questions about your business. A designer who quotes without understanding your goals is guessing.
  • Vague proposals. If you cannot tell what is included, disputes are more likely later.
  • The designer keeps the domain or accounts in their own name. This can make it hard to leave.
  • Guaranteed rankings. No one can guarantee positions in Google.
  • No mention of testing or launch steps. These are essential, not optional extras.
  • Pressure to sign quickly. A reasonable provider will give you time to consider.
  • No written agreement. Even a simple contract protects both sides. Our article on marketing agency contracts covers terms worth understanding.

Getting the relationship off to a good start

Once you have chosen, agree how you will communicate, who on your side gives feedback and how decisions get recorded. A short kick-off that confirms scope, responsibilities and the first milestones can prevent misunderstandings. FAQs

How many web designers should I speak to?

Speaking to a small number gives you a sense of different approaches without becoming overwhelming. What matters more is giving each the same brief so you can compare like for like.

Should I choose a local web designer?

Not necessarily. Many designers work remotely with clients across the UK. Choose on skills, relevance, communication and support rather than location, unless meeting in person matters to you.

Is the cheapest quote a bad sign?

Not automatically, but check what it leaves out. Content, SEO setup, testing and support are common differences between proposals.

Should I ask for references?

It is reasonable to ask whether you can speak to a past client, particularly for larger projects. Also look at live sites the designer has built.

What should be in the contract?

Scope, deliverables, revisions, payment terms, ownership of accounts and work, support arrangements and what happens if either side wants to end the project. For anything you are unsure about, take independent legal advice.
