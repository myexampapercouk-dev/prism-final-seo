How to Choose a Marketing Agency for a School or College

How to choose a marketing agency for a school or college: what to ask, safeguarding and data checks, procurement points and warning signs to look out for.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start with your enrolment or recruitment goal and your internal capacity, then look for an agency whose skills fit that brief.
  • Ask how an agency handles safeguarding, pupil images and personal data, since these matter more in education than in most sectors.
  • Check who will do the work, how results are reported and what you own if you part ways, and be wary of guaranteed outcomes.

To choose a marketing agency for a school or college, first set out what you need to achieve and who needs to be persuaded, then look for an agency that understands how families and students make education decisions, handles pupil images and personal data with care, reports honestly and fits your procurement rules. Sector knowledge helps, but a clear brief and good questions will do more than any polished pitch to help you tell which agency is right.

This guide is for headteachers, principals, bursars, business managers and marketing leads at UK schools, colleges and trusts. It gives you a practical route from brief to appointment. For a broader look at what specialists offer, see our guide to education marketing agencies in the UK, and for general principles that apply to any sector, our guide on how to choose a digital marketing agency.

Why education is different

Choosing a school or college is a big, emotional and often slow decision. It may involve parents, carers, young people and sometimes employers, each wanting different things. The rhythm of the year matters too, with open events, application deadlines and admission rounds shaping when activity is useful.

Your institution type changes the job as well:

  • Independent schools may need to reach families across a wider area and explain value and fees sensitively.
  • State schools and academies may be focused on local reputation, enrolment numbers and staff recruitment, and their admissions must follow the rules that apply to them.
  • Further education colleges may speak to school leavers, adult learners and employers at once, with several courses and funding routes to explain.
  • Trusts may have to balance a shared identity with the character of each school.

An agency need not have worked with your exact type of institution, but it should ask about these differences early. One that treats you like an online shop is unlikely to be the right fit.

Define what you need before you search

You will get better proposals, and find them easier to compare, if you write a short brief first. It does not need to be elaborate. Try to cover:

  • The goal. For example, filling places in a certain year group, growing applications for particular courses or recruiting staff.
  • The audience. Who decides, who influences the decision and what they need to know.
  • The current picture. Your website, social channels, prospectus, events and any past campaigns, with what you know about how each performed.
  • Key dates. Open events, application deadlines and any governance meetings where decisions are needed.
  • Budget and how it is funded. Even a range will help agencies suggest something realistic.
  • Who will work with the agency. Who supplies information, who signs off and how much time they really have.

Be honest about your capacity. If one busy staff member looks after marketing on top of other duties, an agency that relies on frequent approvals and large quantities of supplied content may struggle. Our guide to school marketing can help you think through which activities suit your situation before you approach anyone.

What to look for in an agency

Understanding of the decision journey

Ask how the agency would map the route from first awareness to application or enrolment. A good answer should mention research with current families or students, the role of open events and word of mouth, and what happens after someone enquires. Advertising alone rarely solves an admissions challenge if the follow-up is weak.

Skills that match your brief

Some agencies are strongest in brand and creative work, others in search and paid media, and others in websites. Match this to your need. If you want an updated site, look at their approach to school website design, including accessibility and how easily your staff can make updates. If you need families to find you for local searches, explore their search experience. If you need a whole programme, ask how the different parts would be coordinated.

Honest reporting

Ask to see an example report, with confidential details removed. Look for measures linked to your goals, such as enquiries, open event bookings or applications, and not only impressions or followers. Many influences affect enrolment, so a careful agency will be open about what can and cannot be attributed to its work.

Tone and values

Education marketing relies on trust. Read the agency's sample work and ask yourself whether it would feel right to your families and staff. Marketing should reflect the institution as it really is. Exaggeration can cause problems later, when a family's experience does not match the promise.

Safeguarding, images and data

This is where education differs most from other sectors, and it is reasonable to raise it early. You remain responsible for your safeguarding and data protection duties, so any agency must be able to work within your policies.

Questions worth asking include:

  • How do you handle photography and video of pupils, including consent and any children who must not be identified?
  • If your team visits our site, what arrangements do you make, and will you follow our safeguarding and visitor procedures?
  • How do you store images and personal data, who has access and how long do you keep them?
  • Will you act as a data processor for us, and will you sign an appropriate agreement?
  • How do you make sure that names and identifying details are not placed alongside pupil images unless this has been agreed?
  • How would you target adverts without using inappropriate data about children?

Rules on data protection and on marketing to young people can be complex, and they depend on your circumstances. Guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office, the Department for Education and the advertising regulators is a useful starting point, and your data protection officer or legal adviser should review anything significant. Treat an agency that seems unconcerned by these questions as a warning sign.

Claims and accuracy

Schools and colleges often want to promote exam results, inspection outcomes and destinations. These can be strong messages, but they must be accurate, up to date and fairly presented. Ask how the agency checks claims and who signs them off. Agree a simple rule: nothing goes out unless a named person at the institution has confirmed it.

The same applies to fees, funding, bursaries and admissions criteria. Anything misleading could harm your reputation and may create regulatory problems. Where admissions or funding rules apply, check the current requirements for your institution type rather than relying on an agency's general knowledge.

Procurement and governance

Publicly funded institutions in particular may have to follow procurement rules, financial regulations or trust policies. Find out what applies to you before you speak to suppliers. It may affect how many quotes you need, who has to approve the spend and how the work should be advertised. Your business manager, trust finance lead or an adviser can confirm this.

Whatever your funding, it helps to agree:

  • Who has authority to choose the agency and approve its work.
  • How you will compare proposals, ideally against criteria that you set in advance.
  • What you will tell governors or trustees, and when.
  • How you will show that the spending offered value for money.

A simple scoring table can be helpful here:

CriterionWhat to look for
Fit with briefProposal answers your stated goal and audience
Relevant experienceEducation work, or clear evidence of transferable skills
Safeguarding and dataClear, confident answers and willingness to follow your policies
Team and workloadNamed people, sensible demands on your staff
ReportingMeasures linked to your goals, plainly explained
Cost and termsCosts are clear, including extras and notice periods

Questions to ask at the pitch

  1. Who will actually work on our account, and will we meet them?
  2. What do you need from us each month, and how much staff time will that take?
  3. Can you show relevant work and tell us what you learned from it, including what did not go to plan?
  4. How would you plan around our admissions calendar and school holidays?
  5. What would you advise us not to spend money on, and why?
  6. Who will own the website, advertising accounts, images, domain names and content if we end the contract?
  7. What are the notice terms, and what would a handover look like?

Pay attention to the questions the agency asks you, too. Curiosity about your community, your competitors and the reasons families choose you is a good sign.

Warning signs

  • Guaranteed outcomes. No agency can promise a specific number of pupils or top search positions.
  • Generic proposals. If the document could be for any organisation, it has probably not been thought through for yours.
  • Vague reporting. Lots of activity with no clear link to your goals is hard to judge.
  • Accounts held by the agency. You should normally own, or at least retain full access to, your advertising and analytics accounts.
  • Reluctance to discuss safeguarding. It should be a straightforward conversation.
  • Testimonials you cannot verify. Ask to speak with a past or current client if you can.

FAQs

Does the agency need to have education experience?

It helps, particularly for understanding admissions cycles and safeguarding. However, strong skills and a real willingness to learn your context can matter as much. Ask how they would close any gaps in their sector knowledge.

Should a school hire an agency or do marketing in-house?

Many institutions use a mix. Staff know the community and can capture daily stories, while an agency can add specialist skills in areas such as search, paid media or web development. The right balance depends on your budget and capacity.

Can a multi-academy trust use one agency for all its schools?

It can, subject to its procurement rules. Ask how the agency would balance shared resources with the identity of each school, and how it would report across the group.

What should a contract include?

It would normally set out the scope, fees, notice period, ownership of work and accounts, data protection responsibilities and safeguarding expectations. Have it reviewed by whoever advises your institution on contracts.

When should we appoint an agency?

Work backwards from your key admissions dates, and allow time for your procurement process and for the agency to get to know you. It is usually better not to leave the appointment until just before your main recruitment period.

Next steps

Write your short brief, confirm your procurement rules and draw up your questions before you speak to anyone. That preparation will make it far easier to compare agencies on fair terms.
