Restaurant Website Design: Menus, Bookings and Getting Guests Through the Door

Website design for UK restaurants: readable menus, table bookings, allergen information, opening hours, private dining pages and a site that works on a phone.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most restaurant visitors want a small number of things fast: the menu, the location, the opening hours and a way to book, so put those first.
  • Menus published as text pages, rather than only as downloadable files, are usually easier to read on a phone and easier for search engines to understand.
  • Allergen information, prices and booking terms need to be accurate and kept current, so check the rules that apply to your business and review them regularly.

A restaurant website has an unusual job. Most people who visit already have a rough idea that they might eat with you. They have seen a friend's photo, walked past the window or found you on a map. The website is where they confirm the decision: is the menu right, is it open tonight, can I get a table, and how do I get there? A site that answers those questions in a few taps can turn interest into a booking. One that hides the menu in a slow file or buries the booking button can quietly send people elsewhere.

This guide covers website design for UK restaurants, from small independents to multi-site groups. It focuses on the website itself. For promotion, such as social media, local search and reviews, see our guide to restaurant marketing. If you run a cafe or coffee shop, our guide to cafe website design covers the daytime, walk-in side of hospitality in more detail.

What diners look for

Visitors to a restaurant site usually want answers to a short list of questions:

  • What kind of food is it, and what does the menu look like?
  • Roughly how much will a meal cost?
  • Is it open when I want to go?
  • Can I book, and how?
  • Where is it, and is there parking or good transport nearby?
  • Can it cater for my dietary needs, children or a group?

Design the homepage around those questions. A strong image, a one-line description of the food, the opening hours and a clear "Book a table" button often do more than long paragraphs about the founding story. That story still has a place, but lower down or on an about page. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise content, and our guide to hero section design covers the top of the page.

Menus that work on a phone

The menu is often the most visited page on a restaurant site. Many restaurants upload a PDF of their printed menu, which is quick but has drawbacks: it can be slow to open on mobile data, hard to zoom, and less easy for search engines to read. A menu built as a normal web page, with headings for each section and dishes listed as text, is usually easier for diners to use.

Some practical points:

  • Show prices clearly, and keep them in step with the printed menu.
  • Use short, appetising descriptions rather than long ones.
  • Separate menus for lunch, dinner, set menus, drinks and children if you have them.
  • Mark dietary options, such as vegetarian or vegan, consistently, and explain any symbols.
  • Show a date or "last updated" note if the menu changes often.

If you still want a printable version, offer the PDF as an extra rather than the only option. For seasonal menus that change frequently, make sure whoever updates the menu can do so easily in the content management system without needing a developer.

Allergen and dietary information

UK food businesses have legal duties around allergen information, and the specific requirements depend on how food is sold. For example, the rules for food served in a restaurant differ from those for prepacked food or food sold for delivery. The Food Standards Agency publishes guidance on what applies in each case, and you should check its current guidance, or take advice, rather than relying on a website template.

On the website, a sensible approach is usually to explain how guests can get allergen information, invite them to tell staff about any allergies when booking and ordering, and avoid claims you cannot guarantee, such as describing a dish as completely free from an allergen if your kitchen cannot ensure that. If you publish allergen details online, keep them accurate and in step with your recipes, because an out-of-date list can be worse than none.

Table bookings

For most sit-down restaurants, the booking route is the most important action on the site. Options include:

  • An embedded online booking widget from a reservations system, showing live availability.
  • A link out to a booking page hosted by your reservations provider.
  • A simple enquiry form for smaller venues that prefer to confirm by phone or email.
  • A clickable phone number for people who prefer to call.

An embedded widget can keep people on your site, but test it carefully on a phone, because some widgets are slow or awkward on small screens. Whatever you choose, place the booking button in the header so it is visible on every page, and explain any deposit, cancellation or no-show policy plainly before people book. Booking terms and any charges should be clear and fair, so check current consumer guidance or take advice if you are unsure. Our guide to online booking systems compares approaches.

Opening hours, location and getting there

Out-of-date opening hours are a common frustration for diners. Show your hours in plain text on the homepage or in the footer, update them for bank holidays and seasonal changes, and keep them consistent with your Google Business Profile. A contact or "Find us" page can include:

  • The full address and a map.
  • Parking options and nearby public transport.
  • Step-free access and accessible toilet information, described accurately.
  • A phone number that is answered during service, or a note on the best time to call.

Our guides to Google Business Profile and footer design cover keeping these details consistent.

Photography that sells the experience

People choose restaurants partly on how the food and room look. Good photography of real dishes, the dining room and the atmosphere usually does more than stock images, which diners can often spot. If you use photos of dishes, make sure they reasonably reflect what is served. Large images can slow a site down, so compress and size them properly. See our guide to image optimisation.

A short video of the kitchen or the room can work well, but keep it light and avoid autoplaying sound. Our guide to website video covers the trade-offs.

Private dining, groups and events

If you take group bookings, private hire or events, these often deserve their own page rather than a line on the contact page. A useful private dining page might explain:

  • The spaces available and approximate capacities.
  • Set menus or options for groups.
  • How minimum spends, deposits or hire fees work, if you use them.
  • How to enquire, with a form that asks for date, numbers and occasion.

Describe only what you actually offer. If the arrangements vary, it is fine to say that details depend on the date and size of the group and invite an enquiry. Our guide to contact forms covers keeping enquiry forms short.

Gift vouchers, takeaway and online ordering

Some restaurants sell gift vouchers, takeaway or meal kits through the website. This can be done with an ecommerce platform such as Shopify or WooCommerce, or through a dedicated ordering system. Before adding these, consider who will manage orders and fulfilment, how vouchers are redeemed and tracked, and what terms apply, such as expiry dates. Food sold for delivery or collection can carry different allergen information requirements from food served in the restaurant, so check the current Food Standards Agency guidance. Our guide to ecommerce website design covers online selling more broadly.

Multi-site restaurants

Groups with more than one restaurant need a structure that makes it easy to choose a location. Typically, each site gets its own page with its own address, hours, menu (if it differs), booking link and photos. A location finder or simple list on the homepage helps visitors pick the right one. Separate location pages can also help with local search. Our guide to multi-location SEO explains how.

Speed and mobile use

Many diners look up restaurants on a phone, often while out or deciding at short notice. A restaurant site should load quickly on mobile data, have tappable phone and booking buttons, and avoid pop-ups that cover the menu. Our guides to mobile website design and website speed cover the essentials. If you use a newsletter sign-up pop-up, make sure it is easy to close and does not block key information; see our guide to pop-ups done well.

Accessibility

An accessible site helps every visitor, including people using screen readers or larger text. Menu text rather than images, sufficient colour contrast, descriptive alt text and buttons that are easy to tap all matter. Our guide to website accessibility covers the basics.

Choosing a platform

Many restaurant sites are built on WordPress or hosted builders such as Squarespace or Wix. Builders can be quick to set up and suit simple sites; WordPress usually offers more flexibility for multiple menus, locations and integrations. Shopify can make sense if online sales are a large part of the business. The deciding factors are usually who will update menus and hours, which booking system you use, and whether you sell online. See our guide to website builders vs custom websites.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A menu available only as a large PDF.
  • Opening hours that differ between the website and Google.
  • A booking button that is hard to find on a phone.
  • Allergen statements that are out of date or overstated.
  • Slow-loading image galleries and autoplaying videos.
  • No clear information about private dining or group bookings.

FAQs

Should a restaurant menu be a PDF or a web page?

A web page is usually easier to read on a phone and easier for search engines to understand. You can still offer a PDF as an optional download.

Do restaurants need allergen information on their website?

Requirements depend on how food is sold. Check current Food Standards Agency guidance and make sure whatever you publish is accurate and up to date.

What is the best way to take table bookings online?

An embedded booking widget or a clear link to your reservations system usually works well, alongside a clickable phone number. Test it on a phone.

Should a restaurant site show prices?

Most diners expect to see prices on the menu. Keep them in step with the printed menu so there are no surprises.

How should a restaurant group structure its website?

Give each location its own page with address, hours, menu and booking link, and make choosing a location easy from the homepage.
