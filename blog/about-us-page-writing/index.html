How to Write an About Us Page That Builds Trust and Encourages Enquiries

How to write an about us page for a UK business website: what to include, how to structure it, tone, photos and calls to action, plus common mistakes to avoid.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good about page is really about your customer: why you are the right people to help them.
  • Honest specifics, such as how you work, who you serve and real photos, are more persuasive than generic mission statements.
  • End with a clear next step so interested visitors know what to do.

The about page is often an afterthought on business websites: a few lines of history, a vague mission statement and a stock photo. Yet visitors who click "About" are usually weighing you up. They want to know who is behind the business, whether you understand their needs and whether you seem trustworthy. A well-written about page answers those questions and moves them closer to getting in touch.

This guide explains what to include on an about us page, how to structure it and how to write it in a way that feels genuine.

What is an about page for?

An about page has three main jobs:

  • Build trust by showing there are real people and a real business behind the site.
  • Show fit by explaining who you help and how you work, so the right visitors recognise themselves.
  • Encourage action by guiding interested visitors to the next step.

Keep these in mind and it becomes easier to decide what belongs on the page and what does not.

Write for the reader, not about yourself

It sounds contradictory, but the best about pages focus on the customer. Instead of opening with when the business was founded, open with who you help and the problem you solve. Then use your story, values and people as evidence for why you are well placed to help.

A simple test: for each paragraph, ask "why would a customer care about this?" If there is no good answer, cut it or reframe it.

What to include

Not every section suits every business, but most about pages benefit from some of the following:

  • A clear opening statement summarising what you do, for whom and where.
  • Your story, briefly: why the business exists and what drives it. Keep it relevant.
  • How you work: your approach and what customers can expect, described honestly.
  • Who you work with: the types of customers or sectors you serve.
  • Your people, if appropriate: names, roles and real photos of the team, with their agreement.
  • Values expressed through specifics rather than buzzwords.
  • Proof: genuine reviews, credentials you hold or links to case studies, where you have them.
  • A call to action pointing to your services or contact page.

Be specific and honest

Phrases like "passionate about excellence" or "customer-focused solutions" appear on countless websites and tell the reader very little. Specifics are more convincing. Rather than saying you value communication, explain how you keep customers updated. Rather than claiming to be the best, describe what you actually do well.

Avoid inflating your size or experience. If you are a small team or work remotely, say so plainly and explain how that suits customers. Never invent awards, team members, client names or results. Visitors increasingly check claims, and being found out can undo any trust the page has built.

Find the right tone

Your about page should sound like your business. A law firm might be measured and reassuring; a creative studio might be more relaxed. Whatever the tone, write in plain English, use "we" and "you" naturally and read it aloud to check it sounds human. If you have defined a tone of voice, apply it here. Our tone of voice guide explains how.

Use real photos

Real photos of your team, premises or work are one of the quickest ways to make an about page feel genuine. They do not need to be elaborate, but they should be well lit and reasonably consistent. Generic stock images of people shaking hands rarely add anything. Add descriptive alt text and keep image files a sensible size so the page stays fast; our website speed guide explains why that matters.

Structure the page for scanning

Many visitors skim. Help them by using clear headings, short paragraphs and a logical order. A common structure is:

  1. Headline and short summary of who you help.
  2. Your approach or what makes you different.
  3. Your story and values.
  4. Team, if relevant.
  5. Proof, such as reviews or credentials.
  6. Call to action.

This is a starting point rather than a rule; adapt it to what your visitors most want to know.

Do not forget SEO

About pages are not usually major search landing pages, but they still matter. Give the page a descriptive title and meta description, mention what you do and the areas you serve naturally, and link to your key service pages. Clear, consistent information about your business also helps search engines and AI tools understand who you are. Our guide to generative engine optimisation explains why consistency matters.

Writing your story without rambling

Every business has a story, but not every detail of it belongs on the website. A useful approach is to pick the parts that explain why you do things the way you do. Perhaps you started the business because you saw customers being poorly served, or because you wanted to specialise in a particular area. Those details help visitors understand your motivation and approach.

Keep it short. A few sentences or a couple of short paragraphs is usually enough. Leave out long lists of dates, every previous job or internal milestones that matter more to you than to customers. If you have a longer history worth telling, consider a separate page and link to it.

Turning values into something meaningful

Many about pages list values such as honesty, quality and integrity. Few customers would expect a business to claim the opposite, so these words alone rarely persuade. To make values meaningful, connect each one to something concrete:

  • If you value transparency, explain how you share pricing or progress with customers.
  • If you value quality, describe the checks or standards you apply.
  • If you value sustainability, explain the specific choices you make, and avoid vague environmental claims you cannot support.

Two or three values explained well are usually more convincing than a long list of single words.

Featuring your team

Showing the people behind a business can make it feel more approachable, especially for service businesses where customers will deal with individuals directly. If you include a team section, consider:

  • Getting each person's agreement before publishing their name, photo or biography.
  • Keeping biographies short and relevant to how each person helps customers.
  • Using photos taken in a consistent style, so the section looks cohesive.
  • Updating the section promptly when people join or leave.

Not every business needs a team section. A sole trader might write in the first person, while some organisations prefer to keep the focus on the business. Choose what feels honest and comfortable.

Linking the about page to the rest of your site

The about page should not be a dead end. Link from it to your main services, relevant examples of work and your contact page. Equally, link to the about page from places where visitors are likely to want reassurance, such as the footer and service pages. This helps visitors move naturally from "who are these people?" to "how can they help me?".

Reviewing and updating the page

An about page that describes services you no longer offer or people who have left can quietly undermine trust. Set a reminder to review it periodically, and whenever something significant changes in the business. Check that facts are accurate, photos are current and links still work.

Common mistakes

  • Opening with company history no one asked for.
  • Relying on generic buzzwords instead of specifics.
  • Using stock photos instead of real ones.
  • Exaggerating size, experience or credentials.
  • Ending without a clear next step.
  • Never updating the page as the business changes.

FAQs

How long should an about us page be?

Long enough to answer the questions visitors have about who you are and how you work. For many small businesses, a few well-structured sections is plenty.

Should I include team photos on my about page?

Where appropriate, yes. Real photos help visitors connect with the people behind the business. Always get team members' agreement first.

Should an about page be written in first or third person?

Most businesses use "we" and "you", which feels direct and personal. Third person can feel distant, but suits some formal contexts.

What should I put on an about page if my business is new?

Focus on why you started, who you want to help, how you work and any relevant background. Honest enthusiasm is better than inflated claims.

Does an about page help SEO?

It plays a supporting role. It reinforces what your business does, links to key pages and helps establish clear information about who you are.
