Ecommerce Stock and Inventory Display: How to Show Availability Honestly

How to display stock on an online shop: in-stock labels, low stock, out-of-stock pages, pre-orders, back-in-stock alerts and SEO, for UK ecommerce stores.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Clear, accurate stock information reduces disappointment, customer service queries and cancelled orders.
  • Out-of-stock products usually deserve a helpful page rather than deletion, especially if they will return.
  • Low-stock messages should always reflect real inventory; false urgency damages trust and may breach consumer rules.

Few things frustrate an online shopper more than finding the perfect product, choosing a size and discovering at checkout that it is unavailable. Or worse, paying for an item and receiving an email days later saying it was never in stock. How you display stock and availability affects trust, conversions, customer service workload and even search visibility. Yet it is often left to whatever a theme does by default.

This guide is for UK ecommerce owners and teams who want to show stock information clearly and honestly. It covers why accuracy matters, how to present availability on product and category pages, how to handle out-of-stock items, pre-orders and back-in-stock alerts, and the SEO points to consider.

Why stock display matters

Stock information answers a simple question: can I have this, and when? Getting it right helps in several ways:

  • Shoppers can make decisions with confidence.
  • Fewer orders are cancelled because items were not actually available.
  • Customer service spends less time answering availability questions.
  • Shoppers trust your shop more when information proves accurate.

Getting it wrong has the opposite effect, and repeated disappointment pushes customers to shop elsewhere.

Start with accurate inventory

No amount of design can fix inaccurate stock data. Before working on how availability looks, make sure the numbers behind it are reliable. That means:

  • Tracking inventory in your ecommerce platform, rather than switching it off.
  • Keeping stock in sync if you sell on several channels, such as your own site, marketplaces and a physical shop.
  • Deciding how to handle stock held at suppliers or made to order.
  • Agreeing who updates stock when deliveries arrive or returns come back.

Shopify and WooCommerce both include inventory tracking, and many businesses connect them to stock or order management systems. The right setup depends on your volume, channels and warehouse arrangements.

Showing availability on product pages

The product page is where availability matters most. Good practice includes:

  • A clear status near the price and add-to-basket button. Simple labels such as "In stock", "Low stock", "Out of stock" or "Available to pre-order".
  • Variant-level availability. If a product comes in sizes or colours, show which options are available as people select them, rather than letting them choose an unavailable size and only finding out later.
  • Delivery expectations. Where helpful, pair stock status with delivery information, such as when an order is likely to be dispatched.
  • Accessible design. Do not rely on colour alone. A green dot means nothing to someone who cannot distinguish colours or uses a screen reader. Always include text.

Our product variants guide covers how to present options so unavailable combinations are clear.

Low-stock messages and honesty

"Only a few left" messages can help shoppers who would be disappointed to miss out. But they must be true. Displaying fake scarcity or false countdowns to push people into buying is a misleading practice that can damage your reputation, and UK consumer protection rules prohibit misleading commercial practices. The Competition and Markets Authority has paid close attention to pressure-selling tactics online in recent years.

If you show low-stock messages:

  • Base them on real inventory data.
  • Choose a sensible threshold that reflects your typical sales.
  • Avoid combining them with other pressure tactics.
  • Consider whether exact numbers or a general message suits you better.

If in doubt about whether a practice is compliant, take legal advice. This guide is not legal advice.

Availability on category pages

Category and search results pages help shoppers narrow down choices, so availability information can save time here too. Options include:

  • Showing an "Out of stock" label on product cards.
  • Moving unavailable products lower in the default sort order.
  • Offering an "In stock only" filter.
  • Showing available sizes on hover or within the card, for fashion and footwear shops.

Avoid filling the top of a category with products people cannot buy. Our category page design guide and site search guide cover these pages in more depth.

Handling out-of-stock products

When a product sells out, it can be tempting to hide or delete it. That is not always the best choice. Consider the situation:

  • Temporarily out of stock. Keep the page live, mark it clearly as out of stock, give an expected return date if you know one, offer a back-in-stock alert and suggest similar products.
  • Permanently discontinued with a close replacement. Consider redirecting the old URL to the replacement product, or keep the page with a clear message and prominent link to the new item.
  • Permanently discontinued with no replacement. Depending on the page's traffic and links, you might keep an informative page with alternatives, redirect to a relevant category, or remove it. Removing a page that attracted visitors and links without any redirect throws that value away.

Whatever you choose, avoid leaving visitors at a dead end. Our 404 page design guide covers what happens when pages do go missing.

Back-in-stock alerts

Back-in-stock alerts let shoppers ask to be notified when an item returns. They capture demand you would otherwise lose and give you useful information about which products to reorder.

Keep the sign-up simple: an email field, the right variant pre-selected, and a clear explanation of what people are signing up for. Send the alert promptly when stock returns, with a direct link to the product. Be clear whether signing up for an alert also means joining your marketing list, and make sure you have appropriate consent for any marketing. Check current ICO guidance or take legal advice if unsure.

Pre-orders and made-to-order items

Pre-orders let customers buy products before they are in stock. They suit new launches, limited runs and items with a known arrival date. If you take pre-orders:

  • Label them clearly on the product page and in the basket.
  • State the expected dispatch date and keep customers updated if it changes.
  • Be clear whether payment is taken now or later.
  • Explain what happens if the customer wants to cancel.
  • Consider how pre-orders mixed with in-stock items will be shipped.

For made-to-order products, explain lead times clearly so customers know what to expect. Consumer rights around delivery timescales and cancellations apply, so make sure your terms are accurate.

Stock information and SEO

Availability affects search too. Product structured data can include availability, which helps search engines understand whether an item is in stock, and Google uses availability information in shopping features. Keep this data accurate and in sync with what the page shows.

Out-of-stock pages that remain live with helpful content can continue to attract visitors and point them to alternatives. Mass deleting sold-out products can create lots of broken links and lost rankings. Our schema markup guide, product page SEO guide and Google Shopping ads guide cover these topics further.

Measuring the impact

Useful things to monitor include how many orders are cancelled due to stock problems, how many shoppers sign up for back-in-stock alerts and how many of them buy, customer service enquiries about availability, and traffic to out-of-stock pages. These insights help you improve both the display and your buying decisions.

Common mistakes

  • Turning off inventory tracking and selling items you do not have.
  • Showing fake low-stock messages or countdowns.
  • Letting shoppers select unavailable variants without warning.
  • Relying on colour alone to show stock status.
  • Deleting sold-out products without redirects.
  • Filling category pages with items that cannot be bought.
  • Promising pre-order dates you cannot meet.

FAQs

Should I show exact stock numbers?

It depends on your products and audience. Some shops show exact numbers when stock is low; others prefer general messages. Either way, keep it accurate.

Should I delete out-of-stock products?

Usually not if they are coming back. Keep the page live with a clear message, alternatives and a back-in-stock alert. For discontinued items, consider a redirect.

Are low-stock messages allowed?

Accurate ones generally are. Fake scarcity can be misleading and may breach consumer protection rules. Take legal advice if unsure.

Do back-in-stock alerts need consent?

Sending a requested alert is different from marketing. If you plan to add people to marketing emails, make sure you have appropriate consent.

Does stock status affect SEO?

Availability can appear in structured data and shopping features, and how you handle sold-out pages can affect rankings and links.

How do I keep stock in sync across channels?

Use your platform's inventory tools and, where needed, a connected stock or order management system so every channel draws on the same numbers.
