Landscaper and Gardener Website Design: Letting the Work Speak

Website design for UK landscapers and gardeners: project portfolios, separating maintenance from design and build, seasonal updates and enquiry forms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Landscaping and garden maintenance attract different customers, so the website should give each its own clear path.
  • A portfolio organised by project, with before and after photos and short stories, does more than a long gallery of loose images.
  • Seasonal changes in demand mean the site should be easy to update, from spring design enquiries to autumn tidy-ups and winter planning.

Few trades are as visual as landscaping. A new patio, a planted border or a transformed back garden is something people can picture themselves enjoying, and the right photos do a great deal of persuading. Yet many landscapers and gardeners rely on a social feed or a one-page site that does not show the range or quality of their work, or that mixes weekly lawn mowing with full garden redesigns in a way that confuses both audiences.

This guide covers the website: how to structure it, show your work and capture enquiries if you are a landscaper, garden designer or gardener in the UK. For promotion beyond your own site, such as reviews, directories and local advertising for trades, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK.

Maintenance customers versus project customers

Many outdoor businesses do some combination of three things, and each attracts a different person:

  • Regular garden maintenance. Mowing, hedge cutting, weeding and seasonal tidies. Customers want reliability, a sense of the areas you cover and whether you have availability. Many are older homeowners, busy families or landlords.
  • Hard landscaping. Patios, paths, driveways, walls, fencing, decking. Bigger budgets, longer decisions and a stronger focus on quality and durability.
  • Garden design and soft landscaping. Planting schemes, whole-garden redesigns and lawns. Customers want ideas, inspiration and confidence in your taste.

If you do all three, the homepage should give people a quick way to choose their path. If you focus on one, say so clearly. A visitor wanting a full garden redesign may hesitate if the first thing they see is a price list for lawn cuts, and a maintenance customer may assume you are too expensive if every image shows a luxury build.

Building a portfolio that sells projects

For hard and soft landscaping, the portfolio is the heart of the site. A few principles help:

  • Organise by project, not by photo. A project page with a handful of images, a short description of the brief and what was done tells a story. A gallery of hundreds of unlabelled photos does not.
  • Use before and after shots. Garden transformations are dramatic. Taking a "before" photo from the same angle as the finished shot is one of the most useful habits a landscaper can build.
  • Mention materials and features. Porcelain or natural stone paving, sleeper beds, a pergola, drainage solutions. This helps people searching for specific ideas and helps search engines understand the page.
  • Show a range of budgets if you work across them. If smaller jobs are welcome, include some, so people with modest plans do not rule you out.
  • Ask permission. Gardens are part of people's homes, so ask customers before publishing images, and avoid showing house numbers or anything that identifies the address.

Good photos make a huge difference. Bright, overcast days often give even light, and a few wide shots alongside close-up details work well. Large image files can slow the site, so compress them before upload. Our guide to website speed optimisation explains how.

Service pages

Separate pages for your main services help visitors and search engines. Examples might include:

  • Patios and paving
  • Fencing and gates
  • Decking
  • Turfing and lawns
  • Planting and garden design
  • Garden maintenance
  • Hedge cutting and tree work, if you offer it

Each page can explain what the work involves, what affects the cost, typical timescales in general terms and a few related projects. For tree work, be careful to describe only what you are qualified and insured to do. Some trees are protected by tree preservation orders or sit in conservation areas, so it is sensible to say you can advise on whether permission may be needed and suggest customers check with their local council.

Similarly, driveway pages may touch on drainage and whether permission is needed for certain surfaces in front gardens. Keep wording hedged and point people to their local authority or official guidance rather than stating rules as fact.

Maintenance pages that answer practical questions

Maintenance customers want straightforward answers:

  • Which areas you cover.
  • Whether you offer regular visits, one-off tidies or both.
  • Whether you take away green waste, and whether you hold a waste carrier registration if you transport it, since businesses carrying waste generally need one. Check current rules with the Environment Agency or the relevant body in your nation.
  • Whether you currently have space for new regular customers.

A simple "availability" note at the top of the maintenance page, which you can update yourself, prevents a lot of wasted enquiries during busy months.

Seasonal updates

Landscaping demand tends to shift through the year. Spring often brings a rush of patio and garden enquiries, summer focuses on maintenance, autumn suits planting and tidy-ups, and winter can be a good time to plan projects. A site that lets you change homepage messages, feature different services and update availability helps you steer enquiries to match your diary.

Platforms like WordPress, Squarespace and Wix all allow this if the site is built with easy editing in mind. A short seasonal blog post or project write-up can also keep the site fresh without much effort.

Enquiry forms for landscaping

A good enquiry form helps you understand the job before a visit. Useful fields might include:

  • Type of work: maintenance, hard landscaping, design or mixed.
  • Postcode.
  • Rough size of the garden or area.
  • A budget range, presented as optional, which helps you suggest options realistically.
  • When they would like the work done.
  • Photo uploads of the current garden.

Keep required fields to a minimum and explain what happens next, for example whether you call to discuss before visiting. See our guide to website contact forms for more, and make sure your privacy notice covers any photos and details you collect.

For maintenance, an online booking or request system can work well for one-off jobs. Our guide to online booking systems covers the options.

Trust details

Landscaping projects can involve significant sums and take place at someone's home over days or weeks. Helpful trust details include:

  • Who you are and photos of you and your team at work.
  • Insurance you hold, described plainly.
  • Any qualifications or memberships you genuinely hold, shown in line with the awarding body's rules.
  • Genuine reviews, with a link to where people can read them.
  • How you approach quotes, deposits and payment stages, if relevant.
  • Any guarantee you offer on workmanship, with what it covers.

Local search

It is also worth thinking about how people describe what they want. Many search for an outcome rather than a trade, such as a low-maintenance garden, a child-friendly lawn or a patio for entertaining. Short project write-ups and service pages that use this everyday language, alongside the technical terms you would use with a supplier, make it easier for the right customers to find you and recognise that you understand what they are after.

Most landscaping and gardening searches are local. An areas covered page, consistent business details and an active Google Business Profile with project photos all help. See our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile. Project write-ups naturally mention locations and are usually more useful than thin pages for each village.

FAQs

Should landscaping and garden maintenance be on the same website?

They can be, but give each a clear path from the homepage and separate pages, because the customers and their questions are quite different.

How many photos should a project page have?

Enough to tell the story, usually a before shot, a few finished views and some details. Quality and clear captions matter more than quantity.

Should I put prices on my landscaping website?

Explaining what affects cost is often more helpful than listing figures. Maintenance services sometimes suit indicative prices if you can keep them up to date.

Do I need permission to show customers' gardens online?

It is good practice to ask, and to avoid showing anything that identifies the address.

Is a social media page enough instead of a website?

Social media is useful for sharing work, but a website gives you organised project pages, service pages for search and a place to capture enquiries that you control.
