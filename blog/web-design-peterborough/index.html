What Peterborough businesses need from a website

Peterborough is one of the faster-growing cities in England, with a strong logistics and distribution sector thanks to its road and rail links, a manufacturing base, a large number of small and family businesses, a diverse population and a city centre built around the cathedral. Many firms here sell to other businesses as well as to the public, which shapes what the website must do.

  • Fast mobile pages. Whether the visitor is a homeowner or a procurement manager, a slow page costs enquiries.
  • Clear service pages. One focused page per service makes it easier for both visitors and search engines to understand what you offer.
  • A lead-focused layout. Quote forms, tap-to-call buttons and booking links in obvious places.
  • Credibility for business buyers. Capability information, accreditations you genuinely hold, and clear contact routes.
  • A structure that scales. Room to add services, locations or e-commerce later without rebuilding.

Websites for logistics, manufacturing and B2B firms

Peterborough's location makes it a natural base for warehousing, haulage, distribution and manufacturing. These businesses often have websites that were built years ago and no longer reflect what they do. A modern B2B site should:

  • Explain capabilities clearly, with separate pages for each core service, such as storage, fulfilment, transport or fabrication.
  • Show the sectors you serve and the kind of work you take on.
  • Make it easy to request a quote, with forms that ask for the right information up front.
  • Include a careers section, because recruitment is often as important as sales.
  • Present accreditations, policies and standards you genuinely meet.

Compliance and accessibility basics

This is general information, so check official guidance or your own adviser for specifics.

  • UK GDPR. If your site collects personal data, including job applications, you need a clear privacy notice.
  • PECR. Non-essential cookies generally need consent before they load, and email marketing has its own consent rules.
  • Accessibility. Readable text, good contrast, keyboard navigation and descriptive image text.
  • Sector rules. Regulated sectors have extra rules on website claims, and approval rests with you.

How to prepare for your website project

The fastest projects are the ones where the groundwork is done early. Before the discovery call, it helps to gather a few things together. None of this needs to be perfect, but having it ready saves weeks later on.

  • A list of your services. Write down every service you offer, which ones earn the most and which ones you want more of.
  • Your best customers. Describe who they are, where they come from and the questions they ask before they buy.
  • Competitor websites. Note two or three local competitors and what you like or dislike about their sites.
  • Photos and logos. Real photos of your premises, team and work are far more convincing than stock images.
  • Access details. Make sure you can log in to your domain registrar, current hosting and Google Business Profile, or know who can.
  • Existing content. If you have brochures, price lists or old web pages, share them so useful material is not lost.

We will turn this into a written brief that sets out the pages, features and timeline, so everyone agrees on the scope before design starts.

Common website mistakes we see

When businesses come to us for a new site, the same problems appear again and again. Avoiding them is often worth more than any design trend.

  • A homepage that tries to say everything. Visitors should understand what you do within a few seconds. Detail belongs on service pages.
  • Hidden contact details. If people have to hunt for a phone number or form, many will simply leave and try a competitor.
  • Thin or duplicated pages. Pages that repeat the same text with a different town name rarely help rankings and can undermine trust.
  • Oversized images. Uncompressed photos are one of the most common reasons a site feels slow on mobile.
  • No tracking. Without analytics and conversion tracking, it is impossible to tell which pages or campaigns bring enquiries.
  • Out-of-date information. Old prices, staff who have left or last year's opening hours quietly damage credibility.

Measuring whether the website works

A website should be judged on what it does for the business, not only on how it looks. We set up analytics and conversion tracking at launch, with consent respected, so you can see how many people visit, where they come from and how many go on to call, enquire, book or buy. Useful things to review each month include the pages people land on most, the searches that bring them in, which forms are submitted and how mobile visitors behave compared with desktop visitors. If a key page attracts visits but few enquiries, that is usually the first place to improve copy, layout or the call to action.
