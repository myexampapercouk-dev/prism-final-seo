Shopify Inventory and Stock Management: The Basics

Shopify inventory management explained: tracking stock, SKUs, locations, overselling settings, stock counts, adjustments and keeping records accurate.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Turn on inventory tracking and give every variant a unique SKU before anything else; accurate stock starts with clean product data.
  • Decide deliberately whether each product can be sold when out of stock, rather than leaving the default in place.
  • Regular stock counts and recorded adjustments keep Shopify in line with what is actually on your shelves.

Selling something you do not have is one of the quickest ways to disappoint a customer. So is showing a product as sold out when there are boxes of it in the stockroom. Good inventory management in Shopify keeps your online stock levels in line with reality, so customers can buy what you have and you can reorder before you run out.

This guide covers the basics of managing inventory in Shopify for UK shops: setting up tracking, SKUs, locations, overselling rules, stock counts and adjustments. It focuses on the back-office side. For how to show stock levels to customers on product pages, see our guide to displaying stock availability.

Why inventory accuracy matters

Inaccurate stock levels cause problems in both directions:

  • Overselling: customers order items you cannot supply, leading to cancellations, refunds, complaints and lost trust.
  • Underselling: products show as unavailable when you have them, so you lose sales you could have made.
  • Poor buying decisions: if records are wrong, you may reorder too much of slow sellers and too little of popular ones.
  • Feed problems: if you list products on Google, stock mismatches between your site and feed can cause disapprovals, as our Google Shopping guide explains.

Step 1: Turn on inventory tracking

Shopify can track quantities for each product variant, but tracking is set per product. Open each product, find the inventory section and make sure quantity tracking is switched on. When tracking is on, Shopify reduces stock when an order is placed and increases it when you restock or process certain returns.

Some items do not need tracking, such as digital products, services, made-to-order items or gift cards. Leave tracking off for those so they are never shown as out of stock by mistake.

If you have many products, use bulk editing or a product export to check tracking settings across the catalogue rather than opening products one by one.

Step 2: Give every variant a SKU

A SKU (stock keeping unit) is your own code for a specific item. Every variant, such as each size and colour, should have its own unique SKU. SKUs make stock counts, reorders, warehouse work and reporting far easier.

Good SKUs are:

  • Unique: no two variants share a code.
  • Consistent: a simple pattern, such as product type, style, colour and size.
  • Readable: short enough for staff to read on a label or packing slip.
  • Stable: they do not change when you rename a product.

If your products have barcodes, add them in the barcode field as well. Barcodes help with scanning in-store, receiving stock and submitting products to Google. Our guide to product variants and bundles explains how to structure variants sensibly in the first place.

Step 3: Set up locations

Shopify lets you add locations: places where you keep stock or fulfil orders from, such as a warehouse, a shop, a home office or a fulfilment partner. Each location holds its own quantity for each variant.

If you only ship from one place, keep it simple with a single location. If you have several, think about:

  • Which locations can fulfil online orders and which are for in-person sales only.
  • The order Shopify should use when deciding where to fulfil an order from.
  • How stock is moved between locations, which Shopify can record as transfers.

The number of locations available depends on your plan, so check the current limits. If you also sell in person, our guide to Shopify POS for UK retail explains how online and in-store stock can share one system.

Step 4: Decide your overselling rules

For each tracked product, Shopify has a setting for whether customers can keep buying when stock reaches zero. This is one of the most important inventory decisions you make, and it should be deliberate.

SituationTypical choice
Fixed stock that cannot be replaced quicklyStop selling at zero.
Made to order or reliably restocked from a supplierAllow selling, with clear delivery expectations.
Preorders for upcoming stockAllow selling, clearly labelled as a preorder with an expected dispatch date.
Limited editions or one-off itemsStop selling at zero.

If you allow selling when out of stock, be honest with customers about when items will ship. Taking money for items you cannot supply for weeks, without telling customers, creates problems. UK consumer law also sets expectations around delivery and cancellation, so check current guidance if you take preorders or backorders regularly.

Step 5: Record your starting stock

Once tracking, SKUs and locations are set up, enter accurate starting quantities. The best time is after a full physical count. For a larger catalogue, export the inventory list, fill in the counted quantities in a spreadsheet and import it back. Double-check a few products afterwards to make sure the import worked as expected.

Day-to-day inventory tasks

Receiving new stock

When stock arrives, add it to Shopify promptly. Shopify can record incoming stock against purchase orders or transfers, depending on your setup, so you can see what is on the way and mark items received when they arrive. If you use a simpler approach, at least update quantities on the day stock arrives.

Returns

When you process a return, decide whether the item goes back into sellable stock. Damaged or opened items may not be resaleable. Shopify gives you the choice to restock when processing a return or refund, so make sure staff choose correctly each time.

Adjustments

Stock changes for reasons other than sales: breakages, samples, items used for photography, theft or counting errors. Record these as adjustments rather than quietly changing numbers. Shopify keeps a history of inventory changes for each variant, which helps you understand where stock is going.

Low stock alerts and reordering

Know your reorder points: the quantity at which you should place a new order, based on how quickly the item sells and how long your supplier takes. Shopify's reports can show stock levels and sales over time. Some shops use a simple weekly routine of checking low stock items and placing orders.

Regular stock counts

Even with good processes, recorded stock drifts away from reality over time. Regular counts keep it accurate. Options include:

  • Full counts: counting everything, perhaps once or twice a year.
  • Cycle counts: counting a small section regularly, so the whole range is covered over a few weeks or months.
  • Targeted counts: counting fast sellers or high-value items more often.

Count at a quiet time, or pause fulfilment for the items being counted. Compare the physical count with Shopify, investigate large differences and record adjustments. Repeated gaps on the same products usually point to a process problem worth fixing, such as returns not being restocked or stock being taken without being recorded.

Selling on several channels

If you sell through several channels, such as your website, social media shops, marketplaces or in person, stock should ideally come from one shared pool. Shopify's sales channels can share inventory, so a sale on one channel reduces stock available on all of them. If any channel is managed separately, overselling becomes much more likely.

Before adding a channel, check how it will sync stock and orders with Shopify. If you have moved from another platform, our guide to moving from Etsy to Shopify covers data and stock considerations.

Bundles and kits

Bundles, such as gift sets, are tricky because one sale should reduce the stock of several products. Shopify has options for bundles that can draw on the stock of component products. Check how your chosen approach handles inventory before launching bundles, and test that stock reduces correctly when a bundle sells.

When you outgrow the basics

Shopify's built-in inventory tools suit many small and medium shops. Signs you may need more include:

  • Many locations or a third-party warehouse that needs tight syncing.
  • Complex purchasing, such as multiple suppliers and lead times per product.
  • Manufacturing or assembly where raw materials need tracking.
  • Integration with accounting or wholesale systems.

At that point, you may look at dedicated inventory or order management systems connected to Shopify. Choose carefully, check integration quality and avoid adding tools you do not yet need. If you are planning bigger changes to your store, our guide to briefing a Shopify developer helps you explain your requirements clearly.

A simple inventory routine

  • Daily: process returns correctly and receive any new stock.
  • Weekly: review low stock items and place reorders.
  • Monthly: run a cycle count on a section of your range and review adjustments.
  • Before busy periods: do a fuller count and check overselling settings, as covered in our seasonal sales preparation guide.

FAQs

How do I stop Shopify selling out-of-stock products?

Turn on quantity tracking for the product and make sure the option to continue selling when out of stock is switched off.

What is a SKU and do I need one?

A SKU is your own unique code for each product variant. It is not strictly required, but it makes stock management, counting and reporting far easier.

Can Shopify track stock in more than one location?

Yes. Shopify supports multiple locations, each with its own stock levels. The number allowed depends on your plan.

How often should I count stock?

It depends on your range and volume. Many shops combine an occasional full count with regular cycle counts of smaller sections.

Why are my Shopify stock levels wrong?

Common causes include returns not being restocked correctly, unrecorded breakages or samples, channels not syncing, and import errors.
