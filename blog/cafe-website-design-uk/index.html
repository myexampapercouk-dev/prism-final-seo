Cafe and Coffee Shop Website Design: Building a Site That Brings People Through the Door

Cafe and coffee shop website design for UK owners: menus, opening hours, maps, pre-orders, events, photography and mobile speed that turn searches into visits.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most cafe website visitors want a few facts fast: where you are, when you are open and what you serve.
  • A text-based menu, accurate hours and a clear map link do more work than elaborate animation.
  • Extras such as pre-ordering, bean sales or event bookings are worth adding only if you can run them day to day.

A cafe or coffee shop website has an unusual job. Very few people read it at length. Most arrive on a phone, often standing on a street nearby or deciding where to meet a friend, and they want answers within seconds: are you open, where exactly are you, do you do oat milk, is there somewhere to sit with a laptop, can I bring the dog? If the site answers those questions quickly and looks like the place feels, it has done most of what it needs to do.

This guide is about the website itself: the pages, the layout, the menu, ordering options and the technical basics. If you are looking for wider promotion such as social media, local listings and review management for food and drink businesses, our guide to restaurant marketing in the UK covers that side, and much of it applies to cafes too.

How a cafe website differs from a restaurant website

Cafes and restaurants overlap, but the way customers use their websites is often different. A restaurant visit is usually planned: people compare menus, check prices and book a table for a specific evening. A cafe visit is more often spontaneous and repeated. Someone might visit three times a week, rarely book anything and decide on the day.

That changes the priorities. A cafe site needs to be lighter and faster, with the practical details right at the top. Table booking may not matter at all. What matters more is atmosphere, opening hours that are always correct, and anything that gives a regular a reason to come back, such as a seasonal menu, a new single-origin coffee or a weekend event.

The pages most cafes need

Keep the structure small. A typical independent cafe can work well with a handful of pages:

  • Home. A strong photograph of the space or the counter, a one-line description of what you are, today's opening hours and your address with a map link.
  • Menu. Food and drink, with dietary labels and a note on allergens (more on this below).
  • Visit or find us. Address, embedded map, parking or public transport notes, accessibility information and whether you welcome dogs, buggies or laptops.
  • About. Who runs the place, where your coffee or ingredients come from and what you care about. Keep it honest and short.
  • Contact. Phone, email and a simple form for enquiries such as private hire or wholesale.

If you have more than one site, give each location its own page with its own hours, address and photos. Combining several cafes onto one page makes it hard for visitors to find the details for the branch in front of them, and it makes it harder for search engines to match each location to local searches.

Make the essentials impossible to miss

Opening hours, address and phone number should be visible without scrolling on a phone, and repeated in the footer. A few practical points help:

  • Write hours as plain text, not inside an image, so they can be read by screen readers and search engines.
  • Show seasonal or bank holiday changes clearly, and remove them once they pass. Out-of-date hours are a quick way to send someone to a locked door.
  • Make the phone number tappable and the address link straight to a map app.
  • Keep the details identical to your Google Business Profile so customers do not see two different answers.

Getting the menu right

The menu is usually the most visited page after the homepage, and it is often the weakest. A common pattern is a photo or PDF of the printed menu. That is quick to upload but awkward on a phone: text is small, it cannot be searched, and it is easy to forget to replace when prices or dishes change.

A menu built as normal web text is better in almost every way. It reads cleanly on any screen, it can be updated in minutes and it helps search engines understand what you serve, which matters if someone searches for something specific such as a vegan brunch or a particular style of coffee near them.

Some suggestions for the menu page:

  • Group items the way customers think: coffee, other drinks, breakfast, lunch, cakes and bakes.
  • Use clear dietary labels such as vegetarian, vegan and gluten-free, and explain what they mean in a short key.
  • Decide whether to show prices. Many customers find it helpful, but only if you will keep them current.
  • Mark which items change seasonally or daily so people are not disappointed.

Allergen information

UK food businesses have legal duties around allergen information, and the rules differ depending on how food is sold, for example food made and packed on the premises for direct sale has its own labelling requirements. The Food Standards Agency publishes guidance for businesses. A website does not replace the information you provide in person, but it is sensible to tell visitors how to find out about allergens, invite them to speak to staff, and avoid wording that could be read as a guarantee that an item is free from a particular allergen. If you are unsure what applies to you, check the Food Standards Agency guidance or speak to your local authority's environmental health team.

Photography and atmosphere

People often choose a cafe on feel: the light, the seating, whether it looks calm or lively, whether it seems a good place to work or to chat. Photographs carry that more than any copy can.

Aim for a small set of honest images rather than lots of stock photography. Useful shots include the counter, the seating area at a quiet time and a busy time, a few signature drinks and dishes, and the outside of the building so people recognise it when they arrive. Photos of the outside are underrated; they help first-time visitors find you on a busy street.

Large images can slow a site down, so they should be resized and compressed before going live. Our image optimisation guide explains how to keep photos sharp without making pages heavy.

Online ordering, pre-orders and click and collect

Some cafes take orders through their site, whether that is coffee ahead for collection, lunch pre-orders for local offices or celebration cakes. This can be useful, but it is not automatically the right move.

Questions worth asking before adding ordering:

  • Will someone check and prepare online orders during the morning rush without slowing the queue?
  • Do you already use a till or delivery app that offers ordering, and would a second system cause confusion?
  • Is demand steady enough to justify the setup and any transaction fees?

For simple cases, a clear order form or a link to an ordering tool you already use may be enough. For cafes that sell more online, such as cakes for collection, gift vouchers or coffee subscriptions, a proper ecommerce setup on a platform like Shopify or WooCommerce may make more sense. The right answer depends on volume and how you operate on the day.

Selling beans, merchandise and gift cards

Roasters and cafes with a strong following sometimes sell coffee beans, equipment or merchandise online. That is effectively a small online shop sitting alongside the cafe site, with its own needs: product pages, delivery options, stock levels and returns. It is worth planning as a separate part of the site rather than bolting a few products onto the menu page. Our ecommerce website design guide covers what a good shop structure involves.

Events, private hire and community

Many cafes host open mic nights, book clubs, art exhibitions, workshops or private parties. If that is part of what you do, give it a home on the site.

  • An events page with upcoming dates in plain text, updated regularly, and older events removed.
  • A private hire page explaining capacity, the kinds of events you can host and what information you need from an enquiry.
  • A short enquiry form asking for the date, group size and type of event, so you can reply with something useful straight away.

Keep forms short. Asking for everything up front can put people off; the essentials are usually enough to start a conversation. Our guide to website contact forms has more on designing forms people actually complete.

If you take paid bookings for workshops or classes, an online booking tool can save a lot of back and forth. Our online booking systems guide compares the main approaches.

Choosing a platform

A simple cafe website can be built on most mainstream platforms. Hosted builders such as Squarespace and Wix can work well for a small brochure site that the owner wants to update themselves. WordPress gives more flexibility for events, multiple locations or content such as a blog. If online sales are a significant part of the business, Shopify or WooCommerce are worth considering.

Think less about features and more about who will update the site. A cafe menu and hours change often. If the person running the cafe cannot update them easily on a quiet afternoon, the site will drift out of date. Our comparison of website builders and custom websites sets out the trade-offs.

Local search basics for cafes

People searching for a coffee shop nearby are usually shown a map and a list of businesses before any websites. Your Google Business Profile does a lot of the work there, but your website supports it. A few website-side points:

  • Mention your area naturally in page titles and copy, for example the neighbourhood or high street you are on.
  • Use a consistent name, address and phone number everywhere.
  • Give each location its own page if you have more than one.
  • Make the site fast, especially on mobile data.

Keeping it current

The biggest risk to a cafe website is not poor design; it is neglect. A menu from two seasons ago, a summer opening time in winter or an event that happened last year all send a quiet signal that nobody is paying attention. Put a simple routine in place: check hours and the menu whenever they change, review events weekly, and look over the whole site every few months. FAQs

Does a small cafe really need a website?

Not every cafe does, but a simple site gives you one place you control with accurate hours, menu and location, which social profiles and listings can point back to.

Should I upload my menu as a PDF?

It is usually better to publish the menu as normal web text. It is easier to read on a phone, easier to update and more useful for search engines.

Can customers order coffee ahead through my website?

Yes, it is possible, but it only works well if your team can handle online orders during busy periods. Consider demand and your existing till or ordering tools first.

Do I need to list allergens on my website?

Your legal duties depend on how you sell food. Check the Food Standards Agency guidance. Many cafes direct customers to ask staff rather than listing full allergen details online.

Which platform is best for a cafe website?

There is no single best option. Squarespace or Wix suit simple sites, WordPress offers more flexibility, and Shopify or WooCommerce suit cafes selling products online.
