What a Conversion Rate Optimisation Audit Covers

What a conversion rate optimisation audit covers: analytics checks, user journeys, page reviews, forms, trust, speed and a prioritised plan for testing.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • A conversion rate optimisation audit should check that your data can be trusted before it draws any conclusions from that data.
  • A useful audit looks at whole journeys as well as single pages, including forms, checkout, trust signals, speed and mobile use.
  • The output should be a prioritised list of evidence-backed changes and test ideas, not a general list of design opinions.

A conversion rate optimisation audit is a structured review of how people move through your website and where they fail to take the action you want. It normally covers your tracking and analytics, your main user journeys, key page templates, forms or checkout, trust signals, speed and mobile experience, and finishes with a prioritised plan of what to fix or test first. The aim is to replace guesses about why visitors do not enquire or buy with evidence you can act on.

This guide is for UK business owners and marketing managers who are thinking about commissioning an audit, or who have received one and want to judge whether it is any good. If you are also weighing up outside help for the ongoing work, our guide to choosing a conversion rate optimisation agency covers that decision. Here we focus on the audit itself: what goes into it, what comes out and what to question.

What an audit is for

An audit is a diagnostic step. It is meant to answer three questions: where are people dropping out, why might that be happening, and which changes deserve your time and budget first? It is not a redesign, and it should not be a list of things the reviewer personally dislikes.

It also differs from other reviews you may have seen. A technical SEO audit asks whether search engines can find and understand your pages. A PPC audit asks whether your paid campaigns are set up and spending sensibly. A conversion audit starts where those stop, once a person has landed on the site, and asks what happens next. The three can overlap, and findings from one often help another.

Agreeing goals and scope

A good audit starts with a conversation rather than a crawl. The reviewer needs to understand what counts as a conversion for your business, because the answer changes what matters. For a retailer it may be a completed order. For a service business it may be a qualified enquiry, a booked call or a quote request.

Expect to be asked about:

  • Primary and secondary goals. For example, a purchase as the main goal and a newsletter sign-up as a lesser one.
  • Lead quality or order value. More form submissions are not an improvement if the extra enquiries are unsuitable.
  • Audiences. Who you want to reach, and what they usually need to know before acting.
  • Traffic sources. Which channels bring people in, because a visitor from a paid advert may arrive with different expectations from someone reading a blog post.
  • Constraints. Platform limits, legal wording you must keep, brand rules and how much development time is available.

Scope matters too. An audit might cover the whole site or a single journey, such as a paid landing page through to an enquiry. A narrower scope can be more useful when budget is limited, provided it covers the journey that matters most to the business.

Checking your data can be trusted

This is the part that is easiest to skip and the most damaging to skip. If your tracking is wrong, every later conclusion is shaky. An auditor will typically check:

  • Whether conversion events fire once, at the right moment, and on every relevant page or device.
  • Whether figures broadly agree with another source, such as orders in your ecommerce platform or enquiries in your inbox or CRM.
  • Whether internal visits, test orders and obvious spam are distorting reports.
  • Whether cookie consent settings are affecting what is recorded, and how that should change the way you read the numbers.
  • Whether traffic sources are labelled consistently, so that campaigns can be compared fairly.

If tracking problems are found, fixing them may need to come first. It can feel frustrating to spend audit time on measurement, but a test you cannot measure tells you very little. Consent and data protection rules also affect what you can track, so take advice if you are unsure what your set-up should do.

Quantitative review: where people drop out

Once the data looks reliable, the audit uses it to find the leaks. This usually means looking at funnels, such as landing page to product page to basket to checkout, or service page to contact page to form completion. The reviewer compares steps and looks for places where people leave in larger numbers than the surrounding steps would suggest.

Numbers are most useful when they are split. Averages can hide a lot, so an audit will often break results down by:

  • Device type, such as mobile and desktop.
  • Traffic source, such as organic search, paid search, email and social.
  • New and returning visitors.
  • Page template or product category.
  • Browser, which can reveal a bug that affects only some visitors.

Be careful with small numbers. If a page has had only a handful of visits, a change in its conversion rate may mean nothing. A careful auditor will say when a sample is too small to support a conclusion rather than presenting it as a finding.

Qualitative review: why people drop out

Analytics shows where something is going wrong, but rarely why. That is where qualitative evidence comes in. Depending on budget and scope, an audit may include:

  • Session recordings and heatmaps. These can show hesitation, rage clicks and content that goes unseen, subject to appropriate privacy settings.
  • On-site surveys. A short question, such as what nearly stopped you today, can surface objections you had not thought of.
  • Customer service insight. Your team and your inbox often know which questions keep coming up.
  • Customer interviews or user testing. Watching someone attempt a task can be very revealing, even with a small group.
  • Review analysis. Your customers' own words show what they value and what worried them.

Not every audit includes all of these. Ask which sources were used, because a report based only on one person's walkthrough of the site is a different thing from one that draws on customer evidence.

Page and journey review

The reviewer then works through your key pages as a visitor would, usually on a phone as well as a desktop. They will consider questions such as these.

Landing pages and service pages

Is it clear within a moment what you offer and who it is for? Does the page match the advert or search result that brought the person there? Is there one clear next step? Our guide to landing page design goes further into the structure of these pages.

Product and category pages

For ecommerce, the audit will look at how easy products are to find and compare, how well images and descriptions answer questions, whether delivery and returns information is visible before checkout, and how stock and variants are shown. Our guide to ecommerce CRO has more on these areas.

Forms and checkout

Forms deserve close attention because they sit right at the point of decision. An auditor will check how many fields you ask for and whether each is needed, how errors are handled, whether a guest checkout is available where relevant, and whether payment and delivery options are clear before someone commits. They should also try the form on a real phone, because problems often appear only there.

Trust and reassurance

People need reasons to believe you. The review will look at whether genuine reviews, case studies, accreditation, company details, contact information and clear policies are visible at the moments people need them. Any trust signal must be real and accurate, because misleading claims can create legal risk as well as losing customers.

Speed, accessibility and bugs

Slow pages, broken buttons and layouts that shift while loading can stop people completing a task. So can poor colour contrast, unlabelled form fields and elements that cannot be used by keyboard. A conversion audit is not a full accessibility audit, but it should flag obvious barriers, and those fixes often help everyone.

What you should receive at the end

The deliverable matters as much as the review. A long document that nobody acts on has little value. A useful output normally includes:

ItemWhat it should tell you
Data health summaryWhat can be trusted, what cannot, and what to fix
Findings with evidenceEach issue, where it occurs and what supports it
Prioritised action listWhat to do first, based on likely impact, confidence and effort
Quick fixesClear errors that can simply be corrected without testing
Test hypothesesChanges worth testing, what you expect to happen and why

A hypothesis should link an observation to a change and an expected result. For example: visitors on mobile are leaving at the delivery step and survey responses mention unexpected costs, so showing delivery costs earlier may reduce drop-off. That is much more useful than a note saying the checkout could be better.

How to judge an audit

Whether you are buying an audit or reading one, a few checks help separate solid work from filler:

  • Is the evidence shown? Each significant recommendation should say what supports it.
  • Is it specific to you? If the advice could apply to any website, it has probably not looked closely enough at yours.
  • Is uncertainty admitted? Nobody can promise that a change will lift results by a set amount before it has been tried. Be wary of anyone who does.
  • Is it prioritised? A flat list of many items leaves all the hard decisions to you.
  • Can your team act on it? Recommendations should take account of your platform, budget and available development time.

What to do after the audit

Start by correcting anything that is plainly broken, as these changes do not need testing. Next, work through the higher-priority items, testing where you have enough traffic for a test to mean something. Where you do not, you can still make sensible changes based on the evidence and watch results over a longer period, accepting that you will have less certainty about cause and effect.

Keep a simple log of what changed and when. It helps you interpret later results and stops the same ideas being revisited. Treat the audit as a starting point: visitors, offers and competitors all change, so it can be worth repeating parts of the review over time, or after a redesign or a major change to your traffic.

FAQs

What is the difference between a CRO audit and a website audit?

The term website audit can mean many things, often technical health or SEO. A conversion audit focuses specifically on how visitors behave and what stops them taking a chosen action, though it may flag technical issues that affect this.

Do I need a lot of traffic for an audit to be useful?

Not necessarily. Lower traffic limits how much you can conclude from analytics and A/B tests, but qualitative research, journey reviews and fixing clear problems can still be valuable. The audit should be adapted to the data you have.

Can I do a conversion audit myself?

You can do a useful first pass by checking your tracking, trying your own forms on a phone and reading customer questions. An outside reviewer brings a fresh view, because it is hard to see your own site as a newcomer would.

How much access will an auditor need?

Usually read-only access to analytics and any testing or recording tools, plus an idea of your sales or enquiry data. Share access in line with your own security and data protection policies, and remove it once it is no longer needed.

Will an audit guarantee more sales?

No. An audit identifies likely problems and opportunities, but results depend on what you implement, your offer and your market. Treat any guarantee of a specific uplift with caution.

Next steps

If you want an audit to pay off, decide what a conversion means for your business, check your tracking and gather the customer questions you already have. That preparation makes any review, internal or external, far more useful.
