European Accessibility Act: What It Means for Your Website and Online Shop

A plain-English guide to the European Accessibility Act for websites and online shops: who it may affect, what to check and how to fix common issues.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • The European Accessibility Act brings accessibility requirements to many consumer-facing digital services in the EU, including a lot of ecommerce, and can affect non-EU businesses selling into the EU.
  • WCAG-based accessibility work, covering structure, contrast, keyboard use, forms, images and checkout, is the practical route most businesses take to meet the expected standard.
  • Scope, exemptions and enforcement vary by member state and situation, so check current official guidance in each country you sell to and take legal advice where needed.

The European Accessibility Act is an EU law that sets accessibility requirements for certain products and services, including many ecommerce websites and apps that sell to consumers. If your business sells online to customers in the EU, it may apply to you even if you are based outside the EU, so it is worth checking where you stand and planning accessibility work into your website now.

We are a UK-based web design studio working remotely with businesses across Europe. We are not lawyers, and this guide is not legal advice. It explains the Act in plain terms, what it usually means for a website or online shop in practice, and how to approach an audit and fixes. Rules are implemented through each member state's national law and can change, so check current official guidance in the countries you sell to.

What the European Accessibility Act is

The Act is an EU directive designed to make common products and services more accessible to people with disabilities and to align accessibility rules across member states. Each EU country has turned it into national law, and its requirements have applied since June 2025, with some transition arrangements for existing products and services.

For digital businesses, the parts that matter most cover services such as ecommerce, banking, e-books, transport ticketing and certain communications services. In practice, that means websites, mobile apps and the online journeys customers use to find, buy and pay for products.

Does it apply to your business?

Whether the Act applies depends on what you sell, who you sell to and the size of your business. Broadly:

  • Consumer ecommerce selling to customers in the EU is generally within scope.
  • Microenterprises providing services, defined by staff numbers and turnover, are exempt from the service requirements in the Act, although national rules and other obligations still need checking.
  • Businesses based outside the EU, including UK businesses, may still be covered when they offer services to consumers in the EU.
  • Purely business-to-business services may fall outside the consumer focus, but the line can be blurry.
  • Exemptions such as disproportionate burden exist, but they come with conditions and documentation requirements.

Because the details depend on national implementation and your circumstances, take legal advice if you are unsure. Even where the Act does not strictly apply, accessible websites tend to be easier for everyone to use, and accessibility work often improves SEO and conversion as a side effect.

What the European Accessibility Act means for websites in practice

The Act sets functional requirements rather than a line-by-line checklist. The harmonised European standard for digital accessibility, EN 301 549, draws on the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), so most businesses use WCAG at level AA as their practical benchmark. In everyday terms, your site should be:

  • Perceivable. Text alternatives for images, captions for video, enough colour contrast, and content that works when zoomed.
  • Operable. Everything usable with a keyboard, visible focus states, no traps, and enough time to complete tasks.
  • Understandable. Clear labels, predictable navigation, helpful error messages and plain language.
  • Robust. Built with proper HTML so assistive technologies such as screen readers can interpret it.

The Act also expects businesses providing services to publish information about how the service meets accessibility requirements, often in an accessibility statement. Check what your target countries require.

Common problems we find on online shops

Most accessibility issues on ecommerce sites sit in a handful of places. The table below lists typical problems and the usual fixes.

AreaCommon problemTypical fix
Navigation menusDropdowns only open on hover and cannot be used by keyboardRebuild menus with keyboard and screen reader support
Product imagesMissing or meaningless alt textWrite descriptive alt text for informative images
Colour and textLow contrast on buttons, prices or sale labelsAdjust brand colours or add contrast-safe variants
Product optionsSize and colour swatches with no text labelsAdd accessible labels and selected states
Forms and checkoutPlaceholder text instead of labels; vague error messagesVisible labels and specific, linked error messages
Pop-ups and bannersCookie banners or offers that trap focus or cannot be closedAccessible dialogs with clear close controls

How to approach an accessibility audit

An audit should combine automated testing with manual checks, because automated tools only catch part of the picture. A sensible approach is:

  1. Choose key journeys. Home page, navigation, search, category, product page, basket, checkout, account and contact.
  2. Run automated checks to find obvious issues such as missing labels and contrast failures.
  3. Test with a keyboard only, moving through each journey without a mouse.
  4. Test with a screen reader on desktop and mobile.
  5. Check zoom and reflow at high magnification and on small screens.
  6. Review content for clear headings, link text and plain language.
  7. Prioritise fixes by impact on completing a purchase, then by effort.

Repeat the process in each language version of your site. Translated pages can introduce new issues, such as missing language declarations or untranslated labels on form fields. Our guide to a website content audit covers a different kind of review, but the same discipline of documenting, prioritising and re-testing applies.

Platform choice and accessibility

Shopify, WordPress and WooCommerce can all produce accessible sites, but the theme, plugins and apps you choose make a big difference. Many accessibility problems come from themes with poor markup or from apps that inject inaccessible widgets. Accessibility overlay tools that promise instant compliance are widely criticised by accessibility specialists and are not a substitute for fixing the underlying site.

If you are planning a new build or redesign, write accessibility into the brief from the start. It is far cheaper than retrofitting. Our general guide to website accessibility covers design principles, and our guide to colour and typography explains how to pick a palette with enough contrast.

What it costs to fix

Costs depend on the size of your site, your platform and how many issues an audit finds. Small fixes such as alt text, labels and contrast can be quick. Rebuilding navigation, product options or a checkout journey takes longer. In some cases, a rebuild on a better theme is more economical than patching an old one.

Questions to ask your web builder

  • Which standard will you build and test against?
  • How will you test with keyboards and screen readers?
  • Which apps or plugins could affect accessibility, and how will you check them?
  • Will you provide guidance so our team keeps new content accessible?
  • How will each language version be tested?
  • Will you help us draft or update an accessibility statement?

Clear answers to these questions are a good sign. Vague promises of instant compliance, or a reliance on a single automated score, are a reason to ask more before you commit to a supplier.

FAQs

What is the European Accessibility Act?

It is an EU directive setting accessibility requirements for certain products and services, including many consumer ecommerce services. Each member state has implemented it in national law, and its requirements have applied since June 2025, with some transition arrangements.

Does the European Accessibility Act apply to UK businesses?

It can apply to businesses outside the EU, including UK businesses, when they provide in-scope services to consumers in the EU. Whether it applies to you depends on your services and size, so check current official guidance and take legal advice.

Are small businesses exempt?

Microenterprises providing services are exempt from the Act's service requirements, based on staff numbers and turnover thresholds. Other rules may still apply, and making your site accessible is still good practice, so check your position with an adviser.

Which standard should my website meet?

Most businesses use WCAG at level AA as the practical benchmark, because the harmonised European standard EN 301 549 draws on it. Check current official guidance in your target countries for the exact expectations.

Will an accessibility overlay make my site compliant?

Overlay tools are widely criticised by accessibility specialists and do not fix underlying problems in your code, content or checkout. A proper audit and fixes to the site itself are the reliable route.

How long does accessibility remediation take?

It depends on the number and type of issues. Content fixes can be quick, while navigation, product option and checkout changes take longer. An audit gives a prioritised list so the most important journeys are fixed first.
