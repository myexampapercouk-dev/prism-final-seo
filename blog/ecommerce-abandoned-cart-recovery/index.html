Abandoned Cart Recovery: How UK Online Shops Can Win Back Lost Sales

A practical guide to abandoned cart recovery for UK ecommerce: why shoppers leave, fixing checkout friction, reminder emails and staying within UK data rules.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Fix the reasons people abandon first: surprise costs, forced accounts, slow pages and unclear delivery.
  • Reminder emails work best when they are helpful, brief and sent only where you have a lawful basis.
  • Treat abandoned baskets as feedback about your checkout, not only as a list of people to chase.

Abandoned cart recovery is the work of bringing back shoppers who added items to their basket but left before paying. It has two halves. The first is reducing abandonment by removing friction from the basket and checkout. The second is following up with people who still leave, usually by email, in a way that is useful and respects UK data protection rules.

Many shop owners jump straight to reminder emails. They help, but they only recover a portion of people who leave for reasons you could have fixed. This guide starts with the causes, then covers reminders, discounts, tracking and the legal points to check.

Why shoppers abandon their baskets

Not every abandoned basket is a lost sale. Some people add items to compare prices, save a list for later or check delivery costs. That behaviour is normal. The baskets worth worrying about are the ones where the shopper intended to buy and something stopped them.

Typical reasons include:

  • Unexpected costs. Delivery charges or other fees appearing late in checkout.
  • Forced account creation. Being made to register before paying.
  • A long or confusing checkout. Too many fields, steps or distractions.
  • Limited payment options. The shopper's preferred method is not offered.
  • Unclear delivery or returns. Not knowing when the order will arrive or how to send it back.
  • Trust concerns. A site that feels unfinished, slow or unfamiliar.
  • Technical problems. Errors, broken discount fields or pages that do not work on mobile.

You can see which of these apply to your shop by walking through your own checkout on a phone as a new customer, and by looking at where people drop out in your analytics.

Reduce abandonment before you chase it

Show costs early

Shoppers dislike surprises at the final step. Show delivery options and costs on product pages, in the basket or in a clear delivery information page linked from both. If you offer free delivery above a threshold, say so near the basket total. Our guide to ecommerce delivery and returns pages covers what to include.

Offer guest checkout

Let people buy without creating an account. You can invite them to save their details after the order is complete, when they have nothing to lose. Our guide to customer accounts and wishlists explains how to balance accounts with a quick checkout.

Simplify the checkout

Remove fields you do not need, use address lookup where your platform supports it, and keep the header and footer minimal during checkout. Shopify and WooCommerce both offer streamlined checkout options; the key is not to add clutter on top through apps or custom code.

Offer familiar payment methods

Card payments plus widely used wallets such as Apple Pay, Google Pay and PayPal cover many UK shoppers. Express payment buttons can let returning customers skip several steps. Check what your payment provider supports and test each method on mobile.

Build trust at the point of payment

Clear returns information, contact details, recognisable payment logos and a site that loads quickly all help. See our guide to website trust signals for more ideas, and our Shopify speed optimisation guide if pages feel slow.

Abandoned cart emails

Once your checkout is in good shape, reminder emails can bring back shoppers who were interrupted or undecided. Most major ecommerce platforms and email tools, including Shopify, WooCommerce extensions and Klaviyo, can send them automatically.

When reminders can be sent

A reminder can only be sent if you have the shopper's email address, which usually means they entered it at the start of checkout or are logged in. Whether you can send it, and what it can contain, depends on UK data protection and electronic marketing rules. See the section on legal points below.

What a good reminder includes

  • A clear subject line that says what the email is about.
  • The items left in the basket, with images and names.
  • A single button that returns the shopper to their basket with items intact.
  • Helpful information such as delivery options, returns and how to contact you.
  • Your brand voice, kept brief and friendly.

Avoid guilt, fake urgency or claims that stock is running out unless it genuinely is. Our guide to stock availability display covers honest ways to show limited stock.

How many reminders and when

A short sequence is usually enough: an initial reminder, then perhaps a follow-up. The right timing depends on your products. A low-cost impulse item suits a quicker reminder; a considered purchase may suit a gentler pace. Test what fits your customers and stop the sequence as soon as an order is placed.

Should you offer a discount?

Discounts in abandoned cart emails can recover sales, but they carry risks. Regular customers may learn to abandon baskets on purpose to get a code, and your margins shrink. Many shops prefer to start with a reminder that offers help and information, keeping any incentive for a later message or for higher-value baskets.

If you do offer a discount, make the terms clear, set a sensible expiry and make sure the code applies automatically or is easy to enter. A code that fails at checkout does more harm than no code at all.

Other recovery channels

Retargeting ads

Platforms such as Meta and Google let you show ads to people who visited your site. These can remind shoppers about products they viewed. They rely on tracking technologies, so your cookie consent setup must be in place. Our cookie banner design guide covers the basics.

Text messages

SMS reminders are possible but feel more personal and intrusive than email. Only consider them where you have clear permission for marketing texts, and keep them rare.

On-site reminders

When a returning visitor comes back, showing their saved basket or a gentle prompt can help them pick up where they left off. Avoid exit pop-ups that block the page or are hard to close, especially on mobile.

Legal points to check in the UK

This section is general information, not legal advice. If you are unsure how the rules apply to your shop, take advice from a qualified professional.

  • UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act. You need a lawful basis to process a shopper's email address for reminders, and your privacy notice should explain how you use it.
  • PECR. The Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations cover marketing emails and texts. Whether an abandoned cart email counts as marketing, and whether the soft opt-in applies, depends on the circumstances. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance worth reading.
  • Cookies and tracking. Non-essential tracking, including for retargeting, generally requires consent under PECR.
  • Consumer protection. Claims about prices, discounts and stock should be accurate. The Competition and Markets Authority has published guidance on online choice architecture and pressure selling.
  • Unsubscribe. Give an easy way to opt out of further emails, and honour it promptly.

Measuring abandoned cart recovery

Track a few things over time rather than chasing a single figure:

  • How many sessions reach the basket and checkout, and where people drop out.
  • How many reminder emails are sent, opened and clicked.
  • How many orders come from reminders, and whether those customers return.
  • Whether discount codes are being used by people who would likely have bought anyway.

Google Analytics and your platform's own reports can show most of this. Compare before and after when you change the checkout, so you know whether the change helped.

A simple plan to get started

  1. Complete a test purchase on your phone as a new customer and note every point of friction.
  2. Fix surprise costs, forced accounts and broken elements first.
  3. Check your privacy notice, consent and email settings against current ICO guidance.
  4. Set up a short reminder sequence with helpful, honest wording.
  5. Review results regularly and adjust timing, wording and any incentives.

FAQs

What is an abandoned cart?

An abandoned cart is a basket where a shopper added one or more products but left the site without completing the purchase. Some are casual browsing; others are genuine buyers who hit a problem or were interrupted.

Are abandoned cart emails legal in the UK?

They can be, but it depends on how you collected the email address, your lawful basis and whether the email counts as marketing under PECR. Check ICO guidance and take legal advice if you are unsure.

Should every abandoned cart email include a discount?

Not necessarily. A helpful reminder without a discount may be enough for many shoppers, and routine discounts can train customers to wait. Consider incentives selectively.

Which platforms support abandoned cart emails?

Shopify has built-in abandoned checkout emails, and WooCommerce supports them through extensions. Email platforms such as Klaviyo and Mailchimp also offer automated flows that connect to major shop platforms.

What is the first thing to fix to reduce abandonment?

Start with surprises. Make delivery costs, timescales and returns clear before checkout, and allow guest checkout. These changes address frequent causes of hesitation without any extra tools.
