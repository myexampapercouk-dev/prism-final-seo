Solar Panel Installer Website Design: Clear, Careful and Easy to Trust

Solar panel installer website design for UK firms: honest savings wording, MCS and consumer code details, battery and EV pages, and survey-led enquiries.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Solar customers are often wary of overpromising, so cautious, specific and verifiable wording tends to build more trust than bold claims.
  • Avoid quoting fixed savings, payback periods or export earnings as facts; explain the factors involved and offer a personalised assessment instead.
  • Only claim certifications, consumer code membership or scheme eligibility you can evidence, and point to official sources for schemes and grants.

Solar panel installation is a trade where the website has to work unusually hard on credibility. A homeowner considering solar is about to spend a significant sum on something they cannot easily judge themselves. They may have read about mis-selling in the past, received unsolicited calls, or seen adverts promising savings that seemed too good to be true. A solar installer's website therefore succeeds less by sounding exciting and more by sounding careful, specific and honest.

This guide is about the website itself: structure, wording, compliance details and enquiry forms. For wider promotion, such as reviews and local advertising, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK. If solar is one part of a wider electrical business, our guide to electrician website design covers the general electrical side. Nothing here is legal or financial advice, and scheme rules change, so check current official guidance before publishing.

Wording that does not overpromise

The biggest risk on a solar website is claims about money. How much a household might save, how quickly a system might pay for itself and how much it might earn from exporting electricity all depend on many factors: roof orientation and pitch, shading, system size, location, when the household uses electricity, whether there is a battery, the tariff they are on and future energy prices. None of these are known when someone first lands on your site.

Safer approaches include:

  • Explaining the factors that affect savings, in plain language, rather than quoting a figure.
  • Making it clear that any estimate you give is an estimate, based on stated assumptions, and not a guarantee.
  • Avoiding phrases such as "free electricity", "pays for itself in X years" or "cut your bills by half" unless you can substantiate them for the specific customer.
  • Being careful with environmental claims. Statements about carbon savings should be accurate and not exaggerated, and the regulator for advertising has published guidance on green claims that is worth reading.

Advertising rules apply to claims made on your own website as well as in adverts, and consumer protection law prohibits misleading information. If you are unsure whether wording is acceptable, take advice before publishing.

Certification and consumer protection details

Customers increasingly check whether an installer is certified, and some funding or export arrangements depend on it. In the UK, the Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS) certifies installers and products for small-scale renewable installations, and MCS certification is commonly required for a household to access the Smart Export Guarantee. Many installers also belong to a consumer code approved under the Chartered Trading Standards Institute scheme, which sets standards for how renewable products are sold.

On your website:

  • Only state certifications and memberships you currently hold, and keep them up to date if anything changes.
  • Follow each scheme's rules about displaying logos and wording.
  • If you use a third party for any part of the work, such as scaffolding or electrical connection, be clear about who does what.
  • Do not imply that a customer will qualify for any particular scheme. Eligibility depends on their circumstances and the scheme's rules.

Grid connection is another point worth a short explanation. Installations are generally notified to, or need approval from, the local distribution network operator, depending on size. You can say that you handle notification or applications where required, if you do, without going into technical detail.

Grants, schemes and VAT

Government schemes, grants and tax treatment for solar and batteries change from time to time and can differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. It is tempting to put headline claims on the homepage, but if the rules change and your site is not updated, you could mislead customers.

A safer pattern is a single page that explains in general terms that schemes may be available, links people to official sources such as Ofgem or the relevant government website, and says you can talk them through options during a survey. Review that page regularly. If you mention VAT treatment, describe it cautiously and check current HMRC guidance, since reliefs can be time-limited or conditional.

Pages that suit a solar installer

  • Home. What you install, where you work, your certifications (if held), and a clear way to request an assessment.
  • Solar panels. How systems work in plain language, what affects suitability and what an installation involves.
  • Battery storage. Only if you install batteries. Explain what a battery does and does not do, and avoid implying it will always increase savings.
  • EV chargers. If you install them, a separate page, since searchers are often looking for chargers alone.
  • Commercial or agricultural solar. Only if you genuinely offer it, as these customers have different questions.
  • How it works. The stages from enquiry to commissioning, described as you actually carry them out.
  • FAQs. Planning permission, roof condition, maintenance, what happens in a power cut and similar questions.
  • Projects. Photos of real installations, with customer permission.

Answering suitability questions honestly

Many visitors want to know whether their home is even suitable. You can help by explaining the general factors, such as roof direction, shading from trees or nearby buildings, roof condition and available space, while making clear that only a proper assessment can confirm it.

Planning permission is a common question. Many domestic rooftop installations fall within permitted development in England, but there are conditions and exceptions, for example for listed buildings, conservation areas and flats. Rules differ in other nations. A short, carefully worded answer that encourages people to check with their local planning authority, or that says you will advise during the survey, is safer than a blanket "no planning permission needed".

Power cuts are another common misunderstanding. Many grid-connected systems switch off during a power cut for safety reasons unless specific backup equipment is installed. If you mention this, describe it accurately for the systems you fit.

Enquiry forms and online estimates

Some installers add an online calculator that estimates system size or savings from a postcode and roof details. These can generate interest but also create risk: if the estimate is presented too confidently and the real figure differs, the customer may feel misled. If you use one, make its assumptions visible, label results clearly as indicative estimates, and avoid presenting them as quotes.

A simpler enquiry form often works just as well:

  • Postcode.
  • Property type and roof type, if known.
  • Interest in panels, battery, EV charger or a combination.
  • Rough annual electricity use, optional, with a note that a recent bill helps.
  • Optional photos of the roof.

Our guide to website contact forms covers form design in more depth. Whatever you collect, handle it securely and explain in your privacy notice how it is used. Make it clear that submitting the form will not lead to repeated unsolicited calls, if that is your practice.

Building trust without pressure

Pressure selling has given parts of the home improvement market a poor reputation, so it is worth avoiding anything that feels like it. Countdown timers, "only three slots left" banners and exaggerated discount claims tend to work against a solar installer. Calm, factual pages with clear contact details, a physical business address where appropriate, real photos of the team and installations, and genuine reviews do more. Our guides to website trust signals and an honest FAQ page cover these principles.

Aftercare is a trust signal in itself. Explain, in general terms, what support you offer after installation, how monitoring works if relevant, and how guarantees are structured, keeping your workmanship guarantee distinct from manufacturer warranties on panels, inverters and batteries.

Keeping the site current

Solar is a fast-moving sector. Product ranges, scheme rules and tariffs change, and an out-of-date website can mislead. Choose a platform that lets you edit pages easily, such as WordPress or a hosted builder, and set a reminder to review scheme, grant and VAT wording regularly. FAQs

Can I show savings figures on my solar website?

Be very cautious. Savings depend on many variables, so explain the factors and offer a personalised estimate with clear assumptions rather than headline figures.

Should I mention MCS on my website?

If you are MCS certified, yes, accurately and in line with the scheme's rules. Do not imply certification you do not hold.

Should I list grants and schemes?

Mention them in general terms, link to official sources and review the page regularly, since rules change and eligibility varies.

Is an online solar calculator a good idea?

It can be, provided assumptions are visible and results are clearly labelled as indicative, not as a quote or a guarantee.

Do customers need planning permission for solar panels?

Often not, but there are exceptions and rules vary by nation and property. Encourage customers to check with their local planning authority.
