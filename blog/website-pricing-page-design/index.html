Website Pricing Page Design: Presenting Prices, Packages and Quotes Clearly

How to design a website pricing page that explains packages, fixed prices or quotes clearly, with guidance on layout, wording, FAQs and UK pricing rules.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A pricing page helps visitors work out whether you are a realistic fit, which can save time on both sides.
  • Choose a format that suits how you actually price: fixed packages, starting prices, ranges or quote-based pricing.
  • Be clear about what is and is not included, show prices honestly and answer common questions on the page.

For many businesses, the pricing page is one of the most visited pages on the website. Visitors who have read about a service or product often want to know one thing next: roughly what it costs. If they cannot find out, some will contact you to ask, and others will simply leave to look elsewhere.

Not every business can or wants to publish exact prices, and that is fine. But even businesses that quote individually can create a useful pricing page. This guide covers the main pricing page formats, how to lay them out, what to include, pitfalls to avoid and the UK rules to bear in mind.

Why a pricing page matters

A pricing page helps visitors decide whether to take the next step. For buyers, it reduces uncertainty. For the business, it can filter out enquiries that are not a good fit and bring in people who already understand roughly what to expect.

Even when prices vary, a pricing page can explain what affects the cost, give examples of typical scopes or set out starting points. That context often matters as much as the numbers themselves.

Choosing a pricing format

The right format depends on how you genuinely price, not on what looks good. Common approaches include:

Fixed packages or tiers

Two to four packages, each with a set price and a list of what is included. This suits products and services with predictable scope, such as software subscriptions, memberships or standardised service bundles. It makes comparison easy and allows visitors to self-select.

Starting prices

"From" prices give a baseline and explain that the final cost depends on requirements. This suits services with a typical minimum but variable scope. Be careful that the "from" price is genuinely achievable, as advertising a starting price that few customers can actually get could be misleading.

Price ranges

Ranges show typical costs for different types of project, for example a range for a small project and a range for a larger one. This works for businesses that quote individually but want to set expectations.

Quote-based pricing with explanation

Some businesses price every job individually. A pricing page can still explain how quotes work, what factors affect cost and what happens after an enquiry. This is more useful than a page that simply says "contact us for a quote".

Product price lists

Some businesses, such as trades or clinics, have a list of standard services with individual prices. A clear, well-organised list, grouped by category, can work better than packages here.

Laying out tiered pricing

If you use packages, a few layout principles help:

  • Keep the number of tiers small. Too many options can make choosing harder.
  • Give each tier a clear name and audience. A short line such as "for sole traders" or "for growing teams" helps visitors self-select.
  • Use a consistent structure. List features in the same order in each tier so differences are easy to spot.
  • Highlight differences. Make it obvious what each step up adds, rather than repeating identical features.
  • Put the call to action in each tier. Make the next step obvious, whether that is buying, starting a trial or booking a call.

Highlighting one tier as "most popular" or "recommended" is common, but only do so if it is accurate. Claiming a package is the most popular when it is not could mislead customers.

For longer feature lists, a comparison table below the summary cards lets interested visitors see the detail without cluttering the main view. On mobile, cards usually need to stack vertically, and wide comparison tables may need a different layout. Our mobile website design guide covers this.

What to include on a pricing page

What is included

Spell out what the price covers. Vague phrases such as "full support" or "everything you need" leave room for misunderstanding. Specific descriptions help visitors compare and reduce disputes later.

What is not included

Equally important is what costs extra. Common examples include third-party fees, hosting, travel, materials, add-ons and ongoing costs after an initial project. Mentioning these upfront builds trust and avoids surprises.

VAT

Make clear whether prices include or exclude VAT. Rules on how prices must be shown differ depending on whether you sell to consumers or businesses. Prices shown to consumers generally need to include VAT and other compulsory charges, so check current guidance or take advice if you are unsure.

Billing terms

Explain how and when customers pay: upfront, monthly, annually, in stages or on completion. If there are minimum terms, notice periods or cancellation conditions, state them clearly.

Factors that affect price

If prices vary, list the main factors, such as size, complexity, timescale or materials. This helps visitors estimate where they might fall and makes quotes easier to understand.

Frequently asked questions

A short FAQ section on the pricing page can answer common questions, such as whether there are setup fees, whether plans can be changed or how payment works. Our guide to FAQ pages covers how to write them.

A clear next step

Finish with a clear call to action: buy, start, book a call or request a quote. If the next step is an enquiry, explain what happens next, such as how quickly you aim to reply or what information you need. Our guide to website contact forms covers designing enquiry forms.

Common pricing page mistakes

Hiding the price entirely

A pricing page that says only "contact us" gives visitors nothing to work with. Even a short explanation of how pricing works, with typical ranges or starting points, is more helpful.

Burying extra charges

Adding compulsory fees late in the process, or hiding them in small print, frustrates customers and may breach consumer protection rules. UK rules on drip pricing require compulsory fees to be included in the headline price for consumers. Check current guidance from the Competition and Markets Authority or take advice.

Fake urgency

Countdown timers and "offer ends tonight" messages that reset or are not genuine can mislead customers and may break consumer protection law. Only use time-limited offers if they are real.

Misleading reference prices

Showing a struck-through "was" price implies a saving. If the higher price was never genuinely charged for a meaningful period, the comparison may be misleading. Guidance exists on fair pricing practices, so check it before using reference prices.

Jargon

Industry terms may mean little to buyers. Use plain language to describe features and inclusions. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing clearly.

Out-of-date prices

Prices that no longer match quotes or checkout damage trust. Make pricing pages part of your regular content review. Our website content audit guide explains how.

Pricing pages for service businesses

Service businesses often worry that publishing prices will put people off or let competitors undercut them. These concerns are understandable, and the right approach depends on your market. A middle ground is to publish typical ranges or starting points, explain what drives cost and link to case studies or examples of work that show what customers get. Linking from each service page to the pricing page helps visitors find this information when they are ready.

Pricing pages for online stores

Online stores show prices on product pages, but a dedicated page can still help. It might explain delivery charges, subscription options, trade or bulk pricing, or price matching policies. Delivery costs are a common source of basket abandonment, so making them easy to find can help. Our guide to delivery and returns pages covers this, and our guide to ecommerce subscriptions covers presenting recurring pricing.

Pricing pages and SEO

Many people search for the cost of a service before contacting anyone. A pricing page, or helpful articles on what affects cost, can match those searches. Use a clear title and main heading, include the service names people search for and explain pricing in text rather than only in images, so search engines and AI tools can read it. Our guide to schema markup explains structured data, which can help describe products and offers.

Testing and improving

Pricing pages are a good candidate for measurement. Track how many visitors view the page, click each call to action and go on to enquire or buy. If you have enough traffic, test changes to layout, wording or the order of tiers. Changes to prices themselves are commercial decisions that need careful thought, but presentation can often be improved independently. FAQs

Should I put prices on my website?

It depends on your business, but some pricing information usually helps visitors. Starting prices, ranges or an explanation of how quotes work are all options.

How many pricing tiers should I offer?

There is no fixed number, but a small set is usually easier to compare than many options.

Do prices on my website need to include VAT?

Prices shown to consumers generally need to include VAT. Business-to-business pricing can differ. Check current guidance or take advice.

Is it acceptable to mark a package as most popular?

Only if it is true. Inaccurate labels could mislead customers.

What if every project is priced differently?

Explain the factors that affect cost, give typical ranges where you can and set out how the quote process works.
