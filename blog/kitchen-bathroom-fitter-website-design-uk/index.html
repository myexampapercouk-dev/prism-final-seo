Kitchen and Bathroom Fitter Website Design: Selling a Project People Can Picture

Kitchen and bathroom fitter website design for UK firms: project galleries, supply-and-fit versus fit-only pages, survey booking and honest timelines.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • A kitchen or bathroom is a considered purchase, so the website has to support weeks of research rather than a single urgent phone call.
  • Be clear about whether you supply and fit, fit only, or design as well, because visitors often arrive unsure which kind of firm they need.
  • Gas, electrical and some building work carries regulatory requirements, so describe who carries out each trade and only claim registrations you actually hold.

Nobody books a new kitchen in a panic. A homeowner thinking about a kitchen or bathroom refit will usually browse for weeks, save photos, compare a handful of firms and talk it through with whoever they live with before they ever pick up the phone. That makes the website of a kitchen or bathroom fitter quite different from the site of an emergency trade. It is less about a big call button and more about helping someone picture the finished room, trust the people who will be in their home for days or weeks, and understand what happens between first enquiry and handover.

This guide is about the website itself: pages, galleries, enquiry forms and the details specific to kitchen and bathroom work in the UK. For getting found more widely, such as reviews, directories and local advertising, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK. If plumbing repairs are a large part of your business, our guide to plumber website design covers the emergency side, which this article deliberately leaves out.

What kind of firm are you?

Visitors often do not know the difference between a showroom, a design-and-fit company, a supply-and-fit installer and a fit-only fitter. Many end up phoning the wrong kind of business. Your homepage can save everyone time by stating plainly which of these you are:

  • Design, supply and fit. You help plan the layout, source the units, worktops and sanitaryware, and install everything.
  • Supply and fit. The customer chooses from ranges you offer, and you install them.
  • Fit only. The customer buys their own kitchen or bathroom, perhaps from a large retailer, and you install it.
  • A mix. Many firms do all three. If so, a short explanation of each option, and who it suits, is more useful than a list of services.

Fit-only work in particular deserves its own page. People who have already bought a flat-pack kitchen search for fitters specifically, and they want to know whether you will install units bought elsewhere, what condition deliveries need to be in, and who is responsible if a part is missing or damaged. Set out your position rather than leaving it to a phone call.

Galleries that actually help

For this trade, photographs are the main persuasive content. But a wall of thumbnails with no context does less than you might expect. A more useful approach is to build project pages:

  • A short description of the room and the brief, in general terms, without identifying the customer or their address.
  • Before and after photos where you have them, taken from similar angles.
  • A note of what the job involved, for example moving a soil stack, replacing a bath with a walk-in shower or knocking through to a dining room.
  • The type of finish, such as handleless units, quartz-effect worktops or wall-hung sanitaryware, described generically rather than by named brand unless you have the right to use the brand.

Always get the customer's permission before publishing photos of their home, and take care not to reveal house numbers, post or anything else that could identify them. Our guide to website image optimisation covers keeping large photo galleries fast, which matters because kitchen photos tend to be big files and visitors browse them on phones.

Grouping projects by type, such as kitchens, bathrooms, en suites, wet rooms and utility rooms, lets visitors jump to what they care about. Filtering by style can help on larger sites, though a simple category structure is often enough.

Separate kitchen and bathroom sections

If you do both, treat them as two clear sections rather than one combined "kitchens and bathrooms" page. The searches are different, the visitors are often different, and the questions are different. A bathroom visitor may be asking about accessible showers, small en suites or wet rooms; a kitchen visitor may be thinking about islands, appliances and layouts. Each section can have its own overview, projects and frequently asked questions.

Within bathrooms, specific pages for wet rooms, en suites and accessible or adapted bathrooms can be useful if you genuinely offer them. Accessible bathroom work in particular draws visitors who may be arranging work for an older relative, and they will value plain information about what you can and cannot do. If funding schemes such as disabled facilities grants come up, point people to their local council rather than describing eligibility yourself, since rules vary.

Being clear about the trades involved

A kitchen or bathroom refit is rarely one trade. It may involve plumbing, electrics, plastering, tiling, flooring and sometimes gas or structural work. Customers increasingly ask who will actually be doing each part, and your site can answer that honestly.

  • Gas. Work on gas appliances such as hobs and boilers must be carried out by someone on the Gas Safe Register. If you do this work in-house, say so accurately; if you bring in a registered engineer, say that instead.
  • Electrics. In England and Wales, some electrical work in kitchens and bathrooms is notifiable under building regulations. It is usually handled either by an electrician registered with a competent person scheme or through building control. Describe how you handle it without overstating your own qualifications. Rules differ in Scotland and Northern Ireland, so check what applies where you work.
  • Structural changes. Removing walls or altering drainage may need building regulations approval. You do not need to explain the law on your website, but saying that you will advise whether approval may be needed, and work with building control where it is, reassures planners.

Only display scheme logos you are entitled to use and follow each scheme's guidance on how to show them. Avoid vague phrases like "fully qualified" that do not tell the visitor anything they can check.

Explaining the process and timeline

One of the most useful pages a fitter can have is a plain account of how a project runs with you. Only describe what you actually do, but typical stages might include:

  1. First enquiry, with photos and rough measurements if the customer has them.
  2. A home visit or survey to measure and discuss the brief.
  3. A design or written quote.
  4. Ordering, and an honest note that lead times for units and worktops vary.
  5. Installation, including how long the customer might be without a working kitchen or bathroom.
  6. Handover, snagging and how aftercare works.

The "how long will I be without a kitchen" question matters to people. Avoid promising specific durations on the website, because every job differs, but you can explain the factors that affect it and how you keep customers informed. If worktops are templated after units go in, which is common with stone and quartz-type surfaces, say so, because it explains a gap that otherwise looks like a delay.

Pricing without false precision

Customers want an idea of cost, and kitchen and bathroom prices vary enormously with size, specification and the amount of building work. Rather than publishing figures that may mislead, you can explain what drives cost: layout changes, moving plumbing, the type of worktop, tiling extent, whether the customer is supplying their own units and so on. If you do publish guide prices, make clear what they include, whether VAT is included and that a survey is needed for a firm quote.

If you take deposits, say how payment stages work in general terms. If you offer finance through a third party, be careful: offering or promoting consumer credit is regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority, and the wording and permissions involved need checking with your finance provider before you put anything on the site.

Enquiry forms for considered projects

A good enquiry form for a fitter asks enough to be useful without becoming a questionnaire. Useful fields might include:

  • Kitchen, bathroom, en suite or something else.
  • Rough timescale, for example "within three months" or "just researching".
  • Whether they have already bought units or sanitaryware.
  • Postcode, so you can check coverage.
  • Optional photo upload of the current room.

The timescale question helps you prioritise without putting anyone off, and the "already bought" question tells you instantly whether it is a fit-only enquiry. Our guide to website contact forms goes into form design in more depth. If you offer home visits at set times, an online way to request a survey slot can work well; see online booking systems for the trade-offs.

Trust for people letting you into their home

A kitchen or bathroom refit means having people in the house for an extended period. Visitors want to feel they know who is coming. Things that help, where true, include photos of the people who actually do the work, an explanation of how you protect floors and keep dust down, how you handle waste removal, and whether you have public liability insurance. Our guide to website trust signals covers the general principles.

Guarantees deserve careful wording. If you offer a workmanship guarantee, say what it covers and for how long, and keep it separate from manufacturer warranties on units, appliances or taps, which are set by the manufacturer. If you belong to an insurance-backed guarantee scheme, describe it accurately and only if you are a member.

Local pages, done properly

Kitchen and bathroom fitters often cover a wide area because projects are larger and worth travelling for. Area pages can help, but only if each one says something real, such as projects completed nearby (with permission), typical housing in that area and any practical notes about access or parking. Thin copies of the same page with the town name swapped tend to be poor for visitors and may be treated as low value by Google.

Choosing a platform

Most fitters do not need online checkout. A well-built WordPress site suits firms that want many project pages and regular updates, while Squarespace or Wix can work for a smaller portfolio. What matters most is that adding a new project with photos is easy enough that you actually do it. Our comparison of website builders versus custom websites can help with that choice.

FAQs

Should I have separate pages for kitchens and bathrooms?

Usually, yes. They attract different searches and different questions, and separate sections let each one show relevant projects and answer its own FAQs.

Should I advertise fit-only work?

If you offer it, a dedicated page helps, because people who have bought a kitchen elsewhere search specifically for fitters. Explain your terms for units supplied by the customer.

Can I put customer photos on my website?

Only with the customer's permission, and avoid anything that identifies them or their address.

Should I publish kitchen prices?

Many fitters explain what drives cost rather than listing figures, since jobs vary so widely. If you publish guide prices, be clear about what is included and about VAT.

Do I need to mention building regulations?

You do not need to explain the rules, but saying how you handle notifiable electrical work, gas work and structural changes reassures customers. Check the current requirements for the nations you work in.
