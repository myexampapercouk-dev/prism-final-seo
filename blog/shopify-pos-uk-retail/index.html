Shopify POS for UK Shops Selling In Store and Online

A guide to Shopify POS for UK retail shops: unified stock, in-store and online orders, click and collect, hardware, staff, returns and when it fits.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify POS lets a physical shop and an online store share one product catalogue, stock count and customer record.
  • Click and collect, in-store returns of online orders and ordering out-of-stock items for delivery are where a joined-up setup helps most.
  • Check hardware, card reader availability, plan features and integrations against how your shop actually trades before committing.

Many UK independent shops now sell both over the counter and online. Running those two channels on separate systems often causes problems: stock counts drift apart, an item sells online that has already gone in the shop, customer details live in two places and reconciling sales takes time. Shopify POS is Shopify's point of sale system, designed so that a physical shop and an online store run from the same back office.

This guide explains what a joined-up setup involves, where it tends to help, what to check before switching and how to plan the move. Shopify's features, plans and hardware change over time and can vary by country, so check current details with Shopify for the UK.

What Shopify POS is

Shopify POS is an app that runs on a compatible tablet or phone, used alongside a card reader and optional hardware such as a receipt printer, barcode scanner and cash drawer. Because it is part of Shopify, sales made in the shop are recorded in the same admin as online orders, against the same products and stock levels.

Shopify offers different levels of POS functionality, with more advanced features available on higher tiers. Which level you need depends on factors such as how many staff and locations you have and which features matter to you. Check Shopify's current plans for what is included.

Why one system for shop and website

The main benefit of running both channels on one platform is a single source of truth.

  • Shared stock. When an item sells in the shop, the stock available online updates accordingly, reducing the risk of selling something you no longer have.
  • One product catalogue. Products, prices, variants and images are managed once rather than in two systems.
  • Unified customer records. A customer who buys in store and online can have one profile, with consent handled properly, which helps with service and marketing.
  • Combined reporting. Sales from both channels can be viewed together, making it easier to see what is selling.

This does not remove the need for good processes. Stock still needs counting, and staff need to record sales, returns and transfers consistently for the numbers to stay accurate.

Where a joined-up setup helps customers

Some of the most useful features connect the two channels from the customer's point of view.

  • Click and collect. Customers buy online and collect from the shop. Staff can see and fulfil pickup orders from the POS. Clear collection instructions and notifications matter here, and our guide to transactional emails covers the messages involved.
  • Local delivery. Shops can offer delivery within a set area, managed alongside other orders.
  • Returning online orders in store. Customers can bring back an online purchase to the shop, which many prefer to posting it.
  • Ordering items not in the shop. If a size or colour is out of stock in store but available elsewhere, staff can place an order for delivery, keeping the sale.
  • Showing stock online by location. For multi-location retailers, shoppers can see availability at particular shops. Our guide to stock availability display covers how to present this.

Hardware and payments

Shopify POS needs a compatible device and a card reader. Shopify sells its own hardware in the UK, and compatibility with other devices and readers varies, so check the current list before buying anything. Consider:

  • Whether you need a fixed counter setup, a mobile setup for markets and events, or both.
  • Receipt printers, barcode scanners, label printers for price tags and cash drawers.
  • Reliable wifi in the shop, and how the system behaves if the connection drops. Check current offline capabilities.
  • Payment processing fees for in-person card payments, which can differ from online fees. See Shopify's current UK pricing.

We do not quote hardware or subscription prices here because they change. For our own fees for setting up a store, see our pricing page.

Markets, pop-ups and events

Not every bricks and clicks business has a permanent shop. Many UK makers and small brands sell at markets, fairs and pop-up events alongside their website. A mobile POS setup can suit this well: a phone or tablet with a card reader lets you take payments on a stall and record each sale against the same stock as your online store. That helps avoid the common problem of selling the last unit of something at a weekend market while it still shows as available online.

A few practical points for events: check how the system handles patchy mobile signal at outdoor venues, take a realistic amount of stock and record it against an event location if you want to track it separately, and consider how you will capture email sign-ups from stall visitors with proper consent. After the event, reviewing what sold can inform both your online merchandising and what to bring next time.

Staff, permissions and day-to-day use

In a shop with several staff, it helps to give each person their own login or PIN, with permissions appropriate to their role. That way, for example, applying discounts or processing refunds can be limited to certain staff, and sales can be attributed to individuals if you track performance. Train staff on common tasks such as sales, exchanges, returns, gift cards and pickups before going live. Gift cards sold in store and online can work across both channels; see our guide to gift cards and vouchers.

Is Shopify POS right for your shop?

Shopify POS tends to suit retailers whose shop and online store sell broadly the same products and who want them tightly connected. It is worth looking closely at fit if:

  • You rely on specialist functions, such as hospitality table service, complex made-to-order workflows, or industry-specific systems, which a general retail POS may not cover.
  • Your accounting, stock or supplier systems need to integrate. Check that integrations exist for the tools you use and that they handle the data you need.
  • You have very large catalogues or complex stock across many locations and warehouses.
  • Your current website runs on another platform, such as WooCommerce. Using Shopify POS generally means the online store also moves to Shopify, or you need a separate integration.

If your website is not yet on Shopify, our guides to moving from WooCommerce to Shopify and Shopify vs WooCommerce explain what is involved.

Planning the switch

Moving a shop's tills and stock to a new system needs careful planning, ideally at a quieter time of year rather than just before a busy season. Key steps include:

  • Clean up product data. Make sure products, variants, barcodes and SKUs are consistent. Duplicate or inconsistent records cause problems once channels share stock.
  • Do a full stock count. Start with accurate stock levels across the shop and any storage.
  • Set up locations. Each shop or stockroom can be a location with its own stock.
  • Decide which products sell where. Some items may be shop-only or online-only.
  • Test before go-live. Run test sales, refunds, exchanges and pickup orders. Our guide to staging and testing covers testing more generally.
  • Plan migration of customer data and gift card balances from any old system, checking data protection obligations.

The online side still needs attention

A unified system does not make the website successful on its own. The online store still needs clear navigation, good product pages, fast loading and a way for people to find it. Local shops can benefit from showing location, opening hours and click and collect options prominently, and from a well-maintained Google Business Profile. FAQs

What is Shopify POS?

Shopify POS is Shopify's point of sale system for in-person selling. It runs on a compatible device with a card reader and shares products, stock and customers with your Shopify online store.

Does Shopify POS sync stock with my online store?

Yes, in-store and online sales draw from the same stock records in Shopify, so levels update across channels. Accurate counts and processes are still needed.

Can customers return online orders in my shop?

Shopify POS supports processing returns of online orders in store. Set out your returns policy clearly for both channels.

Can I use Shopify POS with a WooCommerce website?

Shopify POS is designed to work with a Shopify store. Using it with another platform would need a separate integration, so many retailers move the website to Shopify too.

Which card readers work with Shopify POS in the UK?

Compatibility depends on the country and changes over time. Check Shopify's current UK hardware list before buying.
