Ecommerce Customer Accounts and Wishlists: Designing Pages Shoppers Actually Use

Plan ecommerce customer account and wishlist pages: guest checkout, sign-in, order history, returns, saved items and privacy, so UK shoppers come back.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Customer accounts should make repeat buying and after-sales tasks easier, never stand in the way of a first purchase.
  • Order history, tracking, returns and saved addresses are the features most account holders actually need.
  • Wishlists work best when they are easy to use without friction and connect sensibly to stock and email updates.

Customer accounts and wishlists are often treated as box-ticking features on an online shop. A template includes them, they get switched on, and nobody looks at them again. Yet these pages sit at the heart of the relationship between a shop and its returning customers. A well-designed account area makes reordering simple, cuts down on customer service emails and gives people a reason to come back. A clumsy one adds friction, frustrates loyal customers and can even put first-time buyers off.

This guide is for UK ecommerce owners and teams planning a new store or reviewing an existing one. It covers when to ask for an account, how to handle sign-in and guest checkout, what to include in the account area, how to design wishlists that people use, and the privacy and security points to keep in mind.

Should shoppers need an account to buy?

For most online shops, the answer is no. Forcing people to create an account before they can check out is a well-known source of abandoned baskets. A first-time buyer wants to pay and get on with their day, not set up a password for a shop they have never used before.

A common approach is to:

  • Offer guest checkout prominently.
  • Let returning customers sign in quickly if they want to use saved details.
  • Invite guests to create an account after their order is placed, using the details they have already entered.

There are exceptions. Trade-only shops, businesses with wholesale pricing or stores that need to verify eligibility may need accounts before purchase. Even then, explain why and keep the process short. Our ecommerce CRO guide covers checkout friction more widely.

Making sign-in painless

If returning customers struggle to log in, they will either give up or check out as a guest, losing the benefits of their saved details. Good sign-in design includes:

  • A clear link to the account area in the header, usually with a recognisable person icon and text label.
  • Short forms that work well with browser and password manager autofill.
  • A straightforward password reset process that sends emails promptly.
  • Helpful error messages that explain what went wrong without revealing whether an email address is registered, which is a security consideration.
  • Options such as passwordless sign-in by email link or code, where your platform supports it.

Shopify, for example, offers customer account options including sign-in by one-time code, and WooCommerce supports accounts through WordPress with a range of configuration choices. Which approach suits you depends on your platform, your customers and how much you want to customise.

What to include in the account area

An account dashboard should focus on the tasks customers actually come to do. For most shops that means:

  • Order history. A clear list of past orders with dates, items, totals and status.
  • Order tracking. Links to delivery tracking where available, so customers do not need to email to ask where their parcel is.
  • Returns and exchanges. A simple way to start a return or see how, linked to your returns policy.
  • Reorder. A quick way to buy the same items again, especially useful for consumables.
  • Saved addresses. Delivery and billing addresses customers can edit.
  • Account details. Name, email, password and communication preferences.
  • Invoices or receipts. Downloadable where customers might need them, particularly for business buyers.

Depending on your shop, you might also show subscription management, loyalty points, saved payment methods handled by your payment provider, or trade pricing. Only add features you will support properly. An empty loyalty section or broken returns link damages trust.

Clear returns information matters inside and outside the account area. Our delivery and returns pages guide covers how to present policies, and our ecommerce subscriptions guide covers recurring orders.

Designing the dashboard

Keep the account area tidy and easy to scan. Some practical points:

  • Show the most recent order and its status near the top.
  • Use plain labels such as "Orders", "Addresses" and "Account details".
  • Make sure the layout works on mobile, where many customers check orders.
  • Keep consistent branding so the account area feels part of the shop, not a bolted-on system.
  • Offer a clear route to customer service if something is wrong.
  • Include an obvious sign-out link.

Test the account area as a real customer would: place an order, track it, start a return, change an address. Small gaps become obvious quickly.

Wishlists: what they are for

Wishlists, sometimes called saved items or favourites, let shoppers keep products they are interested in without adding them to the basket. They can help with:

  • Considered purchases, where people compare options over days or weeks.
  • Gift ideas, when a shopper wants to share a list with friends or family.
  • Returning visits, giving people a quick way back to products they liked.
  • Understanding demand, by showing which products are being saved.

Not every shop needs one. If most of your purchases are quick, low-cost and repeat, a wishlist may add clutter without much value. If you sell higher-value or gift-friendly products, it is often worth considering.

Designing wishlists people use

A wishlist only helps if it is easy to use. Good practice includes:

  • Visible save buttons. A heart or bookmark icon on product and category pages, with a text label or accessible name so its purpose is clear.
  • Clear feedback. A visible change when an item is saved, and an easy way to remove it.
  • Saving without an account, at first. Letting guests save items in their browser, then prompting them to sign in or create an account to keep the list across devices.
  • Variant awareness. Remembering the chosen size or colour where relevant.
  • Stock and price information. Showing whether saved items are still available and their current price.
  • Easy move to basket. One action to add a saved item to the basket.
  • Sharing options. A shareable link for gift lists, if that suits your products.

Connecting wishlists and email

Wishlists can feed useful, relevant emails, such as a reminder when a saved item is back in stock or a note when it drops in price. Used thoughtfully, these messages can be genuinely helpful. Used carelessly, they feel intrusive.

Make sure any marketing emails based on saved items are covered by appropriate consent, and let people control what they receive. UK rules on electronic marketing and data protection apply, and the details depend on your circumstances, so check current ICO guidance or take legal advice. Our ecommerce email marketing guide explains how these flows fit into a wider email programme.

Privacy and security

Customer accounts store personal data, including names, addresses, order histories and contact preferences. That brings responsibilities. Consider:

  • Collecting only the information you genuinely need.
  • Explaining in your privacy notice how account data is used.
  • Letting customers update their details and request deletion of their account, in line with your obligations.
  • Using HTTPS throughout, which most platforms provide by default.
  • Leaving card storage to your payment provider rather than storing card details yourself.
  • Keeping platforms, themes, apps and plugins up to date, particularly on self-hosted stores.

UK data protection law sets out rights and duties around personal data, and the specifics depend on your situation. This guide is not legal advice. For self-hosted shops, our WordPress security guide covers the basics.

Encouraging account creation without pressure

If accounts help your customers, make the benefits clear rather than forcing sign-up. Explain what an account offers, such as faster checkout, order tracking or saved items. The order confirmation page is a natural place to invite a guest to create an account, as most details are already known. Avoid pre-ticked marketing consent boxes or bundling marketing sign-up into account creation without a clear choice.

Measuring success

Track how these features are used so you can improve them. Useful questions include:

  • What proportion of orders come from signed-in customers versus guests?
  • How often do customers use reorder, tracking or returns from their account?
  • How many items are saved to wishlists, and how many of those are later bought?
  • Do customer service enquiries about order status fall after improving the account area?

Analytics events can capture much of this. Our GA4 setup guide explains how to track key actions.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing account creation before checkout.
  • Hiding the sign-in link or making password reset unreliable.
  • Leaving account pages unstyled or broken on mobile.
  • Adding wishlist buttons with no clear label or feedback.
  • Losing saved items when a guest signs in.
  • Sending wishlist emails without proper consent.

FAQs

Should I offer guest checkout?

For most shops, yes. Forcing account creation before purchase tends to put first-time buyers off. You can invite guests to create an account after ordering.

What should a customer account page include?

Order history, tracking, returns, saved addresses and account details cover what most customers need. Add other features only if you will support them.

Do I need a wishlist on my online shop?

Not always. Wishlists suit considered or gift purchases. For quick, repeat purchases, they may add little value.

Can guests use a wishlist?

Many setups let guests save items in their browser, then prompt them to sign in to keep the list across devices.

Can I send emails about wishlist items?

Back-in-stock or price-drop emails can be helpful, but marketing emails need appropriate consent. Check current guidance or take legal advice.

Do Shopify and WooCommerce include customer accounts?

Both support customer accounts, with different options for sign-in and customisation. Wishlists usually need an app, plugin or custom development.
