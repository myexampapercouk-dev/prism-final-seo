Website Design for Builders and Construction Companies in the UK

Website design guide for UK builders and construction firms: services, project case studies, accreditations, quote requests, tenders, recruitment and platforms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A construction website needs to show the type of work you do, where you do it and evidence that you do it well.
  • Accurate project case studies, genuine accreditations and a clear quote request route help both homeowners and commercial clients judge fit.
  • Claims about accreditations, insurance, guarantees and compliance should be specific, current and supportable.

For builders and construction companies, much new work still comes from recommendations, repeat clients and relationships. Even so, the website plays an important role. Homeowners check it before inviting someone to quote, commercial clients and main contractors look at it before adding a firm to a tender list, and potential recruits look at it before applying. A site that is out of date, vague or hard to use on a phone can quietly cost opportunities.

This guide is about the website itself for UK builders, general contractors, extension and renovation specialists, groundworks firms, commercial fit-out contractors and similar businesses. It covers the pages you need, project case studies, accreditations, quote requests, tender-ready information, recruitment and choosing a platform. If you are an individual tradesperson looking for wider marketing advice, our tradesman marketing guide covers local promotion, reviews and lead generation more broadly.

Know who the site is for

Construction firms often serve more than one audience, and each looks for different things:

  • Homeowners want to know you do the type of work they need, in their area, and that you are trustworthy and tidy.
  • Commercial clients and developers want evidence of comparable projects, capacity, health and safety standards and relevant accreditations.
  • Main contractors looking for subcontractors want to see your specialism, coverage, accreditations and how to get you on their supply chain.
  • Job seekers want to know what working for you is like and what roles are open.

You do not need separate websites for each, but your navigation and home page should make it easy for each group to find their route. Our navigation structure guide explains how to organise this.

The pages a construction website needs

Home page

State clearly what you build and where, then point visitors to your services, recent projects and a quote request. Strong images of real completed work tend to make more impact than stock photography of hard hats. Our homepage design guide covers the essentials.

Service pages

Give each main service its own page, such as extensions, loft conversions, new builds, refurbishments, groundworks or commercial fit-outs. Explain what the service involves, the types of project you take on, how you typically work and the areas you cover. Separate pages help visitors find relevant information and give search engines a clear page for each service. Our service page design guide has more.

Projects or case studies

Your most persuasive content. More on this below.

About page

Explain the history of the business, the people who run it and how you work. Clients hiring a builder are often making a significant investment and want to know who they are dealing with. Our about us page guide can help.

Accreditations and policies

A page listing genuine accreditations, memberships and relevant policies, explained below.

Contact and quote request

A clear route to request a quote or site visit, plus phone, email and the areas you cover.

Project case studies

Photographs of finished work are good; case studies that explain the project are better. For each featured project, consider covering:

  • The type of project and general location, without revealing private addresses.
  • What the client needed and any challenges, such as access, ground conditions or working around occupants.
  • What you delivered and how.
  • Progress photos as well as finished images, which show quality of work hidden in the final result.
  • A comment from the client, if genuine and given with permission.

Keep details factual. Describe timescales or budgets only if they are accurate and the client is happy for you to share them. Commercial clients may have confidentiality requirements, so check before publishing. Get homeowners' permission before showing their property, and be careful not to reveal security details.

Project pages with genuine detail also tend to be useful for search, as they naturally describe the type of work and places you cover.

Accreditations, insurance and guarantees

Accreditations and memberships can reassure clients, particularly on commercial work where they may be a requirement. Present them carefully.

  • Only display accreditations, memberships and logos you currently hold and are permitted to use. Many schemes have rules about how their logos may be displayed.
  • Keep the list up to date and remove anything that has lapsed.
  • If you mention insurance, describe it accurately and be prepared to provide evidence when asked.
  • If you offer warranties or guarantees, explain clearly what they cover and any conditions, or link to full terms. Avoid vague promises.
  • Be careful with phrases about compliance with building regulations or other standards. Describe your actual responsibilities rather than making broad claims.

Misleading claims about accreditations or guarantees can create legal risk under UK consumer protection rules as well as damaging trust. Our article on website trust signals explains how to present credentials in a way that is helpful rather than cluttered.

Information for commercial clients and tenders

If you work on commercial projects or as a subcontractor, procurement teams may look at your website when assessing you. Consider making it easy to find:

  • The sectors and project types you work in.
  • Relevant accreditations, health and safety information and policies, such as environmental or quality policies, if you have them.
  • Comparable projects, with enough detail to show relevant experience.
  • Your geographical coverage.
  • A contact for tender and procurement enquiries.

Some firms offer downloadable capability statements or policy documents. If you do, keep them current. Our B2B marketing guide covers how business buyers research suppliers more generally.

Quote requests that save time

A well-designed quote request form can help you understand a job before calling back. Consider asking for:

  • The type of work and a short description.
  • The property type and location or postcode area.
  • Rough timing.
  • Whether plans or planning permission are already in place.
  • An option to upload photos or drawings.

Keep the form manageable on a phone, and make your phone number easy to tap for people who prefer to call. Only state response times you can consistently meet. Our contact forms guide explains how to design forms people complete, and our mobile website design guide explains why phone usability matters.

Make sure your privacy notice covers the information you collect through forms and how you use it. The Information Commissioner's Office has guidance for small businesses on UK GDPR.

Recruitment

Many construction firms find it hard to recruit, and your website can help. A careers page that explains what working for you is like, the types of roles you hire for, any training or apprenticeships you genuinely offer and how to apply gives candidates a reason to choose you. Keep vacancy listings current, and remove filled roles promptly.

Getting found locally

Homeowners usually search for builders near them. Clear service area information, a complete Google Business Profile and genuine reviews all help. Avoid creating lots of near-identical location pages with only the town name changed; pages for areas you serve work better when they include genuinely local content, such as relevant projects. Our local SEO guide and Google Business Profile guide explain the basics, and our multi-location SEO guide covers firms working across several areas.

Choosing a platform

Most construction websites do not need ecommerce. WordPress is a common choice for firms that want flexibility, multiple service and project pages and room to grow. Squarespace and Wix can suit smaller builders who want a straightforward site they can update themselves. Our WordPress small business website guide and website builder vs custom website article explain the options. Whatever you choose, make sure the site loads quickly, especially with large project photos; our website speed guide can help.

Keeping the site current

A construction website goes stale quickly if new projects are never added. Set a simple routine to photograph finished jobs, gather client permission and add a case study every so often. Updated accreditations, current vacancies and recent projects all show the business is active. Our website maintenance guide covers the technical side of keeping a site healthy.

FAQs

What should a construction company website include?

Clear service pages, project case studies, an about page, genuine accreditations and policies, a quote request form, a careers page if you hire, and a privacy notice.

Can I show accreditation logos on my website?

Only if you currently hold the accreditation and the scheme permits logo use. Keep the list up to date and remove anything that has lapsed.

Should builders show prices on their website?

Building work varies so much that many firms quote per job. If you show guide prices, make sure they are accurate and explain what affects the final cost.

How do I show projects without revealing clients' addresses?

Describe the general area rather than the address, get clients' permission before publishing, and avoid images that show security details.

Which platform is best for a builder's website?

WordPress suits firms wanting flexibility and lots of project pages. Squarespace and Wix suit smaller builders who want a simple site they can manage themselves.
