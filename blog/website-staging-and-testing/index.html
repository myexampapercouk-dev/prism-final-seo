Website Staging and Testing: How to Check a Site Properly Before It Goes Live

What a staging site is and how to test a website before launch: functionality, forms, checkout, mobile, speed, SEO, accessibility and safe updates after launch.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A staging site is a private copy of your website where changes can be built and tested without affecting real visitors.
  • Testing should cover what customers do, not just how pages look: forms, checkout, emails, mobile, speed and tracking.
  • Staging stays useful after launch for testing updates, plugins and theme changes before they reach the live site.

Most website problems that customers notice could have been caught before they went live. A contact form that sends enquiries nowhere, a checkout that fails on one payment method, a page that breaks on certain phones, or an entire site hidden from Google because a setting was left on. These are rarely the result of bad design; they are the result of not testing properly before launch.

This guide explains what a staging site is, why it matters and how to test a new website, a redesign or a significant change before real visitors see it. It is written for UK business owners and marketing teams who want to understand what good testing involves, whether they are working with a developer, an agency or managing a site themselves.

What is a staging site?

A staging site is a private copy of your website, usually on a separate address, where changes can be built and checked without affecting the live site. Visitors cannot see it, and search engines should not index it. Once everything has been tested and approved, the changes are moved to the live site.

Different platforms handle this differently:

  • WordPress and WooCommerce. Many hosts offer one-click staging environments. Developers may also use a local copy on their own computer as well as staging on the server.
  • Shopify. You can duplicate a theme and preview or edit the copy without publishing it. For larger changes, some businesses use a separate development store. Note that a duplicate theme shares the live store's products, orders and settings, so some changes, like app installs or checkout settings, still affect the live store.
  • Custom builds. Usually have dedicated development and staging environments as part of the deployment process.

Why staging matters

Editing a live website directly is risky. A small change can break a layout, conflict with a plugin or take a page offline while customers are trying to use it. For an online shop, even a short outage or broken checkout means lost orders. Staging gives you somewhere to:

  • Build new pages and features without visitors seeing half-finished work.
  • Test updates to themes, plugins and apps before applying them.
  • Let stakeholders review and approve changes.
  • Roll back easily if something is wrong, because the live site was never touched.

Protect the staging site

A staging site should be private. If search engines index it, you can end up with duplicate content competing with your real site. Common protections include password protection, blocking search engines and restricting access. Password protection is generally the most reliable. A "discourage search engines" setting or noindex tag helps, but must be removed when the site goes live. Forgetting to remove it is one of the most common and damaging launch mistakes, because it can hide an entire site from search results.

Also check that staging does not send real emails to customers, take real payments or sync test orders into your accounting or stock systems. Payment gateways usually offer a test or sandbox mode for this purpose.

What to test before launch

Testing is most effective when you think about what visitors actually do on the site, not just whether pages look right in a screenshot. Here are the main areas to cover.

Content and pages

  • Every page has final content, with no placeholder text or images.
  • Spelling, grammar, phone numbers, email addresses and opening hours are correct.
  • Prices, delivery information and legal pages are up to date.
  • Images display properly and have appropriate alt text.

Links and navigation

  • Main menus, footer links and buttons go where they should.
  • No links point to the staging address or to deleted pages.
  • Links to external services open correctly.
  • The 404 page works and helps people find their way. See our 404 page design guide.

Forms and enquiries

  • Submit every form and confirm the enquiry arrives at the right inbox.
  • Check confirmation messages and any automatic replies.
  • Test validation: what happens if someone leaves a field blank or enters an invalid email.
  • Check spam protection does not block genuine submissions.

Our website contact forms guide covers form design and reliability in more detail.

Ecommerce and checkout

  • Add products to the basket, change quantities and remove items.
  • Test each variant, discount code and delivery option.
  • Complete test orders with each payment method, using test mode.
  • Check order confirmation and other transactional emails arrive and are correct.
  • Confirm VAT and delivery costs calculate as expected.
  • Test customer accounts, password resets and guest checkout.

Mobile and browsers

  • Test on real phones and tablets, not just a resized desktop browser.
  • Check major browsers, including Safari on iPhone, which can behave differently.
  • Check menus, pop-ups and forms are usable on small screens.

See our mobile website design guide for more on mobile usability.

Speed

  • Run key pages through Google's PageSpeed Insights and look at mobile results.
  • Check image sizes and that unnecessary scripts are not loading.
  • Bear in mind staging servers may be slower or faster than live hosting, so recheck after launch.

Our website speed optimisation guide goes into more detail.

Accessibility

  • Navigate key journeys with a keyboard only.
  • Check colour contrast, heading structure and form labels.
  • Run an automated accessibility checker, then review issues manually.

See our website accessibility guide.

SEO

  • Page titles and meta descriptions are in place.
  • Heading structure is logical.
  • If URLs are changing, redirects from old URLs to new ones are mapped and ready.
  • An XML sitemap is generated and the robots.txt file is correct for the live site.
  • Structured data, if used, is valid.

For redesigns and migrations, our SEO migration checklist and guide to redesigning without losing rankings cover the extra steps needed.

Tracking and consent

  • Analytics is installed and records visits and key conversions, such as form submissions and purchases.
  • The cookie banner works and scripts respect visitors' choices.
  • Advertising conversion tracking is in place if you run paid campaigns.

Our GA4 setup guide explains how to set up conversion tracking.

Security and backups

  • The site loads over HTTPS with a valid certificate, with no mixed-content warnings.
  • Admin accounts use strong passwords and unnecessary accounts are removed.
  • Backups are configured and you know how to restore one.

For WordPress sites, see our WordPress security guide.

Who should test?

Developers should test as they build, but they are not the only people who should look. The person who built a feature knows how it is supposed to work and may unconsciously avoid the paths that break it. It helps to involve:

  • Someone from the business who knows the products, services and customers.
  • Someone unfamiliar with the project, who will use it like a new visitor.
  • Whoever handles enquiries or orders, to check they arrive in the right form.

Keep a shared list of issues with a description, page, device and screenshot for each, so nothing is lost and fixes can be checked off.

Launch day

When testing is complete and changes are approved, the site moves from staging to live. On launch day it is worth:

  • Choosing a quieter time rather than your busiest trading period.
  • Taking a full backup of the existing live site first.
  • Removing any search engine blocks and checking robots.txt.
  • Switching payment gateways out of test mode.
  • Repeating key tests on the live site: forms, a real low-value order if appropriate, and tracking.
  • Submitting the sitemap in Google Search Console and checking for errors over the following days.

Staging after launch

Staging is not only for launches. Once the site is live, it remains the safest place to test plugin and theme updates, new features, design changes and platform upgrades. Copy the live site to staging, apply the change, test the key journeys, then push it live. This is one of the main reasons ongoing website maintenance matters: updates are necessary for security, but applying them blind to a live site carries risk.

Be careful when pushing changes from staging to a live online shop. Copying the whole staging database over the live one can overwrite orders and customer data created since the copy was taken. Push code and design changes rather than the database, or plan the timing carefully.

FAQs

Do I need a staging site for a small website?

It is strongly advisable for anything beyond trivial text edits. Even simple sites can break after a plugin or theme update, and staging lets you check first.

Does Shopify have a staging site?

Not in the same way as WordPress. You can duplicate and preview themes, and some businesses use a separate development store for bigger changes.

How long should testing take?

It depends on the size and complexity of the site. An online shop with many products and payment options needs more testing than a small brochure site.

What is the most common launch mistake?

Leaving search engines blocked after moving from staging to live is a frequent and damaging one. Broken forms and missing redirects are also common.
