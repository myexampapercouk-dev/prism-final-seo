Physiotherapy Clinic Website Design: Bookings, Trust and Clarity

Website design for UK physiotherapy clinics: condition and treatment pages, online booking, HCPC registration details, insurer information and careful claims.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Patients usually arrive with a specific problem, so pages organised around conditions and treatments help them decide whether to book.
  • Make booking simple and honest: show what an initial assessment involves, how long it takes and where prices can be found.
  • Describe treatments carefully, show registration details accurately and avoid claims about outcomes that you cannot support.

Someone looking for a physiotherapist is usually in pain or recovering from an injury, often searching on a phone, and wants to know three things quickly: can this clinic help with my problem, when can I be seen, and how do I book. A physiotherapy clinic website that answers those questions clearly, while building trust in the clinicians, does far more than a generic brochure site.

This guide covers the design and content of the website itself. For local search, advertising, referral relationships and other promotion, see our guide to physiotherapy clinic marketing in the UK.

Start with the patient's journey

Most physio patients follow a similar path. They notice a problem, search for help, compare a few clinics, then book an initial assessment. Some come through a GP suggestion, an employer, a sports club or a private health insurer. Your site should make sense at each of those points.

  • A person searching "physio for back pain near me" needs to land on a page about back pain, not a generic home page.
  • An insured patient needs to know whether you work with their insurer and what they need before booking.
  • A returning patient just wants the booking link and the address.

A structure that suits many clinics

  • Conditions: pages for the problems you commonly treat, such as back pain, neck pain, knee injuries or post-operative rehabilitation.
  • Treatments and services: assessment, manual therapy, exercise rehabilitation, sports physiotherapy, and any other services your clinicians are qualified to offer.
  • Your first appointment: what happens, how long it takes, what to wear and what to bring.
  • Prices and insurance: fees or a clear link to them, plus how insured and self-funding patients book.
  • Our team: clinicians, their qualifications and areas of interest.
  • Book online, plus phone and email.
  • Location: address, parking, public transport and accessibility of the premises.

If you have more than one clinic, give each location its own page with its address, opening hours, team and directions. That helps patients and gives search engines clear local information.

Condition and treatment pages

Condition pages are often the most useful pages on a physio site, but they need care. Explain the problem in plain language, describe how an assessment works, outline the kinds of treatment that may be used and when someone should seek urgent medical help instead. Each page should end with a clear route to book.

Avoid promising outcomes. Phrases like "cure your back pain" or "guaranteed results" are not appropriate. Healthcare advertising in the UK is subject to the Advertising Standards Authority's rules and the CAP Code, which generally expect health claims to be backed by evidence. Keep language measured, such as "physiotherapy may help with", and make sure every treatment you describe is one your clinicians are trained and insured to provide.

Write each page for the condition it covers rather than reusing the same text with a different heading. Thin, repeated pages are unhelpful for patients and unlikely to perform well in search.

Showing qualifications and registration accurately

In the UK, "physiotherapist" and "physical therapist" are protected titles, and practitioners using them must be registered with the Health and Care Professions Council. Many patients and insurers will expect to see this. On your team page, list each clinician's role and qualifications, and consider stating their HCPC registration so patients can check it on the public register.

Only display memberships, accreditations or insurer recognition you actually hold, and keep them current. If a clinician leaves, update the team page promptly. If your clinic also employs sports therapists, massage therapists or other practitioners, describe their roles accurately so patients understand who they will see. Our guide to website trust signals covers how to present this kind of information well.

Online booking

Online booking can reduce phone calls and let patients book outside opening hours. Many clinics use a practice management system with a booking widget that can be embedded in the site. When designing around it:

  • Make the booking button visible on every page, especially on mobile.
  • Name appointment types clearly, for example "Initial assessment" and "Follow-up", with durations.
  • Explain any cancellation policy before the patient confirms.
  • Check that the embedded booking tool looks consistent with your site and works on a phone.
  • Offer a phone number for patients who prefer to talk to someone first.

Our guide to website online booking systems covers the wider choices.

Prices and insurance information

Patients appreciate clarity on cost. Whether you list fees on the site or explain them at booking, make it easy to understand what an initial assessment and a follow-up appointment cost. If you treat insured patients, explain the general process, such as whether patients need a pre-authorisation code from their insurer, and avoid listing insurers you are not recognised by. Keep the page updated whenever fees or arrangements change.

Health information and data protection

Contact forms and booking systems on a physio site can collect health information, which is special category data under UK GDPR. Collect only what you need before the appointment, make sure forms are secure, explain how information is used in a clear privacy notice, and check how your booking provider stores data. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance, and your professional body may also have advice. Avoid asking for detailed medical history through an ordinary contact form; that is usually better handled within your clinical system.

Reviews and patient stories

Reviews matter to people choosing a clinic, but they must be genuine. Do not write reviews yourself, edit them to change their meaning or show only selected quotes in a misleading way. If you share patient stories, get written consent, respect confidentiality and avoid implying that every patient will have the same outcome. Linking to your Google reviews is a straightforward way to show independent feedback.

Accessibility and the physical clinic

Many physio patients have limited mobility, are in pain or are older. The site should be easy to read and use: clear text, good contrast, large tap targets and simple forms. Our guide to website accessibility explains the basics.

Information about the premises is part of accessibility too. Say whether there is step-free access, a lift, nearby parking or accessible toilets. A patient on crutches needs to know before they arrive.

Photos and video

Real photos of your clinic, treatment rooms and team are more reassuring than stock images of athletes. Short videos explaining what happens at a first appointment, or demonstrating common exercises, can be useful, but should not replace individual clinical advice. Add a note that exercises should be done as directed by a clinician. Compress images and avoid auto-playing video so pages stay fast.

Local search signals on the site

Most physio searches are local. Use consistent clinic names, addresses and phone numbers across the site and your Google Business Profile, include a map and directions on each location page, and mention the areas you realistically serve without creating near-identical pages for every nearby town. Our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile go further.

Pages for referrers and employers

Some clinics receive patients through GPs, consultants, sports clubs or employers. If that is part of your work, a short page explaining how referrals work, what information you need and how you communicate with the referrer can be helpful. Keep it factual, describe only arrangements you actually have, and do not imply NHS involvement or partnerships that do not exist.

Common mistakes on physiotherapy websites

  • Booking buttons hidden or missing on mobile.
  • Condition pages that promise cures or results.
  • Team pages that are out of date or omit registration details.
  • Unclear prices and insurance arrangements.
  • Forms collecting detailed health information without proper safeguards.
  • No information on parking or step-free access.

FAQs

Should a physio website have a page for each condition?

For the main conditions you treat, yes. Each page should be genuinely useful and written for that condition, not repeated text.

Do I need to show HCPC registration on my website?

Physiotherapists must be HCPC registered to use the title. Showing registration details helps patients check them; confirm any specific requirements with your professional body.

Can I take bookings online?

Yes, most clinic management systems offer a booking widget that can be embedded. Make sure it works well on a phone.

Should I list my prices?

Clear pricing helps patients decide. If you do not list them, explain at booking what each appointment costs.

What can I say about treatment results?

Keep claims measured and evidence-based, in line with the ASA and CAP Code. Avoid promising cures or guaranteed outcomes.
