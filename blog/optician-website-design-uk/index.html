Optician Website Design: Eye Tests, Frames and Patient Trust

Website design for UK opticians and optometry practices: eye test booking, NHS and private services, frame ranges, contact lens pages and patient trust.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • For most independent opticians, the website's main job is to make booking an eye test simple and to explain what happens at the appointment.
  • Patients often want to know whether they qualify for an NHS-funded sight test, so explaining eligibility in plain terms, and pointing to official guidance, is genuinely useful.
  • Frames are a big part of the choice for many people, so good photography of your range and brands you stock can help a practice stand apart from larger chains.

An independent optician sits somewhere between a healthcare provider and a retailer. People come for an eye examination, but many leave having chosen frames, lenses or contact lenses as well. That mix shapes what a practice website needs to do. It has to reassure people that their eye health is in good hands, make booking straightforward, and give a feel for the frames and service that make an independent practice worth choosing over a high street chain.

This guide covers website design for UK opticians and optometry practices, from single-site independents to small groups with a few branches. It focuses on the website itself. For getting found in local search, see our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile, and for wider promotion of health services, our guide to healthcare marketing.

What an optician website needs to do

Before thinking about design, it helps to be clear about the jobs the site has to do. For most practices, those are:

  • Let people book an eye test, or at least request one, without needing to phone.
  • Explain the services on offer, from routine sight tests to specialist appointments.
  • Answer common questions about NHS eligibility, costs and what to bring.
  • Show the frames, lenses and contact lens options available.
  • Build trust in the clinical team and the practice.
  • Make the address, opening hours, parking and accessibility details easy to find.

Everything else, from blog posts to promotions, is secondary to those core tasks. A clean, well-organised site that does those jobs well will usually serve patients better than a busy one that tries to do everything.

Booking an eye test

Booking is often the most valuable feature on an optician site. Many patients, especially working adults, prefer to book outside opening hours. Options include:

  • Online booking linked to your diary, so patients can pick a slot themselves.
  • A booking request form, where patients suggest preferred times and the practice confirms.
  • Click to call on mobile, for those who would rather speak to someone.

Some practice management systems include an online booking feature that can be linked or embedded on your website. If yours does, the website design should make it prominent and easy to use on a phone. If it does not, a well-designed request form can work well, as long as you respond promptly. Our guide to online booking systems covers the general options.

Whichever route you choose, be clear about appointment types. A routine sight test, a contact lens fitting, a children's eye test and a specialist appointment may all need different lengths of time. Letting patients choose the right type upfront saves rescheduling later.

Explaining NHS and private eye tests

One of the most common questions is whether someone qualifies for a free NHS sight test. Eligibility rules differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, and they can change, so a practice website should be careful here. A sensible approach is to:

  • Summarise the main groups who may be eligible in plain language.
  • Make clear that rules vary by nation and that patients should check their own circumstances.
  • Link to official NHS guidance for the relevant nation rather than trying to reproduce every detail.
  • Explain what you offer privately and how that differs, if relevant.
  • Mention NHS optical vouchers towards glasses or lenses, again pointing to official guidance on eligibility.

Getting this right helps patients and reduces phone calls asking the same question. Keep this page up to date, and note when it was last reviewed so you remember to check it.

Service pages that answer real questions

Each main service deserves its own page or clearly separated section. Typical services might include:

  • Eye examinations for adults.
  • Children's eye tests.
  • Contact lens fittings and aftercare.
  • Additional imaging or scans, if your practice offers them.
  • Specialist services such as dry eye clinics, myopia management or low vision support, where you genuinely provide them.
  • Home visits, if your practice offers domiciliary eye care.

For each, explain who it is for, what happens during the appointment, roughly how long it takes and whether there is a cost. Avoid clinical jargon or explain it when you use it. Health information should be accurate and measured, without overstating what any test or treatment can achieve. If you describe eye conditions, keep it general and encourage people to book an examination rather than self-diagnose.

Separate pages also help search engines understand what you offer, which can support people finding you for searches like a children's eye test or a contact lens fitting in your town.

Showing frames and brands

For many independents, the frame selection is a genuine reason to visit. Chains often compete on price, while independents may compete on choice, quality and personal styling advice. The website is a chance to show that. Ideas include:

  • A frames page that showcases the styles and types of eyewear you stock.
  • Good photography of your displays and the practice itself.
  • A section on lens options, such as varifocals, thinner lenses or coatings, explained simply.
  • Information on sunglasses and prescription sunglasses, if you offer them.
  • Details of any styling or fitting help you provide.

If you mention the eyewear brands you stock, make sure you are permitted to use their names and images, and keep the list current. Suppliers sometimes provide marketing materials and guidance on how they can be used.

A full online shop for prescription glasses is a much bigger undertaking, with prescription handling and fitting to consider. For most independents, showcasing frames online and inviting people in to try them is more realistic. If you sell non-prescription items such as sunglasses or eye care products, a small shop on Shopify, WooCommerce or a website builder could work alongside the main site.

Contact lenses and repeat orders

Contact lens wearers are often regular customers. Your website can support them with:

  • A page explaining contact lens fittings, trials and aftercare appointments.
  • Details of any lens subscription or direct debit scheme you run.
  • A simple reorder request form for existing wearers.
  • Clear information about aftercare check frequency.

Contact lens supply is regulated, so be careful about how ordering works and follow professional guidance. If in doubt, check with the General Optical Council or your professional body before adding online ordering features.

Building patient trust

Patients are trusting you with their eye health, so trust details matter. Useful elements include:

  • A team page with photos and roles of your optometrists, dispensing opticians and support staff, with consent.
  • Professional registrations, described accurately. Optometrists and dispensing opticians in the UK are registered with the General Optical Council, and you can explain what that means.
  • Equipment or technology you genuinely use, described without exaggeration.
  • Genuine patient reviews, linked to where they can be read in full.
  • How long the practice has been part of the community, if that is a real strength.

Our guide to website trust signals covers this in more depth. The key is that everything shown should be accurate and verifiable.

Accessibility matters even more here

An optician's website will be visited by people with reduced vision, people who have just had their eyes dilated and older patients who may find small text difficult. Accessibility is important for any website, but it is especially fitting for a practice that cares for eyes. Consider:

  • Good colour contrast between text and backgrounds.
  • Comfortable default text sizes, and layouts that still work when text is enlarged.
  • Clear headings and a logical page structure for screen reader users.
  • Descriptive alt text for images.
  • Buttons and links large enough to tap easily.
  • Avoiding important information hidden only in images.

Our website accessibility guide covers the standards in more detail. Accessibility is also about the physical practice, so state whether there is step-free access, accessible parking or a hearing loop.

Practical details patients look for

Practical information is often what people actually came for. Make it easy to find:

  • Address with a map, and directions or parking notes.
  • Opening hours, including late evenings or Saturdays.
  • Phone number and email, tappable on mobile.
  • What to bring to an eye test, such as current glasses, a list of medications and any NHS eligibility proof.
  • Whether patients should avoid driving after certain tests.

For practices with more than one branch, give each location its own page with its own address, hours and team. This helps patients and supports local search.

Privacy and health data

Forms that collect health information, such as booking forms that ask about symptoms, involve sensitive personal data. Keep forms to what you genuinely need, explain how the information will be used, and make sure your privacy notice covers it. UK GDPR treats health data as a special category with extra protections, so it may be worth taking advice on how your forms and systems handle it. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for health organisations.

Choosing a platform

Most optician websites are service sites with a booking feature, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all be suitable. The decision often depends on how the site needs to connect with your practice management or booking system, how much content you plan to add, and who will update it. FAQs

Should opticians offer online booking?

For many practices, online booking or a booking request form saves time and suits patients who want to book outside opening hours. Check whether your practice management system offers a booking feature that can link to your website.

How should an optician website explain NHS eye tests?

Summarise who may be eligible in plain language, note that rules vary by UK nation, and link to official NHS guidance for patients to check their own circumstances.

Can opticians sell glasses online through their website?

It is possible but involves prescription handling and regulatory considerations. Many independents showcase frames online and invite people in to try them, or sell non-prescription items such as sunglasses.

Why is accessibility important for optician websites?

Many visitors will have reduced vision or have just had their eyes tested, so clear contrast, readable text and a simple structure are especially valuable.

Should each branch have its own page?

Yes, a separate page for each location with its own address, hours and team helps patients and can support local search visibility.
