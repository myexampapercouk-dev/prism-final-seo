Logistics and Courier Website Design: Quotes, Coverage and Trust

Website design for UK logistics, haulage and courier firms: service pages, coverage areas, quote forms, tracking links, compliance details and B2B trust.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Logistics buyers usually want to confirm quickly that you move their type of goods, cover their routes and can respond fast.
  • Separate pages for each service, such as same-day courier, pallet distribution or warehousing, tend to be easier to navigate and to rank than one long services page.
  • Licences, insurance and accreditations should be described accurately and only where you actually hold them, so check current requirements for your operation.

Logistics and courier companies often win work through relationships, tenders and repeat customers rather than through their website. Yet when a procurement manager, an ecommerce seller or a small business owner needs a new carrier, the website is frequently where they check whether a firm looks credible and capable. A clear, fast site that answers the practical questions can make the difference between a quote request and a closed tab.

This guide covers website design for UK logistics businesses, including same-day and multi-drop couriers, haulage firms, pallet network members, freight forwarders and warehousing or fulfilment providers. It focuses on the website itself. For household moves, see our separate guide to removals company website design, and for wider business-to-business promotion see our guide to B2B SEO.

Who visits a logistics website

A logistics site usually serves several quite different audiences:

  • Business buyers comparing carriers for regular contracts.
  • Small businesses or individuals needing a one-off urgent delivery.
  • Existing customers looking for tracking, invoices or a contact number.
  • Drivers, subcontractors or owner-drivers looking for work.
  • Recruits for warehouse, transport office or management roles.

Each group wants something different, so the navigation should make each route obvious. A common approach is to keep services and quotes front and centre, add a clear "Track a delivery" or "Customer login" link in the header, and give recruitment its own section so it does not compete with sales. Our guide to website navigation explains how to structure menus for mixed audiences.

Lead with what you move and where

The first question most buyers ask is whether you can handle their consignment. A homepage that opens with a vague slogan about "delivering excellence" tells them nothing. Instead, state plainly what you carry, how and where, for example: same-day courier services across the Midlands, or palletised freight nationwide with next-day options. Then point to the main services and a way to get a price. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise this content.

Give each service its own page

Logistics firms often offer a mix of services. Separate pages let you explain each properly and give search engines a clear page to show for each kind of search. Depending on what you actually offer, pages might cover:

  • Same-day and dedicated courier work.
  • Multi-drop and scheduled delivery routes.
  • Pallet distribution and part loads.
  • Full loads and haulage.
  • Temperature-controlled or specialist transport.
  • Warehousing, storage and order fulfilment.
  • International freight and customs support.

On each page, describe the vehicles or equipment used, typical lead times, size and weight limits, and the kinds of goods suitable or unsuitable. Only list services you provide directly or can genuinely arrange, and be clear where work is subcontracted or handled through a network if a customer would reasonably want to know.

Show coverage clearly

Coverage is central to a logistics buying decision. A simple statement, a list of regions or a map graphic showing where you collect and deliver helps visitors self-qualify. If you operate from a depot and offer local same-day work alongside national services, explain the difference. Location pages can be useful for areas you genuinely serve, but avoid creating dozens of near-identical town pages with only the place name changed, as these add little value for readers. Our guide to local SEO covers how to approach location content, and a Google Business Profile can support visibility for each real depot.

Make getting a quote easy

Quote requests are often the main conversion on a logistics site. The challenge is collecting enough detail to price the job without putting people off. A good quote form usually asks for:

  • Collection and delivery postcodes.
  • What is being moved, with approximate size, weight and quantity.
  • Timing, such as same day, next day or a specific date.
  • Whether this is a one-off job or regular work.
  • Contact details and preferred response method.

Consider separate routes for urgent one-off jobs, where a prominent phone number may serve better than a form, and for contract enquiries, where a longer form or a request for a call makes sense. Tell people when they can expect a reply. If you offer instant online pricing for standard parcels or pallets, make sure the calculator is accurate and that any exclusions, surcharges or conditions are visible before booking. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design in more detail.

Tracking and customer logins

Existing customers often visit simply to track a consignment or access a portal. If you use a transport management system or a network tracking page, link to it clearly from the header on every page. Keep the wording obvious, such as "Track a delivery", and make sure the link works well on a phone, since recipients and drivers may use it on the move. If your tracking runs on a separate system, check that the transition feels consistent and that customers can easily get back to your main site.

Build trust with business buyers

Companies handing over goods to a carrier want reassurance that they will arrive safely and on time. Useful trust content can include:

  • Your operating history and the people who run the business.
  • Real photos of your vehicles, depot and team rather than stock imagery.
  • Insurance cover in general terms, such as goods in transit and public liability, if you want to show it.
  • Accreditations, quality standards or industry memberships you actually hold.
  • Case studies or client testimonials, used only with permission and describing genuine work.

For goods vehicle operators, an operator's licence issued through the Traffic Commissioners is a legal requirement for many vehicle types, and some buyers will want to see that you hold one. Requirements depend on vehicle weight and how the vehicles are used, so check current guidance from the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency and GOV.UK before describing your licensing. Never claim an accreditation, membership or insurance level you do not hold. Our guide to website trust signals covers this in more depth.

Explain sector experience honestly

Some carriers specialise in particular kinds of goods, such as medical supplies, construction materials, automotive parts, ecommerce parcels or high-value items. If you have genuine capability in a sector, a dedicated page explaining your vehicles, handling procedures and any relevant training can help buyers in that sector find you. If you are a general carrier, it is better to say so than to create sector pages you cannot back up. Where specialist goods involve regulation, such as dangerous goods, describe your capability carefully and accurately.

Recruitment and driver pages

Many logistics firms are recruiting drivers and warehouse staff. A dedicated careers section can help, with:

  • Current vacancies with clear job details.
  • Information on licence categories or experience needed.
  • Whether you work with employed drivers, agency staff or self-employed owner-drivers.
  • A simple application form that works on mobile.

Keep vacancies up to date and remove filled roles. Be careful with how you describe self-employed arrangements, as employment status rules are complex; take advice if unsure. Collect only the personal data you need and explain how it will be used in your privacy notice, in line with UK GDPR.

Speed, mobile use and accessibility

Logistics customers and drivers often use websites on phones, sometimes with patchy signal. Keep pages lightweight, compress images and avoid heavy video on the homepage. Make phone numbers tappable and forms simple to complete with a thumb. Our guides to website speed optimisation and website accessibility cover practical steps.

Choosing a platform

Most logistics websites are content and lead generation sites rather than shops, so a content management system such as WordPress, or a builder like Squarespace or Wix for smaller operators, is often enough. If you need instant pricing, online booking and payment for standard services, you may need custom development or an integration with your transport management system. Weigh the flexibility you need against cost and ongoing maintenance. See our guides to website builders vs custom websites and website maintenance.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A homepage that never says what you move or where.
  • One crowded services page instead of clear individual pages.
  • Quote forms that ask for too much, or too little to price the job.
  • Hidden tracking links that frustrate existing customers.
  • Stock photos of lorries that are obviously not your fleet.
  • Out-of-date vacancies left live for months.

FAQs

What should a courier website include?

Clear service pages, coverage information, an easy quote route, a visible phone number for urgent jobs, tracking links for existing customers and genuine trust information.

Should a logistics company show prices online?

It depends on the service. Standard parcels or pallets may suit an online calculator, while contract work usually needs a tailored quote. Either way, be clear about what affects price.

Do logistics websites need location pages?

They can help for areas you genuinely serve, but thin pages that only swap the town name are best avoided.

Should I mention my operator's licence on my website?

Some buyers find it reassuring. Describe it accurately and check current GOV.UK guidance on licensing requirements for your vehicles.

Can my website handle driver recruitment?

Yes. A careers section with current vacancies and a simple mobile application form is a common approach.
