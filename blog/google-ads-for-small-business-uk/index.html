Google Ads for Small Business in the UK: How to Start Well

Google Ads for small business works best with tight targeting, high-intent keywords, a focused landing page and proper tracking. Here is a UK starter plan.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start narrow: a few high-intent keywords, a tight location and one campaign, rather than broad targeting that spends quickly on poor clicks.
  • Set up conversion tracking for calls, forms and sales before launch, so you can tell which searches bring customers.
  • Review search terms and add negative keywords every week; most small-account waste comes from irrelevant searches.

Google Ads for small business works best when you start narrow: a handful of high-intent keywords, tight location targeting, a landing page that matches the search, and tracking that counts calls, forms and sales. Done that way, even a modest budget can bring in customers who are actively looking for what you offer.

Done carelessly, the same budget can disappear on irrelevant clicks in days. This guide is for UK small business owners who want to set up Google Ads sensibly, whether they plan to run it themselves or hand it to someone else.

Why Google Ads suits many small businesses

Google search ads show to people who are searching right now. Someone typing "boiler repair Leeds" or "accountant for sole traders" has a need and is close to acting. That intent is what makes search ads valuable for small businesses: you are not persuading people to want something, you are helping them find you when they already do.

Search ads also complement organic search. SEO builds visibility over months, while ads can put you at the top of results quickly. Our guide to SEO for small business explains how the two work together.

Google Ads for small business: a starter plan

Here is the order we recommend for a first campaign.

  1. Define one goal. Calls, quote requests, bookings or online sales. Pick the one that matters most.
  2. Set up tracking first. Track calls from ads, form submissions and purchases. Without this, you cannot tell good spend from bad. Our GA4 setup guide covers the analytics side.
  3. Choose high-intent keywords. Focus on searches that show buying intent, such as the service plus a location, or phrases like "near me", "quote" or "price".
  4. Set tight locations. Target only the areas you serve, and choose the setting that reaches people in those areas rather than people interested in them.
  5. Write specific ads. Mention the service, the area and a reason to choose you that you can genuinely back up.
  6. Send clicks to a matching page. A page about the exact service searched for, not your home page.
  7. Start with a budget you can hold. Run it for several weeks so you gather enough data to judge.
  8. Review weekly. Check the search terms, add negatives and pause what does not work.

Keywords and negative keywords

Keywords tell Google which searches you want to appear for. Match types control how closely a search must match. Broad match casts a wide net and relies on Google's automation; phrase and exact match give tighter control. For a small budget, tighter control usually wins at the start.

Negative keywords are just as important. They stop your ads from showing on searches that will never become customers. Common examples include:

  • "free", "DIY" or "how to", if you sell a paid service
  • "jobs", "salary", "course" or "apprenticeship", if you are not recruiting
  • Areas you do not serve
  • Services you do not offer that sound similar to ones you do

The search terms report shows the real searches behind your clicks. Reviewing it weekly is one of the simplest ways to cut waste.

What will it cost?

Google Ads has no fixed price. You set a daily budget and pay when people click, with click prices set by a live auction that varies by sector, location, time and ad quality. Some local niches have modest click prices; competitive sectors such as legal or finance can be expensive. Treat any figure you read as a rough guide only.

Landing pages: where small budgets are won or lost

A click only has value if the page it lands on turns the visitor into an enquiry or sale. For a small business, a good landing page usually has:

  • A headline that matches the search, such as the service and area
  • A clear phone number and short form above the fold on mobile
  • Reasons to trust you that are true, such as years trading, accreditations you genuinely hold or genuine reviews
  • Fast loading on a phone
  • One clear next step

Measuring results week by week

Google Ads reports a lot of metrics, but a small business only needs to watch a few closely. The most useful are cost per conversion, the number of genuine enquiries or sales, and the quality of those leads. A campaign that produces fewer leads at a slightly higher cost can still be the better one if those leads turn into paying customers more often.

Keep a simple record each week: spend, conversions reported by Google, and real enquiries or orders received. Compare the two. If Google reports far more conversions than you actually receive, your tracking may be counting the wrong actions, such as page views or duplicate form submissions. If you receive more than Google reports, calls or offline sales may not be tracked yet.

It is also worth asking new customers how they found you. Some people click an ad, leave, and call later from a different device, so not every result shows neatly in the platform.

Planning for seasonality

Many small businesses have busy and quiet periods. A garden landscaper may be busiest in spring, a heating engineer in autumn, and a gift shop before Christmas. Plan budgets around those patterns rather than spending the same amount every month. Raising budgets a little ahead of your peak, and trimming them in quieter months, usually makes better use of a limited budget than a flat spend all year. Review last year's enquiries to see when demand really starts to rise.

FAQs

Is Google Ads for small business worth it?

Often, yes, particularly for local services and businesses whose customers search when they need something. It works best with tight targeting, tracking and a matching landing page, and with realistic expectations about the time it takes to refine.

How much should a small business spend on Google Ads?

There is no set amount. Work out what you can afford to pay for a customer, check roughly what clicks cost in your area, and set a budget you can hold for several weeks so you gather useful data.

Can I run Google Ads myself?

Yes. Many small businesses do. The key habits are tracking conversions, reviewing search terms weekly, adding negative keywords and resisting automated suggestions that broaden targeting without clear benefit.

Should I use Performance Max as a small business?

It can work, but it relies heavily on good conversion tracking and quality assets. Many small service businesses find a well-built search campaign is a clearer starting point.

Do Google Ads help my SEO?

Not directly. Paying for ads does not improve organic rankings. However, ads data can show which searches convert, which can inform your SEO and content plans.

How quickly will I get leads from Google Ads?

Ads can show as soon as they are approved, so enquiries can start quickly. Building a consistently profitable account usually takes longer, with results typically improving over three to six months.
