Ecommerce Website Design in the UK: How to Plan a Shop That Sells

Planning an ecommerce website design for a UK brand? Navigation, product pages, basket and checkout, mobile shopping, UK delivery and returns, and platforms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Ecommerce design is about helping shoppers find, judge and buy products with as little friction as possible.
  • Product pages and checkout usually matter more than the home page.
  • UK shoppers expect clear delivery costs, returns information and familiar payment options.

An online shop is judged differently from a brochure website. Visitors are not just reading; they are comparing, hesitating and deciding whether to hand over their card details. Good ecommerce design removes the reasons people leave before buying, from confusing menus to surprise delivery charges at checkout.

Prism Works Studio is a UK ecommerce, web design and digital marketing agency working remotely with brands across the country. This guide covers the design decisions that shape how a shop performs. If budget is your first question, our ecommerce website cost guide covers that separately.

Start with how your customers shop

A shop selling a few hero products needs a very different structure from one with thousands of SKUs. Before thinking about layouts, answer a few questions:

  • Do customers know exactly what they want, or do they browse?
  • Do they shop by category, by occasion, by size, by brand or by problem?
  • Are most visits on mobile?
  • Do customers buy once, or come back regularly to reorder?
  • Do you sell to trade customers as well as consumers?

The answers shape navigation, filters, product page layout and even whether accounts and subscriptions are worth offering.

Navigation and collections

Menus should use the words shoppers use, not internal product codes or clever names. Keep top-level categories few and clear, and use collections to group products in ways that match how people think. For larger catalogues, filters for size, colour, price and key attributes matter enormously, and search needs to handle misspellings and synonyms well.

Product pages do the selling

Many shoppers land directly on a product page from search or ads, never seeing the home page. Each product page needs to answer the questions that stand between interest and purchase.

Basket and checkout

Checkout is where small annoyances cost real money. Common causes of abandoned baskets include unexpected delivery costs, forced account creation, long forms and limited payment options. Design choices that tend to help:

  • Show delivery costs or thresholds early, not just at the final step
  • Allow guest checkout
  • Use UK postcode lookup to speed up address entry
  • Offer payment methods your customers expect, such as cards and popular digital wallets
  • Keep reassurance, such as returns information and secure payment, visible

Our ecommerce CRO guide covers testing and improving checkout over time.

Designing for mobile first

For many UK shops, most visits come from phones. That means thumb-friendly buttons, sticky add-to-basket bars, images that zoom easily, filters that work in a compact drawer and pages that load fast on mobile data. Design and review mobile layouts first, then expand to desktop, rather than squeezing a desktop design onto a small screen.

UK-specific details

  • Prices shown in pounds, including VAT where you sell to consumers
  • Clear delivery options and cut-off times
  • Returns information that reflects consumer rights for online purchases; check the current rules or take advice, as this is not legal advice
  • Delivery information for Northern Ireland, Highlands and Islands, and international orders if you serve them
  • UK spelling and terminology throughout

Brand, without getting in the way

A distinctive brand helps a shop stand out from marketplaces and lookalike competitors. Strong photography, a consistent tone and a clear story on the about page all build trust. The risk is design that prioritises looking impressive over being easy to shop: autoplay video, heavy animations and oversized hero banners that push products below the fold. The best ecommerce design feels branded and still gets out of the way.

Accessibility and speed

Interactive elements such as filters, galleries and basket drawers need to work with keyboards and screen readers. Clear focus styles, labelled form fields and sensible contrast help every shopper, not only disabled customers. Speed matters too, as heavy themes and too many apps slow pages down. See our speed optimisation guide.

Choosing a platform

Most UK brands choose between Shopify and WooCommerce, with custom builds for unusual requirements. Shopify suits brands that want a hosted, well-supported platform; WooCommerce suits businesses already on WordPress that want more control. Our Shopify website cost guide and WooCommerce to Shopify migration guide explore the trade-offs.

Plan for SEO from the start

Category and product pages are often the pages that rank for buying searches. Give them unique copy, sensible URLs and clear headings, and plan redirects if you are replacing an existing shop. Our ecommerce SEO checklist covers the essentials.

Search and filtering on larger catalogues

Once a shop grows beyond a few dozen products, on-site search and filters become some of the most used features on the site. Shoppers who search often know what they want, so a search box that returns nothing for a slight misspelling, or that cannot match "trainers" to "sneakers", loses easy sales. Review what people type into your search, look for queries that return no results and decide whether to add synonyms, redirects to the right collection or new products.

Filters need equal care. Only show filters that make sense for the collection being viewed, display how many products match each option and make it easy to clear choices. On mobile, a filter drawer that resets every time it closes is a common frustration worth testing for.

Trust signals that are honest

A shopper visiting an unfamiliar brand is asking whether it is safe to buy. Visible contact details, a real returns policy, clear company information and genuine reviews all help answer that. What does not help is invented urgency, fake stock counters or badges for awards and partnerships a business does not hold. These can damage trust when shoppers notice and may fall foul of consumer protection rules. Keep claims accurate and let the experience of shopping do the persuading.

Planning content beyond products

Ecommerce sites are often designed around the product grid, with content as an afterthought. Yet size guides, care instructions, buying guides and delivery pages answer the questions that otherwise turn into support emails or abandoned baskets. Plan where these will live in the navigation and how product pages will link to them. Buying guides can also earn search visits from shoppers who are still researching.

After launch: keep improving

An ecommerce design is never truly finished. Once the shop is live, watch where shoppers drop out, which products get views but few sales and what people search for without finding. Small, measured changes to product pages, navigation and checkout, tested one at a time, usually teach you more than a full redesign every few years.

Briefing a designer

A good ecommerce brief does not need to be long, but it should cover your products and catalogue size, your customers and how they shop, the platform you are considering, systems the shop must connect to, such as stock, accounting or fulfilment, and how you will measure success. Share examples of what you like and dislike, and explain why. The clearer the brief, the more useful the proposals you receive.

FAQs

What matters most in ecommerce website design?

Usually product pages and checkout. They are where shoppers decide whether to buy, and small problems there can cost sales.

Should I design for mobile or desktop first?

For most consumer shops, mobile first, because that is where a large share of visits happen. Check your own analytics to confirm.

Is a custom theme better than an off-the-shelf one?

Not always. A well-chosen theme, carefully customised, suits many brands. Custom design makes more sense when you have specific requirements or a strong brand that a theme cannot express.

Do I need customer accounts?

Offer them if customers reorder often or you run subscriptions or loyalty schemes, but always allow guest checkout as well.
