Shopify Store Management UK: What It Covers and How to Choose a Provider

What Shopify store management covers, which tasks UK retailers should keep or outsource, how to compare providers and what to agree before giving anyone access.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify store management is ongoing help with running a store, such as product updates, theme changes, app reviews, promotions and reporting.
  • Keep decisions that depend on your brand and customers close to home, and consider outsourcing technical or repetitive work your team has no time for.
  • Before you sign, agree the scope, response times, staff access, ownership and how out-of-scope work will be charged.

Shopify store management is ongoing help with running an online store: keeping products and collections up to date, making theme changes, reviewing apps, setting up promotions and reporting on performance. For UK retailers it can free up time and fill skills gaps once a store has become too demanding to run alongside everything else. The right arrangement depends on which jobs your team should keep, which are better handed over and how clearly the provider defines what is included.

This guide covers what management usually includes and how to compare providers. If you need a new build or a larger project instead, read our guide to choosing a Shopify agency in the UK.

What Shopify store management covers

There is no standard package, so treat the list below as a set of areas to ask about rather than as a description of what every provider offers.

  • Product and collection updates: adding products, editing descriptions and images, organising collections and keeping details accurate.
  • Theme changes: adjusting sections and layouts, and fixing display problems.
  • App management: reviewing what is installed, removing what is unused and dealing with conflicts.
  • Promotions: setting up discounts, seasonal pages and campaign landing pages.
  • Checks and troubleshooting: regularly testing the journey from product page to checkout and looking into faults.
  • Settings support: helping with shipping, tax, payment and policy settings, with your team or advisers confirming the decisions.
  • Speed and SEO housekeeping: watching for slow pages, broken links, missing details and redirect needs.
  • Reporting: summarising sales, traffic and conversion data, with recommendations.

Some providers also manage marketing, such as email, paid ads or SEO, either as part of the same arrangement or separately. Ask where store management ends and marketing begins, so that nobody assumes the other party is responsible.

What to keep and what to outsource

Handing over everything is rarely the best answer. Your team knows your customers, suppliers and stock in a way an outside provider does not.

Bulk product updatesIf you have the capacityOften

A useful test is to ask whether a task needs knowledge of your business or knowledge of Shopify. Tasks requiring the first tend to stay with you, and tasks requiring the second are stronger candidates to hand over.

Signs you could use store management help

  • Small fixes wait for weeks because nobody has the time or the know-how.
  • You have a pile of apps and are unsure which ones you still need.
  • Product information is inconsistent or out of date.
  • Promotions go live late or need last-minute rescues.
  • You have sales data but rarely turn it into decisions.
  • The store depends on one person, and everything stops when they are away.

If the problem is a poor design or a badly built theme, ongoing management will not cure it. In that case, a one-off project may need to come first, followed by regular support.

How to compare store management providers

  • Scope: does the proposal list actual tasks, or just promise to "manage your store"?
  • Capacity: is there a clear monthly allowance, and what happens to any unused time?
  • Response times: how quickly will they respond to urgent faults, and how do they define urgent?
  • Coverage at busy times: can they support your peak periods, such as Black Friday or your own seasonal rush?
  • Shopify experience: have they worked with stores similar to yours in size and complexity?
  • Change control: do they test changes before publishing them to your live theme?
  • Proactive input: will they bring ideas and warn you of problems, or only work through your task list?
  • Exit terms: how much notice is needed, and what will they hand over if you leave?

Staff access and security

A store manager needs access to your Shopify admin, which can include customer and order information. Treat that seriously from the outset.

  • Stay the owner of your Shopify store and its billing.
  • Give access through Shopify's staff or collaborator options, rather than sharing your own login.
  • Grant only the permissions each person needs, where the platform allows it.
  • Ask how the provider handles customer data, and make sure your contract covers it.
  • Keep a record of apps installed and who approved them, as apps can also access store data.
  • Remove access promptly when someone leaves or the work ends.

Shopify's account options can change, so check its current help pages. If you are unsure of your data protection duties, consult current ICO guidance or take advice.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. Which tasks are included each month, and which cost extra?
  2. How are requests submitted, tracked and prioritised?
  3. How do you handle urgent problems outside normal hours?
  4. How do you test theme and app changes before they go live?
  5. Who will actually work on our store?
  6. Do you recommend apps you have a commercial relationship with?
  7. What do your reports include, and how will they help us decide what to do next?
  8. Who owns any custom code or theme work you produce?
  9. What is the notice period, and what happens at handover?

What to prepare before you start

A provider will get up to speed faster, and you will get better value, if you have the following ready.

  • A list of recurring tasks and how long they take today.
  • A list of known faults and frustrations, roughly in order of importance.
  • Your promotional calendar and busy periods.
  • Installed apps, with any you know are essential marked clearly.
  • Brand guidelines and examples of good product copy.
  • The name of the person who will approve changes and make decisions.

Before a provider makes any changes, confirm that a copy of your theme has been kept. It gives you something to return to if an update causes a problem.

It is also worth agreeing what good looks like in the first few months. That could mean clearing a backlog of known faults, tidying your apps or getting a promotion live without last-minute stress. Simple, practical aims like these give both sides something concrete to review, and they make it easier to judge whether the arrangement is worth continuing. Review them together regularly and adjust the scope as your store changes.

Warning signs

  • A monthly fee with no clear list of what it covers.
  • Requests to use your own login or to transfer store ownership.
  • Promises of specific sales increases.
  • Changes published to your live store without being checked.
  • A habit of adding apps to solve every problem, which can raise costs and slow your store.
  • Reports full of figures with no recommendations.

FAQs

Is store management the same as Shopify marketing?

Not usually. Management keeps the store accurate and working well, whereas marketing brings people to it. Some providers do both, so confirm which is included in your agreement.

Do I need a monthly plan or occasional help?

If you have regular updates or an active promotional calendar, a monthly plan can make sense. If your needs are occasional, ad hoc support may be enough. Reviewing the past few months of tasks should make the choice clearer.

Can a store manager handle my customer service?

Some might, but it is often a separate role that calls for knowledge of your products and policies. If you do outsource it, agree tone, response times and how complaints or refunds get handled.

Will I lose control of my store?

You should not. Keep ownership of the store and billing, grant only the access needed and agree who approves changes before they go live.

Can store management improve my sales?

It can support sales by removing faults, keeping product information accurate and making promotions run smoothly. Results also depend on your products, pricing and marketing, so treat any promise of specific growth with caution.

Can you manage a store you did not build?

Providers commonly take on existing stores. Expect them to review the theme, the apps and the settings first, because earlier work can affect what is safe to change.
