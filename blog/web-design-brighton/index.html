Web Design in Brighton: Websites for Independent Retailers and Wellness Practitioners

Web design for Brighton independent shops, makers and wellness practitioners: sites that join up shop and online sales, bookings and a distinctive local voice.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Brighton independents often sell in a physical shop and online at once, so the website should keep stock, opening times and collection options in step with the shop.
  • Wellness practitioners need booking-led sites that explain sessions plainly, set expectations honestly and avoid health claims that cannot be backed up.
  • Prism Works Studio works with Brighton businesses remotely, planning, writing and building sites over video calls and shared drafts.

Web design in Brighton has a particular challenge: many of the businesses that make the city feel like Brighton are small, personal and strongly opinionated about how they look. An independent shop in the North Laine, a maker selling homeware from a studio in Kemptown, a yoga teacher running classes in Hove or a massage therapist working from a treatment room near Preston Park all want a website that sounds like them, not like a template. At the same time, the site has to do practical work: take orders, take bookings and answer the same questions every day so the owner does not have to. Prism Works Studio works with Brighton businesses remotely, so planning, writing and design reviews happen over video calls and shared documents.

This guide focuses on two groups that sit at the heart of the local economy: independent retailers who sell both in person and online, and wellness practitioners who need people to book. If your business is more of a creative studio or agency, much of the advice on portfolios in our Bristol web design guide applies as well.

When the shop and the website are one business

A Brighton independent rarely has an ecommerce operation and a shop as separate things. It is the same owner, the same stock and often the same back room. The website problems that cause the most friction tend to come from the two drifting apart: an item shown as available online that sold over the counter that morning, opening hours that changed for winter but not on the site, or a click-and-collect option nobody remembered to switch off over a holiday.

Planning the site around the way the shop actually runs avoids most of this:

  • One stock record. If you sell in person and online, a platform that links your till and your online catalogue keeps quantities in step. Shopify, for example, offers point of sale tools for this; our guide to Shopify POS for UK retail covers how it works.
  • Clear fulfilment choices. Delivery, local collection and in-store reservation should each be explained once, in plain words, with any cut-off times.
  • Opening hours you can edit in seconds. Seasonal changes, bank holidays and the odd closed day for a buying trip should not need a developer.
  • Honest stock messages. If something is one of a kind or handmade to order, say so on the product page rather than letting a customer find out after paying. Our guide to stock availability display goes into the options.

Keeping a distinctive voice without losing clarity

Brighton shoppers are used to independents with personality, and a site that feels corporate can undersell a shop that is anything but. The trick is to put the personality where it helps and keep the functional parts predictable.

Where personality helpsWhere predictability helps
Homepage introduction and about pageNavigation labels and menu structure
Product descriptions and maker storiesBasket, checkout and account pages
Photography, colour and illustrationDelivery, returns and contact information
Email newsletters and blog postsSize guides, care instructions and prices

A playful product description can sell an item; a playful checkout button label usually just makes people hesitate. Our articles on writing product descriptions and tone of voice cover how to keep a consistent style across a site.

Telling the maker story properly

Many Brighton shops stock work by local or small-scale makers, and many makers sell directly. Where that is true, the story behind a product is part of its value. A short maker profile, photos of the workspace, and a note on materials and process can help a customer understand why a mug or print costs what it does.

Only say what is true and checkable. Claims such as "sustainable", "eco-friendly" or "plastic-free" should reflect what you actually do, as the Competition and Markets Authority has published guidance on environmental claims that businesses are expected to follow. Specific statements, such as what packaging is made from, are both more useful and easier to stand behind than general labels.

Websites for wellness practitioners

Brighton and Hove have a broad wellness community: yoga and pilates teachers, massage and sports therapists, counsellors, nutrition coaches, breathwork and meditation teachers, and practitioners of complementary therapies. Their websites share a job that is quite different from a shop's: helping someone who may be nervous, in pain or unsure what they need decide to book a first session.

A booking-led site for a practitioner usually needs:

  1. A plain explanation of each service, who it suits and what happens in a session.
  2. Session length, location and how to prepare, so nothing feels like a surprise.
  3. Prices or a link to them, plus cancellation terms stated clearly.
  4. An introduction to the practitioner, including relevant qualifications and memberships genuinely held.
  5. Online booking or a simple enquiry form, with a note on response times.

Our guide to online booking systems compares the approaches, and our yoga and pilates studio and therapist website design guides go deeper into those specialisms.

Careful wording on health and wellbeing

Wellness sites are where enthusiasm most easily turns into claims a business cannot support. Saying a therapy can treat or cure a condition is a different matter from describing how a session works and how clients often describe feeling afterwards. The Advertising Standards Authority and the Committee of Advertising Practice publish guidance on health claims in marketing, including on websites, and it is worth reading before you write service pages.

As a general approach, which is not legal advice:

  • Describe what you do and what a session involves rather than promising outcomes.
  • Avoid suggesting your service replaces medical advice, and consider adding a note encouraging people to speak to their GP where relevant.
  • Only use client testimonials that are genuine and that the client has agreed to share, and check they do not make claims you could not make yourself.
  • If you are regulated or registered with a professional body, follow its rules on how you describe your work.

Sensitive information and enquiry forms

Practitioners often need some health information before a first session, such as injuries, medication or pregnancy. That kind of data is treated as special category data under UK GDPR, so think carefully about whether you collect it through the website at all, or later through a more secure intake process. If you do collect it online, store it securely, limit who can see it and explain your handling in your privacy notice. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance aimed at small organisations. Our article on website contact forms covers how to keep forms short and clear.

Local search in Brighton and Hove

Both shops and practitioners benefit from showing up when someone nearby searches. Local visibility depends partly on the website and partly on a well-maintained Google Business Profile.

  • Use the names people search for. Brighton and Hove are often searched separately, and neighbourhood names such as Kemptown, Hove or the Lanes can matter if that is genuinely where you are.
  • Keep your name, address and phone details consistent between the website, your Google Business Profile and any directories.
  • Give each service its own page rather than one page listing everything, so each can match specific searches.
  • Add photos that show the real place, from your shop front to your treatment room, so people know what to expect.

Practitioners who work from home or visit clients may not want a public address on their profile, and Google offers a service-area option for that situation. Our local SEO guide and Google Business Profile guide explain the setup.

Choosing a platform

For a shop selling physical products, Shopify and WooCommerce are both strong options; the choice tends to come down to how much control you want and who will maintain the site. For a practitioner who mainly needs information pages and bookings, a simpler WordPress or Squarespace site with a booking tool can be enough. Our comparison of Shopify and WooCommerce and our guide to website builders versus custom sites can help you decide.

Photography that shows the real thing

Stock photography works against a Brighton independent. Customers want to see the actual products, the actual shop and the actual person they will meet. Product photos on a plain background help shoppers compare items, while lifestyle shots in the shop or a local setting give context. For practitioners, a warm, natural portrait and a photo of the room where sessions happen do more to reduce nerves than any amount of abstract imagery. FAQs

Can my shop till and website share the same stock?

Yes, if you choose a platform that supports it. Shopify's point of sale tools, for example, can keep in-store and online quantities in step, which reduces the risk of selling the same item twice.

Should a small Brighton shop sell online at all?

It depends on your products and time. Some shops sell their full range, others list a curated selection or offer local collection only. Starting small and growing the online range is a reasonable approach.

What can a wellness practitioner say about results on their website?

Be careful with claims about treating or curing conditions. Describe what a session involves and check the Advertising Standards Authority and Committee of Advertising Practice guidance on health claims before publishing.

Is it safe to collect health information in a booking form?

Health details are special category data under UK GDPR. Collect only what you need, store it securely and explain your handling in a privacy notice, or gather it through a separate intake process instead.

Do I need to show my home address if I work from home?

Not necessarily. Google Business Profile has a service-area option for businesses that do not want a public address, and your website can say you share the location once a booking is confirmed.
