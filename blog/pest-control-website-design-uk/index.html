Pest Control Website Design: Pest Pages, Discretion and Commercial Contracts

Pest control website design for UK firms: pest-by-pest pages, discreet service wording, safety information, commercial contract pages and honest local detail.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most pest control visitors search for the pest, not the service, so a page for each pest you treat often does more work than a single generic services page.
  • Discretion matters: many householders and businesses are embarrassed about an infestation, so explain unmarked visits or quiet arrival only if you genuinely offer them.
  • Keep claims about chemicals, safety and regulation accurate and hedged, and separate domestic call-outs from commercial contract work.

People rarely search for "pest control" in the abstract. They search because they have heard scratching in the loft, found droppings behind the kitchen units, spotted a wasp nest by the back door or been bitten in the night. Their first question is usually "what is this, and can someone get rid of it?" A pest control website that answers that question, pest by pest, in calm and practical language, is in a strong position. One that only lists "rats, mice, wasps, insects" in a single line leaves the visitor to work it out alone.

This guide is about the website itself: how to structure it around pests, how to handle discretion and safety, and how to present commercial work. For wider promotion such as reviews and local listings, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK, and for the basics of being found locally, our local SEO guide.

Structure the site around pests, not just services

A visitor who types "mice in the kitchen" or "wasp nest removal" into Google wants a page about exactly that. A page for each pest you treat gives you somewhere to answer the real questions and gives search engines a clear signal about what each page covers. Typical pest pages, where you treat them, include:

  • Rats and mice, often the most common domestic and commercial enquiry, and worth separate pages because the signs and treatment differ.
  • Wasps and hornets, which are seasonal and urgent, so the page should be quick to scan on a phone.
  • Bed bugs, where discretion and preparation instructions matter a great deal.
  • Fleas, cockroaches, ants and moths, each with its own signs and typical causes.
  • Birds and squirrels, where proofing and deterrence often matter more than treatment.

Each page can follow a similar pattern: how to recognise the problem, why it tends to happen, what your treatment generally involves, how many visits it typically takes, what the customer needs to do before and after, and how to book. Write in general terms about method and avoid promising a fixed number of visits or a guaranteed outcome unless that genuinely reflects your service terms.

Identification help without diagnosis by website

Many visitors are not sure what they have. Short sections on signs, such as droppings, gnaw marks, smells, nests or bites, help them decide which page to read and whether to call. Photos can help, but use your own where possible and avoid anything gratuitous. Make clear that identification on a website is only a guide and that a visit confirms the situation.

Some firms offer a way to send a photo for an initial view. If you do, explain how it works and how quickly you reply, and make sure the photo upload sits within a secure form covered by your privacy notice. Our guide to website contact forms covers file uploads and good form practice.

Discretion as a selling point

An infestation can feel embarrassing. Householders may worry about what the neighbours will think, and a restaurant or hotel will worry about its reputation. If you offer discreet service, say so clearly and explain what it means in practice, for example unmarked vehicles, plain clothing or flexible appointment times. Only describe what you actually do. A vague promise of discretion is less reassuring than a specific, true one.

The same applies to the website itself. Avoid publishing photos or case descriptions that could identify a customer's home or business, and get permission before using any images taken on a job.

Safety information, carefully worded

Customers with children, pets or health conditions often want to know whether treatment is safe. This is a reasonable concern and deserves a clear answer, but it is also an area where overclaiming can cause real problems. Some points to keep in mind:

  • Explain that you will discuss children, pets and vulnerable occupants before treatment, if that is your practice.
  • Describe precautions in general terms, such as how bait stations are placed or how long to keep rooms clear, rather than making blanket statements.
  • Avoid words like "completely safe", "non-toxic" or "harmless" unless you can fully support them for the specific products and methods used.
  • Be careful with claims about "eco-friendly" or "natural" treatments. Environmental claims are covered by UK consumer law and guidance from the Competition and Markets Authority, so they should be specific and backed up.

Rules on the use of pesticides and rodenticides in the UK are set by government and enforced by bodies such as the Health and Safety Executive, and some products are restricted to trained professional users. If you mention training, certification or stewardship requirements, describe your own position accurately and check current requirements rather than relying on general statements. Where the law is relevant, phrase it as information rather than legal advice.

Wildlife and protected species

Some animals people consider pests are protected, at least in certain circumstances. Bats are a well-known example, and there are rules around wild birds and their nests. A short, carefully hedged note explaining that you cannot treat protected species, and what customers can do instead, shows knowledge and responsibility. If you work around nesting seasons or offer proofing instead of removal, explain this on the relevant pages. Suggest customers check official guidance from the relevant UK government body for their nation where the rules matter.

Domestic call-outs and commercial contracts

Domestic and commercial customers want very different things, and a single services page rarely serves both well.

  • Domestic customers usually want a one-off problem solved. They need to know your area, availability, how pricing works and what happens on the visit.
  • Commercial customers, such as food businesses, landlords, warehouses, schools and offices, often want ongoing contracts with routine inspections and records. They may need documentation to show an inspector or auditor.

A dedicated commercial section can explain the sectors you work with, what a typical contract can include if you offer one, how reporting works and how to request a site survey. Be careful not to promise that your service guarantees a pass in a food hygiene inspection or audit; it is fairer to say your records can support the business in showing what pest control is in place.

If you work for letting agents and landlords, a short page on tenancy situations can help, though avoid giving legal views on who is responsible for pest control between landlord and tenant, as that can depend on the tenancy and the circumstances.

Pricing and booking

Many pest control visitors want a rough idea of cost before they call. You do not need to publish fixed prices, but explaining how you charge helps: whether a treatment includes follow-up visits, whether wasp nest removal is priced differently, whether there is a call-out charge and whether figures include VAT. Keep any published prices up to date and avoid misleading "from" figures. Our guide to pricing page design covers presentation.

Wasp work in particular suits online booking during summer. If you take bookings online, show real availability and make sure the system confirms clearly. Our guide to website online booking systems looks at the options. For urgent jobs, a visible phone number and tap-to-call button remain essential.

Seasonality and content

Pest problems change with the seasons: wasps in late summer, mice moving indoors as the weather cools, and so on. You can plan your website around this by updating the homepage messaging through the year and publishing useful, practical content ahead of each season. Seasonal advice pages, written plainly, can be helpful for visitors and give you something genuinely worth sharing. Avoid scare tactics; worried customers respond better to calm, factual help.

Coverage areas and local honesty

List the areas you genuinely cover, and consider a short page for each main area only if you can say something useful about it, such as typical property types or response times. Thin, duplicated town pages claiming a local base you do not have can mislead customers and may conflict with Google's guidelines for local businesses. Keep your name, phone and address consistent with your Google Business Profile.

Mobile, speed and reviews

Someone standing in the garden looking at a wasp nest will be on their phone. Pages need to load fast, with the phone number and booking button visible straight away. Our guides to mobile website design and website speed optimisation cover this. Show genuine reviews honestly, with permission where you quote named customers; fake or misleadingly edited reviews are prohibited under UK consumer law.

Platform choice

A pest control site is mostly content: pest pages, service areas and a commercial section. WordPress handles this well and makes it easy to add seasonal content. Squarespace or Wix can suit a smaller operation with fewer pages. If you sell DIY products online, an ecommerce platform such as Shopify or WooCommerce could be added, though many firms focus on treatment services rather than product sales.

FAQs

Should I have a separate page for each pest?

Usually, yes, for the pests you treat regularly. Visitors search by pest, and a dedicated page lets you answer their specific questions.

Can I say my treatments are safe for pets and children?

Be careful. Explain the precautions you take and that you discuss occupants before treatment, rather than making blanket safety claims.

Should I publish prices?

You do not have to, but explaining how you charge, what is included and whether VAT applies helps customers decide to call.

How should I handle commercial customers?

Give them their own section covering contracts, inspections and reporting, without promising outcomes in inspections or audits.

Do I need to mention protected species?

A short, hedged note is helpful if customers may contact you about animals such as bats or nesting birds, pointing them to official guidance.
