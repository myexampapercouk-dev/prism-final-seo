Nursery Website Design: Helping Parents Choose With Confidence

Website design for UK nurseries and preschools: room pages, inspection reports, funded hours, fees, safeguarding information, visits and waiting lists.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Parents choosing a nursery want to know their child will be safe, happy and learning, so the website should show the rooms, the team and a typical day honestly.
  • Funded hours and fees are confusing for many parents, so clear, carefully worded information about what you offer saves time for everyone.
  • Booking a visit or joining a waiting list should be easy to find on every page, especially on mobile.

Leaving a young child with someone else for the first time is a big step, and parents take the choice of nursery seriously. Many will shortlist a few settings online before visiting, often months ahead of when they need a place. The nursery website is usually their first real look at what you are like. It needs to answer practical questions about places, hours and costs, and give parents a feel for the warmth and care that will make them want to visit.

This guide covers website design for UK day nurseries, preschools and similar early years settings, whether independent, part of a small group or run by a charity or committee. It focuses on the site itself rather than wider promotion. For that, see our guide to nursery marketing.

What parents look for first

Parents arriving on a nursery website often have a short checklist in their head. Make sure the answers are easy to find:

  • The age range you care for.
  • Opening hours and days, and whether you are open all year or term time only.
  • Whether there are places available, or how the waiting list works.
  • Fees, and whether you offer funded hours.
  • Your latest inspection outcome.
  • Where you are, and parking or drop-off arrangements.
  • How to arrange a visit.

A quick facts panel on the homepage can cover several of these at a glance, with links to fuller pages. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise content.

Rooms and age groups

Most nurseries organise children into rooms by age. A page or section for each room helps parents picture where their child will spend the day. Each might include:

  • The age range and typical group size.
  • Photos of the room and its resources, taken when children are not identifiable or with consent.
  • The room leader and key staff, with their consent.
  • Sleep arrangements for babies and toddlers.
  • How children move up to the next room.

Outdoor space deserves its own photos and description, since many parents place a lot of value on outdoor play.

A day at nursery

Describing a typical day is one of the most reassuring things a nursery website can do. Cover arrival and settling, play and learning activities, meals and snacks, naps, outdoor time and home time. Explain how you keep parents informed during the day, whether through an app, a daily sheet or conversations at pick-up.

Food is often a big question. Share sample menus, explain whether meals are cooked on site and describe how you handle allergies and dietary requirements. Be precise and careful when describing allergy procedures.

Learning and development

Parents want to know how their child will learn. In England, early years settings follow the Early Years Foundation Stage framework, and Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own early years frameworks. Your website can explain in plain language how you support learning through play, how key workers track progress and how you share that with families.

If you follow a particular approach, such as a specific educational philosophy or forest school sessions, explain what it means day to day rather than relying on labels. Avoid overpromising outcomes; describe what you do, not what children will definitely achieve.

Inspection reports

Many parents check inspection reports before visiting. In England, nurseries are inspected by Ofsted. In Scotland, by the Care Inspectorate and Education Scotland; in Wales, by Care Inspectorate Wales and Estyn; and in Northern Ireland, registration and inspection involve local health and social care trusts. Your website can:

  • State your most recent inspection outcome accurately.
  • Link to the full report on the regulator's site.
  • Update it promptly after each inspection.
  • Avoid quoting selectively in a way that misrepresents the report.

Inspection frameworks and how outcomes are reported can change, so describe your result in the terms the regulator currently uses.

Funded hours and fees

Government-funded childcare is one of the most confusing areas for parents. Entitlements differ across the four nations, depend on the child's age and family circumstances, and have changed in recent years. A nursery website can genuinely help by:

  • Stating which funded schemes you offer and for which age groups.
  • Explaining how funded hours work at your setting, such as which sessions they can be used for.
  • Being clear about any additional charges for meals, consumables or extra hours, in line with current government guidance on what providers can charge for.
  • Linking to official government information so parents can check their own eligibility.
  • Mentioning whether you accept Tax-Free Childcare and childcare vouchers, where relevant.

Because rules change, avoid restating detailed eligibility criteria that may go out of date. Note when the page was last reviewed.

For fees, publishing session prices or a fee schedule saves time for parents and staff. If you prefer not to publish prices, explain how parents can request them. Be clear about deposits, registration fees, notice periods and what happens on bank holidays or during closures.

Safeguarding and policies

Parents want reassurance that their child will be safe. Your website can show this without being alarming:

  • A short statement on how you safeguard children, and who the designated safeguarding lead is.
  • Information on staff vetting and training, described accurately.
  • Key policies available to read or download, such as safeguarding, behaviour, complaints and privacy.
  • Your approach to mobile phones and photography in the setting.

Keep policy documents current. An outdated policy on the website can undermine trust.

Photos and children's privacy

Photos bring a nursery website to life, but children's images need extra care. Only use photos with clear written consent from parents for website use, and consider whether a child could be identified from the image or caption. Many settings use photos where faces are not visible, or focus on hands, activities, resources and spaces. Never pair a child's photo with their full name. Our guide to website image optimisation covers the technical side of keeping photos fast to load.

Meet the team

Parents are handing their child to people, not a building. A team page with photos, roles and a few words from each person, with their consent, helps parents feel they know who will be caring for their child. Include the manager, room leaders and any special roles such as a SENCo. Describe qualifications accurately.

Visits, registration and waiting lists

The main call to action on a nursery site is usually booking a visit. Make it prominent on every page and easy to do on a phone. Options include:

  • A short visit request form with preferred days and times.
  • A registration or waiting list form, asking for the child's date of birth, required start date and sessions wanted.
  • A clear explanation of what happens after a form is submitted and how long the wait may be.

Forms collect personal information about children, so keep them to what you need and make sure your privacy notice explains how data is used. Our guide to website contact forms has more on this.

Settling in and starting nursery

A page on settling in can reduce anxiety for parents and children alike. Explain how settling sessions work, what to bring, how you support children who find separation hard, and how communication works in the first weeks. A downloadable starter pack can be useful too.

Choosing a platform

Most nursery websites are content and enquiry sites, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all work well. Think about who will update inspection details, policies, menus and fees, and whether the site needs to link with your nursery management software for enquiries or waiting lists. FAQs

Should a nursery publish its fees online?

Publishing fees or session prices saves time for parents and staff. If you prefer not to, explain how parents can request them, and be clear about deposits and extra charges.

How should a nursery website explain funded hours?

State which funded schemes you offer and how they work at your setting, then link to official government guidance so parents can check eligibility, since rules differ by nation and change over time.

Can nurseries use photos of children on their website?

Only with clear written parental consent. Many settings use photos where children cannot be identified, and children's full names should never appear alongside images.

Should a nursery link to its inspection report?

Yes, stating your latest outcome accurately and linking to the full report on the regulator's site is helpful and transparent.

What is the most important call to action on a nursery website?

Usually booking a visit, supported by a waiting list or registration form for parents ready to apply.
