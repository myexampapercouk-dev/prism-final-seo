Website Design for Coaches in the UK: Pages, Booking and Honest Claims

Website design guide for UK life, business and career coaches: clear niche, coaching packages, discovery calls, honest claims, payments, privacy and platforms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A coaching website works best when it speaks directly to a specific person and the change they are looking for.
  • Clear packages, an easy discovery call route and honest descriptions of what coaching can and cannot do build trust.
  • Claims about outcomes need care, and booking, payment and privacy tools should be set up properly from the start.

Coaching is a personal service. Whether you work as a life coach, business coach, career coach, executive coach or in a specialist area such as confidence or leadership, people choose a coach largely on whether they feel understood and whether they trust that person to help. A website cannot replace that first conversation, but it can make the difference between someone booking it and someone clicking away.

This guide is for UK coaches building or improving a website. It covers defining who the site is for, the pages to include, packaging your services, discovery calls and booking, honest claims, payments, privacy and choosing a platform. It is separate from therapy and counselling, which carry different professional and regulatory considerations; our therapist website design guide covers those.

Be clear about who you coach

"I help people live their best lives" may be heartfelt, but it rarely helps a visitor decide whether you are right for them. Coaching websites tend to be stronger when they describe a specific person in a specific situation, for example:

  • New managers struggling with the step up to leading a team.
  • Professionals considering a career change in their forties.
  • Small business owners who feel stuck working in the business rather than on it.
  • Parents returning to work after a career break.

When someone reads your home page, they should be able to think "this is about me". If you coach more than one group, consider a separate page for each, written in the language that group uses to describe their situation.

The pages a coaching website needs

Home page

Open with who you help and the change they want, then give a clear next step, usually a discovery call. Follow with a brief explanation of how coaching with you works, a little about you, genuine testimonials if you have them, and the call to action again. Our hero section guide covers that crucial first screen.

About page

People want to know the person they will be talking to. Share your background, why you became a coach, any coaching training or accreditation you genuinely hold, and your approach. Coaching is not a regulated profession in the UK in the way some health professions are, so be accurate about what any credential means and avoid implying a status you do not have.

Coaching packages or services

Describe the ways people can work with you. More on structuring these below.

How coaching works

Many visitors are unsure what coaching actually involves. A page explaining the format of sessions, how often they happen, whether they are online or in person, and what you expect from the client removes uncertainty. It is also a good place to explain what coaching is not, for example that it is not a substitute for medical, legal, financial or mental health support.

Testimonials

Real words from real clients, used with permission. Our testimonials page guide explains how to gather and present them.

Contact and booking

An easy way to book a discovery call or send a message.

Privacy notice and terms

Needed if you collect personal data or take payments online. More below.

Packaging your coaching

A list of hourly sessions can make coaching feel like a commodity. Many coaches find it clearer to describe a small number of defined offers, such as:

  • A single session or intensive for a specific decision or challenge.
  • A programme of a set number of sessions over a period, with a defined focus.
  • Ongoing coaching on a monthly basis for people who want continued support.
  • Group coaching or workshops if you run them.

For each offer, explain who it suits, what is included, how long it lasts and how to start. Only list inclusions you actually provide. Whether you show prices is your decision; many clients appreciate seeing them, and our pricing page design guide covers different approaches.

Discovery calls and online booking

For most coaches, the main goal of the website is a booked discovery call. Make that easy:

  • Use one clear, consistent call to action throughout the site.
  • Explain what the call is for, how long it lasts, and that there is no obligation.
  • Embed a scheduling tool so people can choose a time without back-and-forth emails.
  • Ask only a few questions when booking, enough to prepare without creating a barrier.
  • Send a clear confirmation with the date, time, and how to join.

Our online booking systems guide explains the options and what to consider when choosing a tool. Some coaches prefer a short enquiry form instead, which works well if you like to read about someone before speaking. Our guide to website contact forms covers that approach.

Honest claims and testimonials

Coaching marketing sometimes leans on dramatic promises: transformed lives, doubled incomes, guaranteed results. Beyond being off-putting to many readers, these claims can create problems. Marketing on your own website is covered by the UK advertising codes, which the Advertising Standards Authority oversees, and claims generally need to be capable of being backed up. Points to keep in mind:

  • Avoid guaranteeing outcomes such as income, promotions or weight loss.
  • Do not present one client's experience as typical without good reason.
  • Only use testimonials that are genuine and that the client has agreed you can publish.
  • Be particularly careful with anything touching on health or mental wellbeing, and signpost appropriate professional support.

Honest descriptions of how you work and what clients can realistically expect are often more persuasive anyway. If you are unsure about a specific claim, check the ASA's guidance or take advice.

Taking payments

If clients pay online, you have several options depending on your platform: payments taken at booking, invoices sent after a discovery call, or packages bought through a simple checkout. Whatever you choose:

  • Make terms clear before payment, including cancellation, rescheduling and refund policies.
  • Explain what happens if a client wants to stop part-way through a programme.
  • Check your obligations under consumer law for services sold online, including cancellation rights, as these can apply to coaching sold to individuals. Guidance from official sources or a legal adviser is worthwhile here.

Privacy and data protection

Coaching conversations are personal, and booking forms may collect sensitive details. Under UK GDPR, you generally need a privacy notice explaining what you collect, why and how long you keep it. Practical steps include:

  • Collecting only the information you need at booking.
  • Using reputable, secure tools for forms, scheduling and video calls.
  • Being clear about how session notes are stored.
  • Setting up a compliant cookie banner if you use analytics or marketing cookies. See our cookie banner design guide.

The ICO's guidance for small businesses is a useful starting point, and you may need to check whether you are required to pay the data protection fee.

Content and search

Most coaches find clients through referrals, social media, networking and search. Your website can support all of these. Helpful content might include:

  • Articles answering questions your ideal clients ask, such as how to know if coaching is right for them.
  • Explanations of common challenges in your niche.
  • A clear page for each main type of coaching you offer.

Use descriptive page titles and the words people actually search for, such as "career coach" or "executive coaching", alongside any location if you offer in-person sessions. If you meet clients in person and are eligible, a Google Business Profile can help with local searches. Our SEO for small business guide covers the basics.

Design that feels personal

  • Real photographs of you, ideally natural and warm, help visitors feel they know you.
  • A short video introducing yourself can be powerful for a personal service. See our website video guide.
  • Clean, readable layouts with plenty of space make it easier to absorb your message.
  • Mobile-first design, since many visitors will arrive from social media on their phones.

Choosing a platform

Squarespace and Wix are popular with coaches because they make it straightforward to build and edit a small site, and both support booking and payments in various ways. WordPress offers more flexibility for coaches who plan to publish a lot of content or sell courses alongside coaching. FAQs

What should a coaching website include?

A clear home page, an about page, your coaching packages, an explanation of how coaching works, genuine testimonials, an easy booking route and a privacy notice.

Should coaches show prices on their website?

Many do, as it helps visitors judge fit before booking a call. Others prefer to discuss fees on a discovery call. Either can work if you are consistent.

Can I promise results on my coaching website?

It is wise to avoid guaranteeing outcomes. Marketing claims generally need to be supportable under UK advertising rules, so describe realistic expectations instead.

Do coaches need a privacy notice?

If you collect personal data through forms, booking or payments, you generally need one under UK GDPR. The ICO's small business guidance is a good starting point.

Which platform is best for a coaching website?

Squarespace and Wix suit coaches who want a small site they manage themselves. WordPress suits those who plan to publish lots of content or sell courses.
