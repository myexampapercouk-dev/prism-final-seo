Website Colour and Typography: A Practical Guide for Small Businesses

A UK guide to choosing website colours and fonts for a small business: building a palette, contrast, font pairing, readable type sizes and consistency.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A small, deliberate set of colours and fonts makes a website look more professional than a wide variety.
  • Readability and contrast come first; a colour or font that looks striking but is hard to read works against you.
  • Defining colours and type as a simple system makes the site consistent and easier to maintain.

Colour and typography shape how a website feels before a visitor reads a single sentence. They influence whether a site looks calm or energetic, traditional or modern, trustworthy or improvised. For a small business, getting them right does not require an expensive rebrand, but it does require some thought. A few deliberate choices, applied consistently, can make a site look far more polished.

This guide explains how to choose website colours and fonts for a small business, how to keep them readable and accessible, and how to apply them consistently across your site.

Start from your brand

If your business already has a logo, brand colours or fonts, the website should build on them. Consistency between your website, signage, social media and printed materials helps people recognise you. If your brand is still loosely defined, it may be worth clarifying it before making website decisions. Our brand identity design guide explains what a brand identity covers.

Think about how you want visitors to feel and what your customers expect. A law firm, a children's party business and an independent coffee roaster will reasonably make very different choices. Look at the kinds of businesses your customers already trust, not to copy them, but to understand the expectations in your sector.

Choosing a colour palette

Keep it small

Most effective websites use a limited palette. A common structure is:

  • A primary brand colour used for key elements such as the logo, headings or highlights.
  • An accent colour used sparingly for calls to action, such as buttons and links, so they stand out.
  • Neutrals, such as off-whites, greys and near-blacks, for backgrounds, text and borders.
  • Functional colours for messages such as errors, warnings and success states.

Neutrals do most of the work on a typical page. Brand colours tend to be more effective when used with restraint, so they draw the eye to what matters.

Think about meaning, but do not overthink it

Colours carry associations, and these vary by culture and context. Rather than relying on general rules about what each colour means, consider what fits your brand and how your colours will work together. Your call-to-action colour should contrast with its surroundings so it is easy to spot.

Tints and shades

A palette is more flexible if each main colour has a few lighter and darker versions. Lighter tints work for backgrounds and highlighted sections; darker shades work for hover states and text on light backgrounds. Defining these in advance avoids improvised colours creeping in.

Contrast and accessibility

Colour choices must keep text readable. Low-contrast text, such as light grey on white or white on a pale brand colour, is hard for many people to read, particularly on mobile screens outdoors or for visitors with visual impairments.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) set out minimum contrast ratios between text and its background. Free contrast-checking tools can tell you whether a colour combination passes. Check:

  • Body text against its background.
  • Text on buttons and coloured sections.
  • Link colours against surrounding text and background.
  • Text placed over images.
  • Form field borders and placeholder text.

Also avoid relying on colour alone to convey meaning. For example, form errors should include a text message, not just a red border. Our website accessibility guide covers these principles in more depth.

Choosing fonts

Limit the number of fonts

Most websites need no more than two typefaces: one for headings and one for body text. Some sites use a single family throughout, varying weight and size for hierarchy. More fonts tend to make a site feel cluttered and can slow it down.

Readability first

Body text needs to be comfortable to read at length. Look for fonts with clear letter shapes, generous spacing and distinct characters, so that letters such as a lowercase "l", uppercase "I" and the number "1" are easy to tell apart. Decorative or script fonts can work for a logo or occasional headline but are rarely suitable for paragraphs.

Serif and sans serif

Serif fonts have small strokes at the ends of letters and can feel traditional or editorial. Sans serif fonts lack these and often feel cleaner or more modern. Both can be highly readable on screens. Choose based on your brand's character and how the font performs at the sizes you need.

Pairing fonts

If you use two fonts, they should look different enough to create contrast but share a similar feel. A common approach is a distinctive font for headings with a neutral, highly readable font for body text. Test pairings with real content rather than sample text.

Licensing and performance

Check that you have the right licence to use a font on your website. Many good fonts are freely licensed for web use, while others require a paid licence. Each font file adds to page weight, so load only the weights and styles you actually use. Our website speed optimisation guide covers performance more broadly.

Type sizes and hierarchy

Typography is not just which font you use but how you use it. A clear hierarchy helps visitors scan the page and understand what matters.

  • Body text size: make it comfortably readable on all devices. Text that is too small is one of the most common readability problems.
  • Headings: use clear size and weight differences between heading levels so the structure is obvious.
  • Line length: very long lines are tiring to read. Limiting the width of text columns helps.
  • Line height: generous spacing between lines makes paragraphs easier to read.
  • Spacing: consistent space between headings, paragraphs and sections makes a page feel organised.

Use a consistent scale of sizes rather than choosing sizes ad hoc. A small set of defined sizes for headings, body text and small print keeps the site coherent.

Colour and type on mobile

Choices that look good on a large monitor can behave differently on a phone. Headings may need to be smaller to avoid awkward line breaks, while body text must remain readable. Contrast matters even more when people use phones in bright light. Test your colours and fonts on real devices. Our mobile website design guide covers designing for smaller screens.

Turning choices into a simple system

The best way to keep colour and typography consistent is to define them as a small system:

  • A named list of colours with exact values, and notes on where each is used.
  • Heading and body fonts with the weights used.
  • A set of text sizes for each heading level, body text and small text.
  • Button and link styles, including hover and focus states.
  • Rules for backgrounds, such as which text colours work on which backgrounds.

Most website platforms and themes let you set global colours and fonts. Setting them centrally, rather than styling each page individually, makes the site consistent and makes future changes much easier. This also helps anyone who adds content later stay within the system.

Common mistakes

  • Using too many colours or fonts.
  • Low-contrast text that is hard to read.
  • Body text that is too small, especially on mobile.
  • Decorative fonts used for paragraphs.
  • Call-to-action buttons that blend into the page.
  • Colours and fonts applied inconsistently from page to page.
  • Loading many font weights that are never used.
  • Website colours that do not match the rest of the brand.

When to refresh colours and typography

You may not need a full redesign to improve how a site looks. Refining contrast, tightening a palette, increasing body text size or replacing a hard-to-read font can make a noticeable difference. If your brand is changing more fundamentally, colour and type are usually part of a wider rebrand; our rebranding guide covers that process. If you are planning larger changes to the site itself, our guide to redesigning without losing rankings is worth reading.

A simple checklist

  • Colours and fonts based on your existing brand, where you have one.
  • A small palette with primary, accent, neutral and functional colours.
  • Contrast checked for text, buttons and links.
  • No more than two typefaces, with only needed weights loaded.
  • Font licences checked.
  • Readable body text size, line length and line height.
  • A consistent scale of heading sizes.
  • Global colour and type settings in your platform.
  • Testing on real mobile devices.

FAQs

How many colours should a website use?

Usually a small set: a primary colour, an accent, a few neutrals and functional colours for messages. Restraint tends to look more professional.

How many fonts should a website have?

Generally no more than two, one for headings and one for body text. A single family can work well too.

What is the best font for a business website?

There is no single best font. Choose one that suits your brand, is highly readable on screens and is licensed for web use.

How do I check colour contrast?

Use a contrast-checking tool to compare text and background colours against WCAG guidelines, and test on real devices.

Should my website colours match my logo?

Usually, yes. Building on your existing brand colours helps people recognise your business across different channels.
