Architect Website Design: Portfolios, Process and Credibility

Website design for UK architects and practices: project portfolios, clear service pages, explaining your process, ARB title rules and fast, image-led pages.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • An architecture website is judged on its projects, so case studies need context, not just a gallery of beautiful photos.
  • Explain the services you offer and how working with you unfolds, because many clients have never commissioned an architect before.
  • Use the title architect only in line with ARB rules, credit photographers and collaborators, and keep image-heavy pages fast.

For an architecture practice, the website is often the portfolio, the brochure and the first conversation rolled into one. Potential clients, from homeowners planning an extension to developers and public bodies, look at your projects to judge your taste, your competence and whether you have done something like their job before. Design matters, but so does clarity: many visitors have never hired an architect and need help understanding what you do and what happens next.

This guide is about the website itself. For profile building, awards entries, social media, PR and lead generation, see our guide to architect marketing in the UK.

Know which clients you want

An architecture website should be shaped by the work you want more of. A residential practice focused on extensions and new homes speaks to private clients who need reassurance and plain language. A practice working on commercial, education or healthcare projects is speaking to procurement teams, developers and consultants who want evidence of relevant experience, process and capability. Many practices do both, and the navigation should let each audience find its route quickly.

Decide which project types, sectors and locations matter most, and let those priorities shape the home page, the order of projects and the service pages.

A structure that works for many practices

  • Projects: the portfolio, filterable by sector or project type if you have enough work.
  • Services: what you offer, such as feasibility, design, planning applications, technical design and contract administration, as relevant to your practice.
  • How we work: the stages of a project with you, fees approach and what clients should expect.
  • About: the practice, its approach, the people and their qualifications.
  • Journal or news, only if you will keep it going.
  • Contact, with an enquiry form that asks useful questions.

Project pages that explain, not just show

Many architecture sites present projects as a full-screen gallery with a one-line title. That can look striking, but it leaves potential clients guessing. A strong project page usually includes:

  • The project type, location (as precise as the client is happy with), and the year completed.
  • The brief: what the client needed and any constraints, such as a tight site, a conservation area or a limited budget.
  • Your response: the key design decisions and why you made them.
  • Your role, particularly if you worked with other practices or only delivered certain stages.
  • Drawings, sketches or diagrams alongside photography where possible.
  • Credits for photographers, contractors and collaborators.

Be accurate about your involvement. If a project was designed while a director worked at another practice, say so. If you delivered only the planning stage, do not imply you saw it through to completion. Do not describe a project as award-winning unless it has won the award named.

Get permission from clients before publishing their homes or projects, and be careful with private residential work: avoid exact addresses and anything that compromises the owners' privacy or security.

Photography and image rights

Architecture is visual, and good photography is central. Check the licence for every image: professional photographers usually license images for specific uses, and you may need permission to use them on your website, social media or in print. Credit photographers in the way your agreement requires. Visualisations should be clearly identified as such, so visitors do not mistake a render for a completed building.

Keeping image-heavy pages fast

Large photographs are the main reason architecture sites load slowly, especially on phones. Export images at sensible dimensions, use modern formats where your platform supports them, let images load as the visitor scrolls, and avoid uploading original camera files. Full-screen video headers look impressive but can be heavy; if you use one, make sure the page still works well without it. Our guides to website image optimisation and Core Web Vitals cover the details.

Explaining services and process

A homeowner who has never commissioned an architect may not know what a feasibility study is, when planning permission is needed or what happens after it is granted. A clear "How we work" page, describing the stages of a project with you in plain English, removes a lot of uncertainty. Many practices structure this around the RIBA Plan of Work stages; if you do, explain what each stage means for the client rather than just listing names.

Be careful not to give definitive planning or building regulations advice on the website. Requirements vary between local authorities and change over time, so phrase guidance in general terms and encourage visitors to discuss their own situation with you.

On fees, you can explain how you charge, for example a percentage, fixed fee per stage or hourly rate, without publishing figures if you prefer. That helps clients understand what to expect before they get in touch.

The ARB and use of the title architect

In the UK, the title "architect" is protected by law, and only people registered with the Architects Registration Board may use it in the course of business. This matters for your website. Describe team members accurately: a registered architect, an architectural assistant, a Part 2 graduate or an architectural technologist each has a different status. If a practice name or description uses the word architect, check that it meets ARB requirements. ARB also has a code of conduct covering how registered architects present themselves and their services.

If you are a RIBA chartered practice or a member of another professional body, you may be able to display its logo, but only within the rules set by that body and only while the membership is current. Check the current guidance from the ARB and any professional body you belong to rather than relying on general advice. Our guide to website trust signals covers how to present credentials well.

Team and about pages

Clients are hiring people, so show who they will work with. Short profiles with role, qualifications and areas of interest are more useful than long biographies. Use real photographs. Keep the page updated as people join and leave, and do not list people who no longer work at the practice.

Enquiry forms that save time

A good enquiry form helps you qualify leads without putting people off. Ask for name, contact details, project type, location and a short description, and optionally a rough timeframe and budget range. Avoid long, mandatory forms. Tell visitors what happens next and when they can expect a reply. Store enquiries securely and explain how you use personal data in a clear privacy notice, in line with UK GDPR.

Choosing a platform

Architecture sites need flexible, image-led layouts that are still easy to update. WordPress offers a lot of flexibility and is widely used. Hosted builders such as Squarespace and Wix make it simple to add projects without technical help. Whatever you use, set up a project template so new case studies are consistent and quick to publish. Our guide to WordPress for small business websites may help if you are weighing up options.

Journal and insight content

A journal can show how your practice thinks, but only if it is kept up. Useful topics include explaining a design approach, what clients should consider before an extension, or reflections on a completed project. Write for potential clients rather than other architects, and avoid presenting general information as definitive planning or regulatory advice. A small number of considered articles is better than a news page that stopped years ago.

Common mistakes on architecture websites

  • Galleries with no context, roles or credits.
  • Huge image files that make pages slow on mobile.
  • Renders presented as if they were completed buildings.
  • Team members described as architects when they are not registered.
  • No explanation of how working with the practice actually works.
  • Navigation so minimal that visitors cannot find the contact page.

FAQs

Who can call themselves an architect on their website?

In the UK, only people registered with the Architects Registration Board can use the title in business. Check current ARB guidance for how this applies to your practice and staff.

How many projects should a portfolio show?

Enough to show your range and the work you want more of, presented well. Quality and context matter more than quantity.

Can I show projects from a previous practice?

Only if you make your role and the practice involved clear, and have any necessary permissions.

Should I publish my fees?

It is up to you. Explaining how you charge, even without figures, helps clients understand what to expect.

Can I use renders on my website?

Yes, but label them clearly so visitors can tell them apart from completed work.
