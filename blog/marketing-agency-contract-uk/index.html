Marketing Agency Contract (UK): What to Check Before You Sign

Before signing a UK marketing agency contract, check the term, notice period, account ownership, reporting and IP. General guide, not legal advice.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Read the term, notice period and renewal clauses first, because they decide how easily you can leave.
  • Make sure ad accounts, analytics, domains, websites and content are owned by your business, not the agency.
  • This is general information only: have a qualified solicitor review any contract before you sign it.

Before you sign a marketing agency contract in the UK, check five things: the minimum term and notice period, who owns the accounts and content, what is delivered and reported each month, how intellectual property transfers, and what happens when the relationship ends. Most disputes between businesses and agencies come from one of those areas being vague.

This guide is general information to help you ask better questions. It is not legal advice, and contract law can turn on the exact wording, so have a qualified solicitor review any agreement before you sign. What to check in a marketing agency contract (UK)

Agency agreements come in many forms: a short letter of engagement, standard terms and conditions plus a statement of work, or a full master services agreement. Whatever the format, the same areas matter. The table below summarises them.

Term, renewal and notice periods

These clauses decide how easily you can change direction. Look for a minimum term (for example, an initial period before notice can be given), whether the contract renews automatically, and how much notice is required to end it. Check when notice runs from: the date you send it, the end of the month, or the end of the current term.

There is no single "standard" across the industry, so compare what each agency offers. For work like SEO, where results typically take 3 to 6 months, a reasonable initial period can be fair to both sides. What matters is that you understand it before you commit, and that the exit route is written down rather than assumed.

Ownership of accounts

This is the clause most likely to cause pain later. Your Google Ads, Meta Business, Google Analytics, Search Console, Google Business Profile, domain registration and hosting should ideally be held by your business, with the agency given user access. If an agency sets up accounts in its own name, you can lose campaign history, audience data and conversion tracking when you move on.

For paid media in particular, account history has real value. Our guides to choosing a Google Ads agency and the PPC audit checklist explain why access and ownership matter. You can also see how we approach paid media management.

Also check how ad spend is handled. Paying the platform directly from your own billing details keeps a clean record and avoids any question over who owns the account. Ask whether any fee is calculated as a percentage of spend.

Content, website and intellectual property

Under UK law, the creator of a work generally owns the copyright unless it is assigned in writing, so an agency may own what it produces unless the contract says otherwise. Look for a clause that assigns or licenses the work to you, and check whether that happens on creation, on payment or only at the end of the contract.

Areas to cover include:

  • Written content such as blog posts, landing pages and ad copy.
  • Design files including logos, brand assets and source files, not just exports.
  • Website code and themes, plus any licensed plugins or templates that cannot be transferred.
  • Photography and stock, where licences may restrict use.
  • Agency tools and methods, which the agency will usually keep.

Scope, deliverables and reporting

Vague scope is the root of most "what are we paying for?" conversations. A good contract or statement of work lists deliverables in plain numbers: posts per month, campaigns managed, pages optimised, meetings held. It should also say how changes to scope are agreed and priced.

Reporting should be defined too. Agree the frequency, the format and which metrics matter to your business, such as leads, sales, revenue or cost per acquisition, rather than vanity numbers. Ask whether you will have direct access to the underlying data in your own analytics, not only a report.

Data protection and confidentiality

If the agency will handle personal data on your behalf, such as email lists, CRM records or form submissions, UK GDPR expects a written agreement setting out how that data is processed. Check that the contract covers security, sub-processors, and what happens to data when the work ends. Confidentiality clauses should protect your commercial information in both directions. The ICO publishes guidance on controller and processor contracts if you want to read further.

Liability, guarantees and performance claims

Be cautious of contracts or sales materials that guarantee rankings or specific results. No agency controls search engines or ad platforms, and reputable ones say so. Instead, look for commitments the agency can control: work delivered, response times, reporting and transparency. Check any limitation of liability clause with your solicitor so you understand where responsibility sits.

Questions to ask before signing

Ask the agency to walk you through any clause you do not understand, and to confirm in writing anything it says verbally about flexibility, ownership or exit arrangements.

Contract checklist

  • The minimum term, renewal and notice period are clear and acceptable.
  • Monthly deliverables are listed in specific numbers.
  • Out-of-scope work and how it is priced is explained.
  • Ad spend is paid directly by you, and any percentage fee is stated.
  • All ad, analytics, domain and hosting accounts are in your business's name.
  • Content, design and code ownership transfer to you, and the timing is clear.
  • Reporting frequency, format and metrics are agreed.
  • A data processing agreement is in place where personal data is involved.
  • The exit and handover process, including any charges, is written down.
  • A qualified solicitor has reviewed the contract.

When the relationship ends

Even good partnerships end, and a business may simply outgrow a supplier or change strategy. A clean exit clause should list what you receive: admin access to all accounts, source files, website backups, content, reports and any outstanding work. It helps to agree a short handover period so a new supplier can pick up without losing momentum. Asking about this at the start is not pessimistic; it is how well-run agencies and clients avoid misunderstandings later. Keep your own record of every login and who holds admin rights, and review it each quarter so nothing depends on one person remembering a password. That simple habit protects you whichever agency you work with.

FAQs

What should I check in a marketing agency contract in the UK?

Check the term and notice period, scope and fees, ownership of accounts and content, intellectual property, reporting, data protection and the exit process. Have a solicitor review it before signing.

Who owns the Google Ads account when I use an agency?

It depends on how the account was set up and what the contract says. Ideally the account is in your business's name, with the agency given access, so the history stays with you.

Do I own the content my agency writes?

Not automatically. Copyright generally stays with the creator unless the contract assigns it to you, so look for a clear clause on when ownership transfers.

What is a normal notice period for a marketing agency?

There is no single standard; terms vary between agencies and services. Compare offers and make sure the notice period and any minimum term are written down before you commit.

Can an agency guarantee results in the contract?

Be wary if it does. No agency controls search engines or ad platforms, so focus on commitments it can control, such as deliverables, reporting and transparency.

Do I need a lawyer to review an agency contract?

It is sensible. This guide is general information only, and a qualified solicitor can explain how the specific wording affects your business before you sign.
