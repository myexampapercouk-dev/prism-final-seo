IT Support Company Website Design: Explaining Managed Services Clearly

Website design for UK IT support companies and MSPs: plain-English service pages, support plans, response times, security claims and client login.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Many people looking for IT support are not technical, so explaining services in terms of business outcomes and everyday problems usually works better than jargon.
  • Support hours, response targets and what plans include should be described accurately, and only in terms you actually commit to in contracts.
  • Security and certification claims carry real weight, so only show schemes and statuses you genuinely hold and describe their scope precisely.

Choosing an IT support company is a decision many small and medium-sized businesses put off until something breaks. The person searching is often a business owner or office manager, not an IT professional. They want to know whether you can look after businesses like theirs, how quickly you respond, what it costs and whether they can trust you with their systems and data. An IT support website that answers those questions plainly has a head start, because the sector is full of sites that talk mainly in acronyms.

This guide covers website design for UK IT support companies, managed service providers and IT consultancies. It focuses on the website itself. For wider promotion, see our guides to B2B marketing and B2B SEO.

Who you are really writing for

An IT support site may be read by several people in the same business:

  • The owner or managing director, who cares about cost, risk and downtime.
  • An office or operations manager, who deals with day-to-day issues.
  • An internal IT person, if there is one, who wants technical detail.
  • Finance, who wants clear pricing and contract terms.

Lead with business outcomes in plain English, such as fewer interruptions, help when something goes wrong, and data that is backed up and protected. Then provide technical detail on deeper pages for those who want it. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing for clarity.

Service pages that explain, not list

Many IT sites present long lists of services: managed IT, cloud, cyber security, backup, networking, VoIP, Microsoft 365, hardware procurement and more. That can overwhelm non-technical visitors. Consider organising the site around a few clear areas, with a page each, explaining:

  • What problem the service solves, in everyday terms.
  • What it typically involves.
  • Who it suits.
  • How it fits with your other services.

Pages organised around common situations can also help, such as "moving office", "starting to work remotely" or "replacing an old server", because that is often how people search. Only describe services you actually deliver, and be clear where you work with third parties. Our guide to website navigation covers structuring service pages.

Support plans and pricing

Cost is one of the first questions business owners ask. Options for presenting it include:

  • Published plans, often per user or per device, with what each includes.
  • "From" prices with an explanation of what affects the final cost.
  • An explanation of how you quote, if every client is different.

Whatever you publish, make sure it matches your contracts. Be clear about what is included and what is chargeable extra, such as project work, hardware or out-of-hours support, and about contract length and notice periods. Pricing shown to business customers still needs to be clear and not misleading, so check current guidance if unsure. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout options.

Response times and support hours

Response times are a key selling point, which makes them easy to overstate. If you show target response or resolution times, make sure they match what your service level agreements actually commit to, explain how priorities are defined, and be clear about support hours and what happens outside them. A precise description, such as the hours your helpdesk is staffed and how urgent issues are handled out of hours, is more credible than vague claims of round-the-clock cover.

Security and certification claims

Cyber security is part of most IT support offerings, and businesses are increasingly cautious. The website should describe what you do in clear terms without promising things no provider can guarantee, such as complete protection from attacks. A few rules:

  • Only show certifications you or your organisation genuinely hold, such as Cyber Essentials or ISO standards, and keep them current.
  • Be precise about scope: a certification may cover your own organisation, not the services you deliver to clients.
  • Describe vendor partner statuses accurately, using the vendor's own rules for names and logos.
  • If you help clients work towards certifications, explain that clearly rather than implying you can certify them yourself, unless you are actually authorised to do so.

The National Cyber Security Centre publishes guidance for small businesses that you can link to as an authoritative source. Our guide to website trust signals covers presenting credentials responsibly.

Sectors you know well

If you have genuine experience in particular sectors, such as professional services, healthcare or education, dedicated pages can explain the specific IT considerations those businesses face. Only create pages for sectors where you actually have experience, and be careful with any regulatory statements, as sector rules on data and systems vary and change.

Local or national coverage

Many IT support businesses combine remote support with on-site visits. Be clear about where you can attend in person and how remote support works elsewhere. If you serve a defined area, location pages can help with local search, but each should contain genuinely useful, specific information rather than duplicated text with the town name swapped. Our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile cover the essentials.

Client login and existing customers

Existing clients need quick access to the support portal, remote support tools and contact numbers. Put a clear "Get support" or "Client login" link in the header, separate from calls to action for new visitors. If you provide a remote support download, make sure visitors are only directed to download it when they have been asked to by your team, and explain how they can confirm a request is genuine, as remote access tools can be misused by scammers.

Enquiries and next steps

Many businesses would prefer a short conversation before committing. Options include a booking tool for a short call, a simple enquiry form or a clear phone number. If you offer an IT review or assessment, describe what it actually involves and whether it is free or charged. Explain what happens after an enquiry and roughly when they can expect to hear back. Our guides to contact forms and online booking systems cover the options.

Helpful content

Practical, plain-English articles can show expertise and attract searches from businesses with specific problems, such as setting up multi-factor authentication or planning a move to cloud email. Date articles, review them regularly because technology changes, and avoid scaremongering. Content that genuinely helps tends to build more trust than alarming headlines. Our guide to AI Overviews and SEO covers how clear, well-structured content can be surfaced in newer search features.

Practise what you preach

An IT company's own website is, in effect, part of its portfolio. Visitors may notice if it is slow, insecure or out of date. Make sure it runs over HTTPS, is kept updated, loads quickly and works on a phone. Forms should be protected against spam and collect only what you need, in line with UK GDPR. Our guides to website speed, website maintenance and accessibility cover the essentials.

Choosing a platform

WordPress is common for IT support sites because it handles structured service pages, location pages and articles flexibly. Hosted builders such as Squarespace or Wix can suit smaller firms with simpler needs. Whatever you choose, make sure security updates are applied promptly. See our guide to website builders vs custom websites.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Jargon-heavy copy that non-technical buyers struggle to follow.
  • Response time claims that don't match your contracts.
  • Overstated security promises or certification scope.
  • Vendor logos used without the right status or permission.
  • No clear route for existing clients to get support.
  • An out-of-date or slow website for a technology business.

FAQs

Should IT support companies publish their prices?

Many buyers appreciate it. Per-user plans or "from" prices can work, as long as they match your contracts and say clearly what is included.

How should an MSP describe response times?

Only in terms that match your service level agreements, with clear support hours and an explanation of how priorities are set.

Can we show Cyber Essentials on our website?

Only if you currently hold it, and describe its scope accurately. Follow the scheme's rules on use of its name and badges.

Should an IT company have location pages?

They can help if you serve particular areas, but each page should contain specific, useful information rather than duplicated text.

How should clients get support from the website?

A clear "Get support" or "Client login" link in the header, separate from new enquiry routes, usually works well.
