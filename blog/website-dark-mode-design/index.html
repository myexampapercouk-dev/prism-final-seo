Website Dark Mode Design: When to Offer It and How to Do It Well

A practical guide to dark mode website design, covering when it is worth offering, colour and contrast choices, images, logos and testing across devices.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Dark mode is optional; it suits some sites and audiences more than others.
  • Good dark themes use dark greys and adjusted colours rather than simply inverting the light design.
  • Logos, images, forms and contrast all need checking in both modes before launch.

Many people now set their phones and computers to a dark appearance, and some websites follow suit, switching to light text on dark backgrounds automatically. Dark mode can look polished and feel easier on the eyes in low light. It can also double the design and testing work, and a rushed version can make a site harder to read.

This guide looks at whether dark mode is worth it for a business website, how it works technically, and the design decisions that separate a good dark theme from a poor one.

What is dark mode on a website?

Dark mode is an alternative colour scheme that uses dark backgrounds with light text. On the web, it usually works in one of two ways:

  • Following the system setting. Browsers tell websites whether the visitor's device is set to light or dark. The site can then show the matching colour scheme automatically.
  • A manual toggle. The site offers a switch so visitors can choose, often remembering their preference for next time.

Many sites combine both: follow the system setting by default and offer a toggle to override it.

Some browsers can also force a dark appearance on sites that do not provide one. The results vary and can look odd, which is one reason some businesses decide to design a proper dark theme rather than leave it to chance.

Does your website need dark mode?

Dark mode is not a requirement, and plenty of excellent websites only offer one appearance. It tends to make more sense when:

  • Visitors spend a long time reading or working on the site, such as documentation, dashboards or web apps
  • Your audience is likely to use the site in the evening or in low light
  • Your audience is technical and expects it
  • Your brand already leans towards a dark look

It may be lower priority for brochure sites where visits are short, or for shops where product photography is designed around a light background. In those cases, the effort might be better spent on speed, content or checkout improvements.

If you are unsure, consider what your visitors do on the site and how long they stay. A dark theme that is half finished is worse than no dark theme at all.

Designing a dark colour palette

The biggest mistake is simply inverting the light design: swapping white for black and black for white. That tends to produce harsh contrast and colours that look wrong.

Use dark greys, not pure black

Many designers use a very dark grey rather than pure black for the main background. Pure white text on pure black can feel harsh for long reading. A slightly softened background and off-white text are often more comfortable.

Show depth with lighter surfaces

In light designs, shadows show that a card or menu sits above the page. Shadows are hard to see on dark backgrounds, so dark themes often use slightly lighter surfaces for raised elements instead.

Adjust brand colours

Brand colours chosen for a white background may look too bright, too dull or unreadable on dark. You may need lighter or less saturated versions of the same hues for dark mode. Agree these variations as part of your brand system so they are used consistently. Our guide to brand identity design covers how colour systems fit into a brand.

Plan colours as tokens

Rather than hard-coding colours throughout the site, define named colour tokens such as background, surface, text, muted text, border and accent, each with a light and dark value. This makes dark mode far easier to build and maintain, and keeps future changes consistent.

Contrast and readability

Text must remain readable in both modes. Check contrast for:

  • Body text and headings against backgrounds
  • Muted or secondary text, which often fails in dark themes
  • Links, in both their normal and visited states
  • Button text and button backgrounds
  • Form fields, placeholders and error messages
  • Focus outlines for keyboard users

Accessibility guidelines set minimum contrast levels, and a dark theme needs to meet them just as the light one does. Our guide to mobile website design also covers readability on smaller screens, where dark mode is often used.

Keep in mind that some people find light text on dark backgrounds harder to read, particularly for long passages. Respecting the visitor's own setting, and offering a toggle, lets each person choose what works for them.

Logos, icons and images

Logos

A dark logo on a dark background disappears. You will usually need a light or reversed version of the logo for dark mode. Many brands already have one for use on dark backgrounds. Check the favicon too, as browsers may show it against a dark tab bar.

Icons and illustrations

Simple icons can often change colour automatically if they are built to inherit the text colour. Illustrations with white backgrounds may look like bright boxes on a dark page and may need alternative versions or transparent backgrounds.

Photography

Photos generally stay as they are. However, product photos shot on white backgrounds will appear as white rectangles in a dark theme. For shops, this is an important consideration. Options include placing product images on a light card even in dark mode, or accepting the contrast as part of the design.

Charts and diagrams

Charts need colours that work on both backgrounds, along with axis lines and labels that remain visible.

Technical considerations

A developer will usually build dark mode using the browser's colour scheme preference and CSS custom properties for colour tokens. A few practical points:

  • Avoid a flash of the wrong theme. If a visitor has chosen dark mode, the page should not briefly appear light before switching. This needs care in how the preference is loaded.
  • Remember the preference. A manual toggle should remember the visitor's choice on return visits.
  • Style form controls. Browsers can adapt built-in controls such as checkboxes and scrollbars if the site declares support for both colour schemes.
  • Check embedded content. Maps, videos, booking widgets and other embeds may not follow your theme and can look out of place.
  • Check emails separately. Email clients handle dark mode in their own ways, so transactional and marketing emails need testing on their own.

Content management systems vary in how easily they support dark mode. Some themes include it, while others need custom work.

Testing dark mode

Dark mode effectively creates a second version of every page. Testing should cover:

  • Every template, not just the homepage
  • Navigation menus, dropdowns and the mobile menu
  • Forms, including validation messages
  • Pop-ups, cookie banners and notifications
  • The basket and checkout on an ecommerce site
  • Pages with embedded content
  • Different browsers and devices

Switch your own device to dark mode for a while and use the site normally. Problems that are easy to miss in a quick check become obvious with everyday use.

Designing the toggle

If you offer a manual switch, make it easy to find and easy to understand. A small icon in the header or footer is common, but icons alone can be ambiguous, so give the control an accessible label that describes what it does. Some sites offer three choices: light, dark and follow system. That gives visitors full control while keeping a sensible default for those who never touch it.

Keep the toggle in the same place on every page and make sure it works with a keyboard as well as a mouse or touch.

Common dark mode mistakes

  • Inverting the light theme rather than designing a dark palette
  • Muted text that becomes unreadable on dark backgrounds
  • Dark logos that vanish
  • White-background images that glare
  • Forgotten components, such as old pop-ups, still showing light
  • Third-party embeds that clash with the theme
  • No way for visitors to switch back if they prefer light

FAQs

Is dark mode better for accessibility?

It helps some people and not others. Offering both appearances, with good contrast in each, and respecting the visitor's own setting is usually the most inclusive approach.

Does dark mode affect SEO?

Dark mode is a visual preference and is not a ranking factor in itself. What matters is that content stays readable and the site remains fast and usable.

Should dark mode follow the device setting?

Following the device setting is a sensible default. Adding a toggle lets visitors override it if they prefer.

Do I need a different logo for dark mode?

Usually yes, if your main logo is dark. A light or reversed version keeps it visible on dark backgrounds.

Can I add dark mode to an existing site?

Often, yes, but it is easier if colours are organised as tokens. Sites with colours hard-coded throughout may need more work first.
