Solicitor Website Design: Practice Areas, Pricing and Trust

Website design for UK solicitors and law firms: practice area pages, price transparency, regulatory information, enquiry handling and clear, accessible copy.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • People looking for a solicitor are often anxious and unfamiliar with legal processes, so clear, plain-English pages for each practice area matter.
  • Solicitors regulated in England and Wales have transparency obligations covering price and service information for certain services, so plan these pages carefully and check current SRA guidance.
  • Enquiry forms and contact routes should be simple, secure and honest about what happens next.

Someone searching for a solicitor is usually dealing with something significant: buying a home, going through a divorce, losing a job, sorting out a parent's estate or facing a dispute. They may never have instructed a lawyer before. A law firm's website needs to reassure them that they have come to the right place, explain things clearly and make it easy to take the next step.

This guide covers website design for UK solicitors and law firms, from high street practices to specialist firms. It focuses on the website itself. For wider promotion, including search, referrals and advertising, see our guide to law firm marketing. Regulatory points here are general and summarised; firms should always check current guidance from their regulator and take compliance advice where needed.

What visitors need from a law firm website

Most visitors are trying to work out:

  • Does this firm handle my type of matter?
  • Will they understand my situation and explain things clearly?
  • What will it cost?
  • Who will actually deal with my case?
  • How do I get in touch, and what happens then?

Design the site so these questions are answered on the first few pages a visitor sees, and especially on mobile. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise.

Structure: personal and business clients

Many firms serve both individuals and businesses. Splitting navigation into "For you" and "For business", or similar, helps each group find the relevant practice areas quickly. Within each, list practice areas in the words clients use: "buying a house" or "conveyancing", "divorce and separation" rather than only "family law", "wills and probate", "employment problems".

Avoid long, undifferentiated lists of every service the firm has ever offered. Focus on the areas you actively want to win work in, and make sure every service listed is one the firm can genuinely handle. See our guide to website navigation.

Practice area pages that actually help

Practice area pages are often where a visitor decides whether to enquire. A good page typically:

  • Opens by acknowledging the client's situation in plain language.
  • Explains, in general terms, how the process usually works and what the firm does at each stage.
  • Answers common questions, such as how long it may take and what the client needs to provide.
  • Introduces the people who handle this type of work.
  • Covers cost information or links to it.
  • Ends with a clear way to get in touch.

Keep explanations general and avoid anything that reads like advice for a specific situation. Add a note that content is general information and not legal advice. Laws differ between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, so be clear which jurisdiction your content covers. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing in plain English.

Price and service transparency

The Solicitors Regulation Authority's transparency rules require regulated firms in England and Wales to publish price and service information for certain services on their website, which currently include areas such as residential conveyancing, probate, some immigration matters, employment tribunal claims for unfair or wrongful dismissal, and some motoring offences, among others. The information generally needs to cover matters such as total cost or the basis of charging, what is included, likely timescales, key stages and the experience and qualifications of the people involved.

The detail and scope of these rules can change, and the SRA has consulted on extending them, so check the current requirements. From a design point of view:

  • Make price information easy to find from the relevant practice area page, not hidden in a footer.
  • Present it in readable HTML rather than only as a downloadable document.
  • Separate your fees from disbursements and VAT clearly.
  • Explain what could increase costs, in plain terms.

Firms regulated by other bodies, such as in Scotland or Northern Ireland, or by other legal regulators, should check their own regulator's rules. Our guide to pricing page design covers layout principles.

Regulatory information and the SRA badge

Firms regulated by the SRA are required to display the SRA's digital badge on their website, and to include certain information such as the firm's regulatory status and details of how to complain. Complaints information typically includes the firm's own complaints procedure and the right to complain to the Legal Ombudsman. Make sure the badge is installed correctly using the SRA's own code, and that regulatory details appear where required, often in the footer and on a dedicated page. Our guide to footer design covers how to fit this in tidily.

Company or LLP details, such as registered number and office, may also be required depending on the firm's structure. Check current rules rather than relying on old templates.

People pages

Clients are hiring people, not just a firm. Individual profiles for solicitors and other fee earners help visitors feel they know who they will be dealing with. Each profile can include a professional photo, role, practice areas, qualifications and a short, human introduction. Be accurate about titles and qualifications: "solicitor" is a protected title, so make sure staff described as solicitors are admitted and hold practising certificates where required, and describe paralegals, legal executives and others correctly.

Only show accreditations, panel memberships and awards the firm or individual actually holds, and follow any rules on how they can be displayed. Avoid unverifiable claims such as "the best family lawyers in the region".

Reviews and testimonials

Genuine client reviews can build trust. Many firms display reviews from an independent platform or their Google reviews. Whatever the source, reviews must be real, not edited to change their meaning, and not presented in a misleading way. Client confidentiality also matters: get clear consent before featuring anything that could identify a client or their matter. See our guide to testimonials pages.

Enquiry forms and first contact

A visitor contacting a law firm may be sharing something sensitive. Make first contact simple and safe:

  • Keep forms short, asking only for contact details, the type of matter and a brief description.
  • Warn people not to include highly sensitive details in a first message, if appropriate.
  • Say clearly whether an initial conversation is free or chargeable, and what it covers.
  • Explain how quickly you aim to respond.
  • Route enquiries to the right team where possible.

Forms should be secure and covered by a clear privacy notice in line with UK GDPR. Remember that submitting an enquiry does not by itself create a client relationship, and it can be sensible to say so. Conflict checks may be needed before you can act. See our guide to website contact forms.

Accessibility

Legal services are used by everyone, including people with disabilities, older clients and people for whom English is a second language. A site with readable text, good contrast, keyboard-friendly navigation and plain language serves all of them better. Offering alternative ways to make contact, such as phone, email and form, also helps. See our guide to website accessibility.

Platform and maintenance

Most law firm sites are built on a content management system such as WordPress, which can handle structured practice area pages, people profiles and regularly updated price information. Whatever the platform, someone at the firm needs to be able to update prices, people and regulatory details promptly. Outdated price information or a profile for someone who left two years ago undermines trust. See our guides to website maintenance and redesigning without losing rankings.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Practice area pages written in legal jargon.
  • Required price information missing, hidden or out of date.
  • SRA badge missing or not using the official code.
  • Inaccurate titles or claims about staff qualifications.
  • Long enquiry forms that ask for too much too soon.

FAQs

Do solicitors have to publish prices on their website?

SRA-regulated firms in England and Wales must publish price and service information for certain services. Check current SRA guidance for the full list and requirements.

Do law firms need to display the SRA badge?

Firms regulated by the SRA are required to display its digital badge on their website, using the official code provided by the SRA.

How should a law firm website organise practice areas?

Splitting by personal and business clients, then using plain-language names for each area, usually helps visitors find what they need.

Can solicitors use client reviews on their website?

Yes, if reviews are genuine, not misleading and do not breach client confidentiality. Get consent before featuring identifiable details.

Should a solicitor's website give legal information?

General information helps clients understand processes, but make clear it is not legal advice and state which jurisdiction it covers.
