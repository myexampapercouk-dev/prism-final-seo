Web Design for Amsterdam Businesses: Working With a Remote UK Studio

Need web design in Amsterdam? What a good Dutch business website needs, how a remote UK studio works with your team, timelines, pricing and what to ask first.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Web design for an Amsterdam business usually means planning for Dutch and English visitors, fast mobile pages and clear routes to an enquiry or sale.
  • Prism Works Studio is UK-based with no office in the Netherlands; we work remotely, one hour behind Amsterdam time, and write in English, working with your team or a translator on Dutch pages.
  • Small websites typically take three to five weeks and are priced in pounds, with euro and other currencies quoted on request.

Web design for an Amsterdam business should give Dutch and international visitors a fast, clear website that explains what you do and makes it easy to get in touch or buy. The right partner plans the structure, languages, content and technical setup together, rather than treating the site as a visual exercise alone.

Prism Works Studio is a UK-based studio that designs and builds websites for businesses across Europe, including the Netherlands. We do not have an office in Amsterdam and will not pretend otherwise. We work remotely, one hour behind Amsterdam time, using video calls, shared documents and a staging site you can review at any point. This guide explains what a good Amsterdam business website needs, how remote projects run, and how to compare options before you commit.

Web design Amsterdam: what businesses usually need

Amsterdam has a large mix of international companies, start-ups, creative agencies, hospitality businesses and professional firms. English is widely used in business, yet many customers still prefer to read and buy in Dutch. That shapes almost every decision on a new site.

  • A clear language plan. Decide whether the site leads with Dutch, English or both, and which pages need each language.
  • Mobile-first pages. Most first visits happen on a phone, often between meetings or while commuting.
  • Simple routes to action. A contact form, booking link or checkout that works without friction.
  • Search-friendly structure. Pages built around what people actually search for, with clean URLs and headings.
  • Accessibility and privacy basics. Readable contrast, keyboard-friendly navigation and a consent setup that respects visitors.

Our website and ecommerce development service covers each of these as part of the build, not as optional extras.

Planning Dutch and English versions of your site

A two-language site needs more thought than a translation plugin. Each version should be written for its own audience, with its own page titles and descriptions, and search engines need clear signals about which version to show to whom. Getting this right at the start is much cheaper than fixing it after launch.

Prism writes in English. For Dutch pages, we work alongside your own team or a native-speaking translator or copywriter you choose, and we build the site so both versions are easy to edit and keep in step. Our multilingual website design guide sets out the structural options, from language folders to separate domains, and the trade-offs of each.

If you only need English at launch, we can still plan the structure so Dutch pages can be added later without rebuilding menus or moving URLs.

How a remote website project runs

Remote projects work well when the process is clear and the decisions are written down. A typical Prism website project follows these stages.

  1. Discovery call. We talk through your goals, audiences, languages, current site and any deadlines.
  2. Proposal and scope. A written scope listing pages, features, languages, responsibilities and the fixed price in pounds.
  3. Structure and content plan. A sitemap and page outlines, so content can be written or translated in parallel with design.
  4. Design. Key page designs shared for comment, then applied across the site.
  5. Build on staging. A private staging site you can click through on your own devices.
  6. Testing and launch. Forms, speed, mobile layouts, redirects and tracking checked before go-live.
  7. Handover. Training, logins and documentation so your team can edit pages confidently.

Our guide on how long it takes to build a website explains where projects tend to speed up or slow down. Content is usually the deciding factor, especially when two languages are involved.

Accessibility, cookies and privacy for Dutch websites

Websites serving customers in the Netherlands should take accessibility and privacy seriously. The European Accessibility Act brings accessibility requirements for many businesses selling certain products and services to consumers in the EU, and privacy rules affect how cookies and tracking can be used.

We build with accessibility in mind from the start, including readable text, sensible heading order, keyboard navigation and descriptive alt text. Our European Accessibility Act guide gives a plain-English overview, and our cookie banner design guide covers how to ask for consent without spoiling the visitor experience.

These notes are general information, not legal advice. Rules and guidance change, so check current official guidance from the relevant Dutch and EU authorities, and take legal advice where your situation needs it.

Online shops for Amsterdam brands

If you sell products, the website is also your shop, and the platform choice matters. Shopify suits many Dutch brands thanks to its multi-currency and multi-language options, while WordPress with WooCommerce gives more control over content-heavy sites. For brands focused on ecommerce in the Netherlands, our Shopify agency guide for the Netherlands covers payments, delivery and platform setup in more depth.

Whatever the platform, a Dutch shop needs clear delivery and returns information, the payment methods local shoppers expect, product pages that answer questions, and a checkout tested on mobile.

Redesigning an existing Dutch website

Many Amsterdam businesses already have a site that ranks for some searches. A redesign should protect that visibility. Before launch, we map old URLs to new ones and set up redirects, so search engines and bookmarked links reach the right page. Our guide to website redirects when relaunching explains the process step by step.

It also helps to agree from the outset who owns the domain, hosting account, design files and content. Our website ownership and handover guide covers what you should hold in your own name.

Questions to ask before hiring a web designer for Amsterdam

  • Who writes the Dutch content, and how is it reviewed before launch?
  • How will language versions be structured and signalled to search engines?
  • What is fixed in the price, and what would count as extra work?
  • Can I see and test a staging site before launch?
  • Who owns the domain, hosting and site files after handover?
  • What support is available once the site is live?

Clear answers to these questions matter more than location. A partner who explains the process plainly and puts it in writing is usually easier to work with, wherever they are based.

FAQs

Do you offer web design in Amsterdam?

Yes, remotely. Prism Works Studio is based in the UK and has no office in Amsterdam or elsewhere in the Netherlands. We design and build websites for Dutch businesses using video calls, shared documents and a staging site you can review throughout the project.

Can you build a website in Dutch?

We build the site so it supports Dutch and English versions. We write in English, and Dutch pages are written or translated by your own team or a native-speaking translator, then added and checked as part of the project plan.

How much does a website cost for an Amsterdam business?

Our small business websites typically cost £1,000 to £2,000 and ecommerce sites £1,500 to £3,500, depending on scope. We price in pounds and can quote euro or other currencies on request. Translation and third-party costs are usually separate.

How long does a website project take?

A small business website typically takes three to five weeks and an ecommerce site five to twelve weeks. Two-language sites can take longer if translated content arrives late, so we plan content timings at the start.

Will my new website meet accessibility rules?

We build with accessibility in mind, including structure, contrast and keyboard use. Whether specific rules apply to your business depends on what you sell and to whom, so check current official guidance and take legal advice where needed.

Can you redesign my site without losing search rankings?

We plan redesigns to protect existing visibility by mapping old URLs to new ones, setting up redirects and checking indexing after launch. No one can guarantee rankings, but careful migration greatly reduces the risk of losing traffic.
