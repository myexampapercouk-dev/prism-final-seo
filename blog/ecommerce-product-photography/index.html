Ecommerce Product Photography: Shots, Style and Images That Help Products Sell

Ecommerce photography explained: which product shots to plan, lighting and editing basics, in-house or professional, and preparing images for your site.

By the Prism Works Studio team · Updated 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Ecommerce photography should answer what a shopper would otherwise check in person: size, texture, detail and use.
  • A consistent style across the catalogue makes a store easier to browse and compare.
  • Decide what to shoot in-house or commission, then size, compress and describe images properly for the web.

Ecommerce photography is the set of product images an online shop uses to show what it sells, from clean main shots to close-ups and lifestyle images. Its job is to give shoppers enough visual information to feel confident buying something they cannot pick up. That means clear, consistent images that show the whole product, the details that matter, its scale and how it is used.

This overview covers which product photography you need, setup and editing basics, whether to shoot in-house, and preparing images for your website. When you are ready to commission a shoot, our guide to writing an ecommerce photography brief covers the document itself.

Start with the questions shoppers ask

Before thinking about lighting or backdrops, list what a customer needs to know. A cushion buyer wants to see the fabric weave and the colour in daylight. A shoe buyer wants the sole, the heel height and how it looks on a foot. A furniture buyer wants to know whether it will fit in their room. Your shot list should answer those questions directly.

Planning shots by product type

Different products need different images. Clothing might need front and back on a model, a flat lay, a fabric close-up and a label detail, while a kitchen gadget might need a clean main image, the product in use, the parts laid out and a size comparison. Group products with similar needs and shoot them together, which helps keep lighting and camera settings consistent. Our photography brief guide shows how to turn this into a shot list.

Lighting and setup basics

Lighting often matters more than the camera. Soft, even light shows shape and colour without harsh shadows. Large windows with indirect daylight can work well for small products, though the light changes through the day. Continuous studio lights or flash with diffusers give more control and consistency, which matters if you are shooting over several days.

  • Use a tripod so framing stays identical from product to product.
  • Use a sweep (a curved background) to avoid a visible line where the floor meets the wall.
  • Use reflectors or white card to fill shadows.
  • Set a fixed white balance rather than automatic, so colours do not drift between shots.
  • Clean products carefully before shooting; dust and fingerprints are far quicker to remove in person than in editing.

Reflective items such as jewellery, glass and polished metal are notoriously tricky because they mirror everything around them. These are often worth handing to a specialist.

Editing workflow

A consistent editing process supports a consistent shoot. Typical steps include cropping to your chosen ratio, aligning the product to the same position in the frame, correcting exposure and white balance, cleaning the background and removing dust. Batch-editing tools can apply the same adjustments across a set of photos, which keeps results uniform and saves time.

Keep the original, unedited files and a high-resolution master of each final image. You will need them for future uses, such as print, marketplaces with different requirements, or a redesigned site with different image sizes.

Images for marketplaces, ads and social

Your product images will often be used beyond your own site. Marketplaces may have specific rules on backgrounds, image size and what can appear in the main photo, so check the current requirements of any marketplace you sell on. Shopping ads typically use your main product image, so a clear, uncluttered shot matters. Our Google Shopping ads guide explains how images fit into the feed. Social platforms tend to favour lifestyle and in-use imagery, and vertical formats may be needed. Planning for these uses at the shoot avoids having to reshoot later.

Consistency matters more than perfection

A catalogue where every product sits on the same background, at the same angle, with similar lighting and cropping looks organised and trustworthy. When images vary wildly, collection pages look messy and products are harder to compare. Write a simple style guide covering background colour, angles, crop and padding, aspect ratio and shadows, and use it every time you add products.

Choose an aspect ratio that suits your theme and stick to it. Square images are a common default, but tall products may suit a portrait ratio. Mixing ratios often leads to awkward gaps in grids.

Colour accuracy and honesty

Photos should show products as they really are. Over-editing colour or removing flaws creates disappointed customers and returns. If a product looks different in different light, say so in the description. Shoppers also rely on images to judge what they are buying, so misleading images can raise consumer law concerns; if in doubt, take advice on how products are presented.

The same applies to AI-generated or heavily edited images. They may be useful for backgrounds or concept work, but a shopper should still be able to trust what the product looks like. Check that any tool's output is accurate, and check the rules of any marketplace or ad platform you use.

Preparing images for the web

Large, uncompressed photos can slow ecommerce pages considerably. Before uploading:

  • Resize to a sensible maximum for your theme rather than uploading straight from the camera.
  • Compress using a modern format where your platform supports it; many platforms convert automatically.
  • Name files descriptively, for example "oak-dining-table-side.jpg" rather than "IMG_4031.jpg".
  • Write alt text that describes what the image shows. It helps people using screen readers and gives search engines context.
  • Lazy-load images further down the page so the first view loads quickly.

Our image optimisation guide and website speed guide go deeper, and our alt text guide shows how to describe product images.

How images support SEO

Product images can appear in image search and in shopping results, and descriptive file names, alt text and structured product data can help. Original photography also distinguishes your pages from competitors using the same manufacturer images. See our ecommerce SEO checklist for how images fit into wider on-page work.

In-house or professional?

Simple products on plain backgrounds can be shot well in-house with a modest setup, a consistent process and patience. Lifestyle imagery, reflective products, food and fashion on models may benefit from a professional. Some stores mix the two: professional hero and lifestyle images, with in-house shots for new variants and detail.

In-houseEquipment, space, staff time, editing and a learning curveSimple products, frequent new lines

Ask for quotes on the same list of products and shots so you can compare them fairly, and be clear about who may use the images and where. Our brief guide covers usage rights.

Product photography mistakes to avoid

  • Showing no sense of scale. Shoppers can be surprised by size, which may lead to returns.
  • Mixing crops and backgrounds. Collection pages become harder to scan.
  • Photographing only one variant. A colour swatch does not replace a real image.
  • Ignoring mobile. Check that key details remain visible on a small screen.
  • Uploading images at full size. Good photos are less useful if the page is slow.

Where photography fits in your store

Photography works alongside layout, copy and trust signals. A strong gallery can still be let down by a cluttered page. FAQs

What is ecommerce photography?

It is photography produced to sell products online, including plain main images, angles, close-ups, scale shots and lifestyle images. It is planned around what a shopper needs to know before buying.

How many photos should a product page have?

There is no fixed number. Include enough to answer the main questions: the whole product, key angles, details, scale and use. Simple products need fewer; complex or high-value ones need more.

Should product photos have a white background?

A plain, consistent background works well for main images and is required by some marketplaces. Lifestyle images can then add context and personality.

Can I use the manufacturer's photos?

Only if you have permission. Even then, original photography helps your pages stand out from other retailers selling the same item.

Do product images affect page speed?

They can. Resize and compress images, and lazy-load those further down the page.

Should every colour variant have its own photos?

Ideally, yes. Shoppers choosing a colour want to see that exact colour, and showing the selected variant's image when someone picks an option can reduce uncertainty. If budget is tight, prioritise your best-selling variants first and add the rest over time.

Is a smartphone good enough for product photos?

For simple products, a recent smartphone on a tripod with good, consistent lighting can produce usable images. Complex, reflective or fashion products may benefit from professional equipment and experience.

How often should I update product photos?

Update them whenever the product changes, when your brand style evolves, or when existing images no longer match the rest of the catalogue. Reviewing your gallery once a year is a sensible habit.

What should alt text say for product images?

Describe what the image shows in plain language, such as the product, colour and angle. Avoid stuffing it with keywords.
