Shopify Checkout Settings: How to Configure Your Checkout

A practical guide to Shopify checkout settings for UK stores, covering customer contact, accounts, form fields, marketing consent, order notes and testing.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify's checkout settings control what customers are asked for, how accounts work and how marketing consent is collected.
  • Asking only for information you genuinely need keeps the checkout simple and supports good data protection practice.
  • Test every change by placing test orders on mobile and desktop before relying on it.

The checkout is where browsing turns into a sale. On Shopify, the core checkout is hosted and maintained by the platform, which brings reliability and security, but it also means you shape it mainly through settings rather than by editing code freely.

Those settings matter. They decide what information customers must enter, whether they need an account, how marketing permission is requested and what happens after an order is placed. This guide walks through the main Shopify checkout settings and how to think about each one for a UK store.

Where checkout settings live

In the Shopify admin, checkout options sit within the settings area, usually under a checkout section. Related settings, such as payments, shipping, taxes and customer accounts, have their own sections. Shopify updates its admin from time to time, so labels and locations can change, but the principles stay the same.

Shopify also provides a checkout editor where you can adjust branding, such as logo, colours and fonts, so the checkout feels consistent with the rest of your store.

Customer contact method

You can choose how customers provide contact details at checkout, for example by email only or by phone or email. Consider how you will communicate about orders. Email is commonly used for confirmations and shipping updates, while phone contact may suit some delivery arrangements. Whatever you choose, make sure your transactional emails and notifications are set up to match.

Customer accounts

Shopify lets you decide whether customers can check out as guests, must sign in, or can choose either. Requiring an account adds a step that may put some shoppers off, especially first-time buyers. Optional accounts let returning customers save time without forcing everyone to register.

Shopify offers different account experiences, and the options available can change. Review what suits your customers, particularly if you offer subscriptions, wholesale pricing or order history features. Our guide to customer accounts and wishlists covers the wider decision.

Form fields

You can control several fields in the checkout form:

  • Full name: whether to require first and last name or last name only.
  • Company name: hidden, optional or required. Useful if you sell to businesses.
  • Address line two: hidden, optional or required. Many UK addresses need it, so hiding it is rarely wise.
  • Shipping address phone number: hidden, optional or required. Some couriers find it useful for delivery.

The general principle is to ask only for what you need. Each extra required field is one more thing for the customer to complete, and collecting unnecessary personal data runs against UK data protection principles. Guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) explains data minimisation if you want to check your approach.

Marketing consent

Shopify can show a checkbox inviting customers to sign up for email or SMS marketing at checkout. You can choose whether the box appears and, in some cases, whether it is pre-ticked.

In the UK, electronic marketing is governed by data protection law and the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations. Generally, valid consent needs to be freely given and clearly indicated, which is why pre-ticked boxes are widely considered unsuitable for collecting consent. There are specific rules for marketing to existing customers in some circumstances. This is general information only, so check the ICO's guidance or take advice on how the rules apply to your business.

Make sure the wording beside the checkbox explains clearly what customers are signing up for, and that your privacy notice covers it. Our article on website terms and privacy policies covers the basics.

Address autocomplete and accuracy

Shopify can suggest addresses as customers type, which speeds up checkout and reduces errors. Accurate addresses mean fewer failed deliveries and customer service queries. Check how suggestions behave for UK addresses during testing.

Tipping, order notes and extra information

Shopify includes optional features such as tipping, which suits some businesses but may feel out of place for others. Consider whether it fits your brand before enabling it.

Many stores want customers to leave notes, such as delivery instructions or gift messages. Depending on your theme and setup, this may be handled through cart notes or checkout customisation. Decide where this information should be collected and make sure your team sees it when fulfilling orders.

Policies and legal links

Shopify lets you add policies, such as refund, privacy, terms of service and shipping, and links them in the checkout footer. Make sure each policy is complete, up to date and reflects how you actually operate. UK consumer law gives shoppers certain rights when buying online, including cancellation rights in many cases, so your refund policy should be consistent with those rights. Again, take advice if you are unsure how the rules apply.

Order processing settings

Checkout settings also cover what happens after payment. Options can include whether to use the shipping address as the billing address by default, whether to automatically fulfil certain orders, such as digital products or gift cards, and whether to archive orders automatically once fulfilled and paid. Choose settings that match your fulfilment workflow, so your team sees what needs attention.

Payments, shipping and taxes

Although configured elsewhere, these settings shape the checkout experience directly. Offer payment methods your customers expect, present clear shipping options and costs, and configure taxes correctly for UK VAT. Surprises at checkout, such as unexpected delivery charges, are a frequent reason shoppers hesitate. Our guide to Shopify store setup covers these foundations.

Checkout branding

Use the checkout editor to add your logo and match colours and fonts to your store. A checkout that feels consistent with the rest of the site reassures customers that they are in the right place. Keep the design clean; the goal is to help customers complete their purchase without distraction.

Customising beyond settings

For more advanced needs, Shopify provides extension points that allow approved apps and custom development to add features to the checkout, such as extra fields or messages. What is possible depends on your Shopify plan and the platform's current capabilities. Shopify Plus offers additional checkout customisation options. Every addition should earn its place, because each extra element adds something for customers to process. An apps audit helps keep checkout extensions under control.

Testing your checkout

After changing checkout settings, test thoroughly:

  • place test orders using Shopify's test payment options,
  • try guest checkout and account checkout if both are available,
  • test on mobile and desktop,
  • check discount codes, shipping options and tax calculations,
  • confirm notification emails arrive and look right, and
  • make sure orders appear correctly in the admin for fulfilment.

Remember to switch test mode off and cancel test orders afterwards.

Improving checkout over time

Watch your analytics for where shoppers drop out between adding to cart and completing an order. Abandoned checkouts can point to problems such as unexpected costs, limited payment options or confusing fields. Our guide to Shopify conversion rate optimisation explores this further.

Building trust at checkout

Customers are being asked to hand over payment and personal details, so reassurance matters. Shopify's hosted checkout is secure by design, but customers still look for signs that they are dealing with a genuine, well-run business. Helpful touches include:

  • clear delivery information, including expected delivery options and any restrictions,
  • easily found links to your refund and shipping policies,
  • contact details or a link to help, so customers know they can reach you,
  • consistent branding, so the checkout feels part of your store, and
  • accurate product names and images in the order summary.

Avoid cluttering the checkout with badges or claims that cannot be backed up. Honest, clear information tends to build more confidence than decoration.

It is also worth reviewing the checkout through the eyes of a first-time customer every so often. Ask someone unfamiliar with your store to buy a low-value item and note anything that confuses them.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forcing account creation when guest checkout would do.
  • Requiring fields you never use.
  • Pre-ticking marketing consent boxes.
  • Leaving placeholder or outdated policies in place.
  • Changing settings without placing a test order.
  • Adding checkout features that distract from completing the purchase.

FAQs

Can I edit the Shopify checkout?

You can configure it through settings and brand it with the checkout editor. Deeper changes use Shopify's checkout extensions, with more options available on Shopify Plus.

Should I allow guest checkout?

Many stores offer guest checkout alongside optional accounts, so first-time buyers are not forced to register. Choose what suits your customers.

Can I pre-tick the marketing checkbox?

Shopify may allow it, but pre-ticked boxes are generally not considered valid consent under UK rules. Check the ICO's guidance or take advice.

How do I test my Shopify checkout?

Use Shopify's test payment options to place orders, check emails and admin records, then switch test mode off when finished.

Why do customers abandon checkout?

Reasons vary, but unexpected costs, limited payment options and lengthy forms are frequent factors worth reviewing.
