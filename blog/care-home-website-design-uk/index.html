Care Home Website Design: Reassuring Families and Making Enquiries Easy

Website design for UK care homes: room and care type pages, inspection ratings, fees information, virtual tours, enquiry forms and reassuring families.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Care home websites are usually read by worried family members making a big decision, often quickly, so clarity and warmth matter more than flashy design.
  • Showing your current inspection rating accurately, explaining care types and being as open as you can about fees all help families shortlist with confidence.
  • Real photos of rooms, communal spaces, gardens and daily life do far more to reassure people than stock images.

Choosing a care home is one of the hardest decisions a family can make. Often it happens under pressure, after a hospital stay or a sudden change in someone's health. The people reading a care home website are usually adult children or partners, searching on a phone late in the evening, trying to work out whether a home is safe, kind and suitable. The website has a real job to do: answer their questions honestly, help them picture their relative living there, and make it easy to arrange a visit.

This guide covers website design for UK care homes, including residential, nursing and dementia care homes, whether independent or part of a small group. It focuses on the website itself. For wider promotion, including local search and referrals, see our guide to care home marketing.

Who the website is really for

Most visitors are not the future resident. They are relatives, and sometimes social workers, hospital discharge teams or financial advisers. Each group has slightly different needs:

  • Families want reassurance about care quality, kindness, activities, food and visiting.
  • Professionals want quick facts: care types, bed availability, funding accepted and how to make a referral.
  • Prospective residents, where able, want to know what daily life is like and how much independence they will keep.

A good structure serves all of them without making anyone dig. Clear navigation, plain headings and an obvious way to get in touch go a long way. Our guide to website navigation structure covers how to organise pages.

Explaining types of care

Families are often unsure of the difference between residential care, nursing care, dementia care and respite care. Your website can help by explaining each type you offer in plain language:

  • What the care type means and who it suits.
  • Whether the home is registered to provide it.
  • Any specialist environments, such as a dedicated dementia unit.
  • Whether you offer short stays, respite or trial stays.
  • End of life care, if provided, written with sensitivity.

Only describe the care you are registered and staffed to provide. If there are needs you cannot meet, it is kinder to say so than to raise false hopes.

Showing your inspection rating

Inspection reports are one of the first things many families check. In England, care homes are inspected by the Care Quality Commission. In Scotland it is the Care Inspectorate, in Wales Care Inspectorate Wales, and in Northern Ireland the Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority. Each publishes reports online.

In England, registered providers are generally required to display their CQC rating on their website, and there are rules about how it is shown. Check current CQC guidance to make sure your website meets the requirements. Elsewhere in the UK, follow the relevant regulator's guidance. In practice, a good approach is to:

  • Show the current rating clearly, using the official format where one is required.
  • Link to the full report on the regulator's site.
  • Keep it updated promptly after each inspection.
  • Avoid quoting selectively in a way that could mislead.

If a recent report raised concerns, being open about what you have done in response can build more trust than silence.

Rooms, spaces and daily life

Families want to picture their relative living in the home. Real photography is essential here. Useful photos include:

  • Typical bedrooms, noting en suite facilities where available.
  • Lounges, dining rooms and quiet spaces.
  • Gardens and outdoor areas.
  • Activities and everyday moments, with consent from anyone pictured.
  • The entrance and exterior, so visitors recognise the home when they arrive.

Consent is particularly important when photographing residents, especially those who may lack capacity. Follow your own policies and take advice where needed. Many homes rely mainly on photos of spaces, staff and activities where residents are not identifiable.

A short video walkthrough or virtual tour can help families who live far away. Our website video guide covers how to add video without slowing pages down.

Beyond photos, describe daily life: menus and dietary needs, activities, outings, hairdressing, faith services, visiting arrangements and how families can stay in touch.

Being open about fees

Fees are often the question families are most anxious about and least able to find. Price transparency is increasingly expected in the sector, and consumer law guidance on care home fees has emphasised giving clear information upfront. Check current guidance from the Competition and Markets Authority and your regulator on what you should publish. Helpful information can include:

  • Weekly fees or fee ranges by care type, if you publish them.
  • What is included and what costs extra, such as hairdressing or newspapers.
  • Deposits, notice periods and how fee increases are handled.
  • Whether you accept local authority funded residents or NHS Continuing Healthcare, and any top-up arrangements.
  • A link to your terms and conditions.

Funding is complicated, so pointing families to official guidance on paying for care, alongside your own information, is genuinely useful.

Staff and the people behind the care

Care is delivered by people, and families want to know who they are. Consider:

  • A welcome from the registered manager, with a photo.
  • Introductions to key staff such as the deputy manager, activities coordinator or chef, with consent.
  • Information about staff training, described accurately.
  • Genuine reviews from families, linked to their source.

Our guide to website trust signals has more on presenting this honestly.

Enquiries, visits and availability

The main action on a care home site is usually arranging a visit or making an enquiry. Make it easy:

  • A phone number in the header, tappable on mobile, with the hours it is answered.
  • A short enquiry form asking only what you need to respond: name, contact details, relationship, care type and timescale.
  • A clear invitation to visit, with how to book a tour.
  • A separate route for professionals making referrals, if useful.

Some homes show current availability. If you do, keep it accurate, as an outdated vacancy notice causes frustration. Because enquiry forms may involve health information about the future resident, keep questions minimal and make sure your privacy notice covers how the data is handled. See our guide to website contact forms.

Accessibility and readability

Many visitors are older, may have visual impairments or may be reading while stressed. Use readable text sizes, strong contrast, plain language and simple layouts. Avoid clutter and pop-ups. Our website accessibility guide covers the standards to aim for. Make sure the site works well on mobile, since many families search on their phones.

Groups with several homes

If you run more than one home, give each its own section with its own photos, rating, care types, fees information and contact details. Families are choosing a home, not a brand, so each home should feel distinct. A location search can help if the group is large.

Recruitment

Care homes often recruit continually, and the website is a good place for a careers section. Describe what it is like to work there, the roles you hire for and how to apply. Keeping recruitment separate from the family-facing content stops either audience getting lost.

Choosing a platform

Most care home sites are content and enquiry sites, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all be suitable. WordPress can be useful for groups that need several home pages and a careers section managed by different people. Consider who will keep ratings, fees and availability updated. FAQs

Do care homes have to show their CQC rating on their website?

In England, registered providers are generally required to display their CQC rating on their website. Check current CQC guidance for the exact requirements, and follow your own regulator's guidance in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.

Should care homes publish their fees online?

Clear fees information is increasingly expected, and consumer law guidance has stressed giving families information upfront. Check current guidance on what applies to your home.

Can care homes use photos of residents?

Only with appropriate consent. Take particular care where residents may lack capacity, and consider using photos of spaces, staff and activities instead.

What is the most important page on a care home website?

Usually the page for each care type, supported by clear contact details and an easy way to arrange a visit.

Should a care home show room availability?

It can help, but only if it is kept up to date. An inaccurate vacancy notice frustrates families and professionals.
