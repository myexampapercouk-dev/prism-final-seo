Scaffolding Company Website Design: Winning Trade and Domestic Work Online

Scaffolding company website design for UK firms: trade and domestic routes, quote forms that capture the right detail, safety wording and project galleries.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Scaffolders serve builders, roofers and contractors as well as homeowners, so the website should give trade and domestic visitors their own clear routes.
  • A quote form that asks for the right details, such as property type, height, access and duration, saves time on both sides.
  • Describe training, insurance and safety practice accurately, and hedge points about permits and regulations rather than stating them as fixed rules.

A scaffolding company sits in an unusual position online. Much of its work comes from other trades: roofers, builders, renderers, painters and main contractors who need access to do their own jobs. At the same time, homeowners search for scaffolding when they are planning a loft conversion, chimney repair or new roof and want to understand what is involved. A scaffolding website needs to work for both, and it needs to show competence and safety at a glance, because nobody wants to hire a scaffolder they are unsure about.

This guide focuses on the website itself. For promotion more broadly, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK. If you work mainly as part of larger building projects, our guide to construction company website design covers the main contractor's perspective, which is useful context for how your trade clients present themselves.

Two audiences, two routes

  • Trade and commercial clients. Builders, roofers, developers, facilities managers and contractors. They want to know you can turn up when promised, handle the type of structure they need, and provide the paperwork their own clients or site managers expect.
  • Domestic customers. Homeowners who have often never hired a scaffolder before. They want to know what the process looks like, how long the scaffold will be up, what it costs in general terms and whether they need permission from anyone.

The homepage can offer both a "for trade and contractors" route and a "for homeowners" route, each leading to pages written for that reader. Trade pages can be brief and factual; domestic pages benefit from more explanation.

Service pages by type of job

Scaffolding covers a wide range of work. Separate pages, for the services you actually offer, help visitors find what they need and help search engines understand each page. Examples include:

  • Domestic scaffolding for roofs, chimneys, gutters and painting.
  • Scaffolding for extensions and loft conversions.
  • Commercial and industrial scaffolding.
  • Temporary roofs and weather protection.
  • Chimney scaffolds and smaller access structures.
  • Edge protection, stair towers or birdcage scaffolds, where relevant.
  • Scaffold alarms or security measures, if you supply them.

Each page can explain when that type of scaffold is used, what the customer needs to think about, and what information you need to quote. Avoid promising particular timescales for erection or dismantling unless they genuinely reflect your scheduling.

A quote form that asks the right questions

Scaffolding quotes depend on details that customers do not always think to give: the type of property, number of storeys, which elevations need covering, what the work is for, access for vehicles and materials, and how long the scaffold will be needed. A well-designed quote form helps by asking for these in plain language. Useful fields can include:

  • Property type and approximate height or number of storeys.
  • Which sides of the building need scaffolding.
  • The job the scaffold is for, such as roofing or rendering.
  • Expected hire period.
  • Whether the scaffold would go on a pavement, road or neighbour's land.
  • Photo uploads of the building.

Keep required fields to a minimum, so people are not put off, and make optional fields clearly optional. Trade clients may prefer to send drawings or a job specification, so allow attachments where practical. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design, spam protection and privacy.

Explaining permits and neighbours, carefully

Homeowners often do not realise that scaffolding on a public pavement or road may need a licence from the local council, or that access over a neighbour's property can raise its own issues. A short explainer page is useful and shows expertise. Keep it hedged: requirements vary between councils, and the rules on neighbours and party walls depend on circumstances. Explain whether you usually handle licence applications for customers, if you do, and suggest customers check with their council or seek advice where the situation is unusual. Do not present this as legal advice.

Safety, training and insurance

Scaffolding is safety-critical work, and visitors want reassurance. Present your credentials accurately and specifically:

  • Describe the training and cards your scaffolders hold, using the correct names, and keep this up to date.
  • State the insurance cover you hold, without implying figures or cover you do not have.
  • Mention memberships of trade associations or accreditation schemes only if you belong to them, and follow each scheme's rules on logos.
  • Explain your approach to inspections and handover, such as whether you provide handover certificates and inspection records, if that is your practice.

Work at height in the UK is subject to health and safety law, and the Health and Safety Executive publishes guidance on scaffolding. You can refer to this in general terms, but avoid stating specific legal duties as if they were your own interpretation, and avoid claims such as "fully compliant" without explaining what that means for your business. Trade clients may also ask for documents such as risk assessments and method statements; saying that these are available on request, if they are, can be enough on the website.

Projects that show capability

Photos of real scaffolds are among the most persuasive content you can have. A project gallery or set of short case studies can show the range of structures you handle, from a terraced house chimney to a commercial job. For each project, a sentence or two on the type of job, the challenge and the structure used is more helpful than a photo alone.

Get permission from clients before naming them or showing identifiable properties, and keep any figures or claims about projects accurate. Avoid stock images of scaffolding: experienced trade clients will spot them, and they undermine trust.

Trade accounts and repeat work

If a large share of your work comes from repeat trade clients, consider a dedicated page explaining how you work with contractors: how to request a scaffold, how adaptations and extensions are handled, how hire periods and extensions are charged in principle, and who to contact. Some firms offer a simple online request form for existing clients. Only describe processes you actually follow, and keep the page practical rather than salesy.

Pricing information

Scaffolding pricing varies too much to publish fixed prices for most jobs, but explaining what affects the price helps both domestic and trade visitors. Factors can include height, the number of elevations, access, hire duration, extra features such as temporary roofs, and any licence fees. Be clear about how hire extensions are charged and whether figures include VAT. Our guide to pricing page design offers ideas on presenting this.

Coverage area and local detail

Scaffolders often work within a practical radius of their yard. Show that area clearly, and consider area pages only where you have real local content, such as projects completed there. Consistent business details across your site and Google Business Profile support local visibility, and our local SEO guide explains the wider picture.

Mobile and speed

Trade clients often check suppliers from site on a phone, so the site needs to load quickly and make calling or requesting a quote easy. Compress project photos so galleries do not slow pages down. Our guides to mobile website design and website speed optimisation cover this in more depth.

Recruitment

Many scaffolding firms are regularly looking for scaffolders and labourers. A simple careers page that explains the roles you hire for, the training you support and how to apply can make recruitment easier. Avoid promising pay, progression or training you cannot guarantee, and handle applicant data in line with your privacy notice.

Platform choice

A scaffolding website is usually a content and lead-generation site rather than an online shop. WordPress suits firms that want many service and project pages, while Squarespace or Wix can work for a simpler setup. What matters most is fast loading, easy updating of the project gallery and a reliable quote form.

FAQs

Should a scaffolding website target homeowners or trade clients?

Usually both, with separate routes and pages, because their questions and decision-making are very different.

What should a scaffolding quote form ask?

Property type, height, which sides need covering, what the job is for, hire period, access and whether the scaffold goes on a public footway, with photo uploads where possible.

Should I explain scaffold licences?

A short, hedged explainer helps homeowners, noting that requirements vary by council and saying whether you handle applications.

Can I use stock photos of scaffolding?

It is better to use photos of your own work, with permission where properties or clients could be identified.

Should I publish prices?

Fixed prices are rarely practical, but explaining what affects cost and how hire extensions work is helpful.
