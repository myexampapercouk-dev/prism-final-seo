Hotel and B&B Website Design: Rooms, Rates and the Book Now Button

Website design for UK hotels, inns and B&Bs: room pages, booking engine placement, photography, location content, policies and building trust with guests.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A hotel or B&B website has one main job: help a guest picture their stay, check availability and book without friction.
  • Room pages, honest photography, clear policies and a well-placed booking button usually matter more than elaborate design effects.
  • The site works best when it is built around how guests actually decide: dates, price, room type, location and reassurance.

For a hotel, inn, guest house or bed and breakfast, the website is where browsing turns into a booking, or doesn't. Guests may find you through search, a map listing, social media or a booking platform, but many will visit your own site before deciding. If it is slow, confusing or out of date, they may go back to wherever they came from and book there, or book somewhere else entirely.

This guide covers website design for UK hotels and small accommodation businesses: independent and boutique hotels, country house hotels, pubs and inns with rooms, guest houses and B&Bs. It focuses on the design and structure of the site itself. For the commercial side of winning more bookings without third-party platforms, see our guide to hotel direct bookings, and for search visibility, our guide to hotel SEO. Much of what follows also applies to self-catering cottages and holiday lets, although their booking patterns differ.

What guests are trying to find out

People looking at accommodation usually arrive with a short list of questions. Your site should answer them quickly, on a phone as well as a laptop:

  • Is there availability on my dates, and what does it cost?
  • What are the rooms like, and which one suits us?
  • Where exactly is it, and what is nearby?
  • Is breakfast included? Is there parking? Can I bring my dog?
  • What is the cancellation policy?
  • Does it feel like the kind of place I want to stay?

The last question matters as much as the practical ones. A guest choosing a small hotel or B&B is often choosing an atmosphere, so the design, photography and writing should give an honest sense of what staying with you is like.

Make the booking button impossible to miss

Whatever booking engine or channel manager you use, the route to booking should be visible from every page. Common approaches include:

  • A persistent "Book now" or "Check availability" button in the header, including on mobile.
  • A date picker near the top of the homepage that passes dates straight into the booking engine.
  • Booking buttons on each room page, ideally pre-selecting that room type.

Many booking engines run on their own domain or in a pop-up, which can feel disjointed. Where your system allows, match its colours and fonts to your site and keep the hand-off smooth, so guests do not feel they have been sent somewhere unfamiliar. If you take bookings by phone or email instead, make those options just as prominent and give a clear idea of how quickly you reply. Our guide to online booking systems covers the wider choices.

Room pages that do the selling

Each room or room type usually deserves its own page, or at least its own clearly separated section. A good room page typically includes:

  • Several real photos of that room and its bathroom, not just one flattering angle.
  • Bed type and size, maximum occupancy and whether extra beds or cots are available.
  • Key features such as view, bath or shower, accessibility features, and whether it is on the ground floor.
  • A starting price or a clear route to live prices.
  • A booking button.

Be specific. "Cosy" can mean charming or cramped, so let photos and plain facts do the work. If some rooms are up steep stairs or in an annexe across the courtyard, say so. Guests who know what to expect are less likely to be disappointed.

Photography is the main design element

For accommodation, photography carries more weight than almost any layout choice. Professional photos of rooms, public spaces, food and surroundings tend to be one of the better investments a small hotel can make. A few principles:

  • Show rooms in good natural light, tidy and made up as a guest would find them.
  • Include the details guests care about: the bathroom, the breakfast, the garden, the bar.
  • Show the location honestly, including the view from the window if it is a selling point.
  • Keep photos current. If a room has been redecorated, update the pictures.

Large images can slow a site badly, especially on rural mobile connections. Images should be compressed and sized properly so the site stays fast. See our guides to website image optimisation and website speed.

Location and things to do

Many guests are choosing a destination first and accommodation second. A location page helps them understand where you are and why it is a good base. Consider including:

  • A map, directions and parking information.
  • Nearest railway station and approximate travel times.
  • Local walks, attractions, beaches, restaurants or events you can genuinely recommend.
  • Seasonal information, such as what the area is like in winter.

This content is useful for guests and can also help the site appear in searches about the area. Write it from your own knowledge rather than copying generic tourist descriptions, and keep opening details hedged or linked to official sources where they may change.

Dining, events and other parts of the business

If you have a restaurant, bar or event space, it may serve a different audience from your overnight guests: locals booking dinner, couples planning a celebration, businesses booking a meeting room. Give each its own clear section with its own call to action, such as a table booking link or an enquiry form, without cluttering the room booking journey. Sample menus are helpful, but mark them as samples if they change often.

Pubs with rooms often face a particular challenge here: the website has to work as a pub site for locals and as an accommodation site for visitors. Clear navigation, with rooms, food and drink, and events as separate top-level sections, usually helps. Our guide to website navigation covers this in more detail.

Policies and practical information

Guests want to know the small print before they book. Make it easy to find:

  • Check-in and check-out times.
  • Cancellation and deposit policy, in plain English.
  • Children, pets and accessibility information.
  • Breakfast times and dietary options.
  • What is and is not included in the price.

Prices shown should be clear about what they include, and any compulsory charges should be visible early rather than appearing at the last step. Consumer protection rules on price transparency apply to accommodation and have been tightening, so check current guidance on displaying prices and fees, and take advice if you are unsure. A clear FAQ page can help; see our guide to FAQ pages.

Accessibility information

Guests with access needs often find accommodation websites vague. Detailed information, such as step-free routes, door widths, bathroom layouts, grab rails and lift access, helps people decide whether you are suitable for them. Photos of accessible rooms and bathrooms are especially useful. Equally, if some areas are not accessible, saying so plainly is better than leaving guests to find out on arrival.

The website itself should also be usable for people with disabilities, with readable text, good contrast, keyboard-friendly navigation and descriptive alt text on images. The Equality Act 2010 applies to services offered to the public, and accessible websites are widely considered good practice. See our guide to website accessibility.

Reviews and trust

Guests often check reviews before booking. You can reference genuine reviews on your own site, but they must be real, not edited to change their meaning, and not selected in a way that misleads. Linking to your Google reviews or showing a short, accurate selection is usually more credible than a page of anonymous praise. Our guide to website trust signals covers other ways to build confidence, such as clear contact details, a real address and an introduction to the owners or team.

Choosing a platform

Most small hotels and B&Bs use a content management system such as WordPress, Squarespace or Wix, with a separate booking engine embedded or linked. The right choice depends on how much you want to edit yourself, how well your booking system integrates and how much flexibility you need. WordPress offers the most flexibility; hosted builders can be simpler to manage. See our guides to website builders vs custom websites and WordPress for small businesses.

Whatever you choose, check that your booking engine works reliably on mobile, that you can update rooms, prices and photos without help, and that the site loads quickly. If you are replacing an older site, take care not to lose existing search visibility; our guide to redesigning without losing rankings explains the main risks.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Hiding the booking button or making guests hunt for prices.
  • Outdated photos that no longer match the rooms.
  • Huge image files that make the site crawl on mobile.
  • Vague room descriptions that leave guests guessing.
  • Policies buried in a PDF or missing entirely.
  • A booking engine that looks and feels completely different from the main site.

FAQs

Does a small B&B need its own website if it is listed on booking platforms?

A website gives you a place you control, where guests can learn about you and potentially book directly. Platform listings and your own site can work alongside each other.

Where should the booking button go on a hotel website?

Usually in the header on every page, including on mobile, with additional buttons on each room page and near the top of the homepage.

Should every room have its own page?

Where rooms differ meaningfully, separate pages or sections help guests compare and choose. Very similar rooms can be grouped as one room type.

How important is photography for a hotel website?

Very. Photos are often the main way guests judge a room. Real, current, well-lit images tend to matter more than any design effect.

What should a hotel include about cancellation on its website?

A plain-English summary of the cancellation and deposit policy, easy to find before booking. Check current consumer guidance on price and terms transparency.
