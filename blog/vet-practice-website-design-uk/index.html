Vet Practice Website Design: Emergencies, Registration and Pet Owners

Website design for UK veterinary practices: emergency information, new client registration, appointment booking, health plans, team pages and owner trust.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Emergency and out-of-hours information should be the easiest thing to find on a vet website, on every page and especially on mobile.
  • New client registration, appointment booking and repeat prescription requests are the everyday tasks the site can take off the phone line.
  • Pet owners choose a vet partly on trust, so honest team profiles, clear fees information and photos of the practice help more than polished slogans.

A veterinary practice website gets visited in very different moods. One owner is calmly comparing practices after moving house. Another is ordering a repeat prescription. Another is panicking at midnight because their dog has eaten something it should not have. A good vet website has to serve all three, and the order of priorities matters. Emergency information first, everyday tasks second, and the reasons to choose your practice woven throughout.

This guide covers website design for UK veterinary practices, from small independent surgeries to multi-site practices, including small animal, mixed and equine. It focuses on the site itself. For promoting a practice more broadly, including local search and social media, see our guide to vet practice marketing.

Emergency information comes first

When an animal is in trouble, the owner needs one thing quickly: who to call. Your website should make that impossible to miss.

  • Show the emergency phone number in the header on every page, tappable on mobile.
  • Explain clearly what happens out of hours. Many practices use a separate out-of-hours provider, and owners need to know where to go and that they should phone ahead.
  • Give the out-of-hours address, with a map link, if it differs from your own.
  • Keep the wording calm and simple. Owners reading it may be stressed.

Check that this information is correct whenever arrangements change. An out-of-date emergency number on a vet website is a serious problem, so it is worth putting a regular check in the diary. The RCVS sets professional expectations around emergency cover, so follow their guidance on what owners should be told.

Registering as a new client

New client registration is a natural task to move online. A registration form might ask for:

  • Owner name, address and contact details.
  • Each animal's name, species, breed, age and sex.
  • Neutering and vaccination status.
  • Any known conditions or current medications.
  • Previous practice details, so records can be requested.
  • Insurance provider, if the owner has one.

Keep the form manageable. Allowing owners to add several pets without repeating their own details makes a real difference for multi-pet households. Explain what happens next, such as whether you will contact them to confirm or whether they can book straight away. Our guide to website contact forms covers good form design.

Because you are collecting personal data, your privacy notice should explain how it is used and stored, and how long you keep it.

Appointments and online booking

Many practices now offer online booking for routine appointments, often through a feature of their practice management system. If yours supports it, the website should give it prominence. Consider:

  • Which appointment types can be booked online, such as vaccinations, nurse clinics or routine consultations.
  • Which should still be phoned through, such as urgent concerns or procedures needing an admission conversation.
  • Making the distinction clear so owners do not book a routine slot for something urgent.
  • Reminders and confirmation messages, which can help reduce missed appointments.

If online booking is not practical, a request form plus a clear phone number is fine. See our guide to online booking systems for the general options.

Repeat prescriptions

Repeat prescription requests are one of the most common calls a practice receives. A simple online request form can help, covering the owner, the animal, the medication and preferred collection or delivery method. Be clear about:

  • How much notice you need.
  • When a check-up is required before a prescription can be renewed.
  • Whether you can provide written prescriptions for owners who want to buy medicines elsewhere, and any fee involved.

Rules on prescribing and supplying veterinary medicines are set by regulation and professional guidance, so keep the wording consistent with what your vets are permitted and required to do.

Explaining services clearly

Service pages help owners understand what you offer and help search engines match your site to what people search for. Depending on your practice, pages might cover:

  • Vaccinations and preventive care.
  • Neutering.
  • Dental care.
  • Surgery and diagnostics.
  • Nurse clinics, such as weight or senior pet checks.
  • Exotic pets, if you genuinely see them.
  • Farm or equine services, if you are a mixed or equine practice.
  • Euthanasia and aftercare, written with care.

For each, explain what it involves and what owners can expect, in plain language. Avoid overstating outcomes. A page on euthanasia deserves particular care: gentle wording, clear practical information about home visits or quiet rooms if you offer them, and information about cremation or aftercare options.

Being open about fees

Owners often worry about vet costs, and pricing transparency in the veterinary sector has been receiving regulatory attention. Practices may be expected to share more pricing information than in the past, so check current RCVS and regulatory guidance on what you should publish. Even where it is not required, a website can help by:

  • Publishing prices for common routine services, or a downloadable price list.
  • Explaining how estimates work for more complex treatment.
  • Describing payment options and when payment is due.
  • Explaining how you work with pet insurance claims.
  • Stating clearly who owns the practice, if that is relevant to the guidance that applies to you.

Keep any published prices up to date. Out-of-date prices cause awkward conversations at the reception desk.

Pet health plans

Many practices offer a health plan covering routine care for a monthly payment. If you do, give it a dedicated page that explains:

  • What is included and what is not.
  • The monthly cost for each species or size, if you choose to publish it.
  • How to sign up, and any minimum term.
  • How the plan differs from insurance, since owners often confuse the two.

A sign-up form or link to your plan provider can sit at the bottom of the page. Make sure the wording matches the actual plan terms.

Team pages and trust

Owners like to know who will be looking after their animal. A team page can include photos, roles and a little about each vet, nurse and receptionist, with their consent. Other trust elements include:

  • RCVS registration of vets and veterinary nurses, described accurately.
  • Practice Standards Scheme accreditation, only if you hold it, and at the correct level.
  • Photos of the waiting room, consult rooms and any separate cat or dog areas.
  • Genuine client reviews, linked to their source.
  • Any special interests or further qualifications your vets genuinely hold.

Our guide to website trust signals covers this in more detail. Photos of real staff and real rooms usually do more than stock images of puppies.

Pet advice and seasonal content

Helpful content can support both owners and search visibility. Examples include guidance on seasonal hazards, preparing for fireworks, travelling with pets or caring for older animals. Keep it accurate, practical and clearly signposted to booking an appointment where needed. Write for owners, not other vets, and review older articles periodically so advice stays current.

Multi-site practices

If you have several branches, give each its own page with address, hours, team, parking and services. Make it clear which branch offers which services, especially if surgery or certain clinics happen at only one site. Let owners choose a branch when booking or registering.

Choosing a platform

Vet websites are largely service sites with forms and a booking link, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all be suitable. Consider how the site needs to connect with your practice management system, who will keep emergency information and prices updated, and whether you might add a shop for pet products later. FAQs

Where should emergency information go on a vet website?

In the header of every page, with a tappable phone number on mobile, and on a dedicated page explaining out-of-hours arrangements and where to go.

Should vet practices publish their prices?

Publishing prices for common services helps owners, and regulatory expectations around price transparency have been developing. Check current RCVS and regulatory guidance for what applies to your practice.

Can clients register with a vet online?

Yes, an online registration form is common and practical. Allow multiple pets per owner and explain what happens after the form is submitted.

Should a vet website offer online booking?

For routine appointments it can save time, often through your practice management system. Make clear which issues should be phoned through instead.

How should a vet website handle euthanasia information?

With gentle, clear wording that explains options such as home visits, quiet rooms and aftercare, so owners can find what they need at a difficult time.
