Image Alt Text: How to Write Useful Descriptions for Your Website

How to write image alt text that helps screen reader users and search engines, with examples for products, logos, team photos, charts and decorative images.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 6 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Alt text describes the purpose and meaningful content of an image for people who cannot see it.
  • Good descriptions depend on context, so the same photo may need different wording on different pages.
  • Decorative images should usually have empty alt text, while logos, buttons and linked images need text that explains their function.

Alt text is a short written alternative to an image. Most visitors never see it, yet it does an important job. A screen reader announces it to people with visual impairments. A browser may display it when an image fails to load. Search engines can also use it to help understand what an image shows and how it relates to the page.

Small business websites often get it wrong in one of two ways: images have no description at all, or the box has been stuffed with keywords. This guide explains how to write useful alt text, with examples you can adapt for product photos, team pictures, logos, charts and more. It applies on WordPress, Shopify and most other website platforms.

What alt text is and where it lives

Alt is short for alternative. In a web page, it is an attribute attached to the image in the code. You do not need to touch the code to add it, because website platforms normally provide a field for it when you upload or insert an image.

Alt text is easily confused with two other things.

  • A caption is visible on the page, usually below the image, and is available to everyone.
  • A title or filename identifies the image, but neither is a replacement for alt text. A filename such as IMG_2041 tells nobody anything.

An image can have both a caption and alt text. They should work together, without repeating each other word for word, as a screen reader may then read the same words twice.

Why it matters

Accessibility

People who use screen readers rely on alt text to know what an image conveys. Without it, they might hear the filename, or nothing at all, and miss information that sighted visitors take for granted. Text alternatives are a basic part of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, which are widely used as a standard for accessible websites.

UK businesses also have legal duties around treating disabled people fairly, which can extend to websites. How those duties apply depends on your circumstances, so take legal advice if you need certainty. Our website accessibility guide covers the wider picture.

Search

Search engines use several signals to understand images, including the surrounding text and the alt text. A clear description may help an image appear in image search, and it adds context to the page as a whole. Treat this as a useful side benefit and not the main reason. Writing for people usually produces the right result for search engines too.

Failed images

If a connection is poor or a file is missing, browsers can show the alt text in place of the image. It is a small safeguard, but it can keep important information on the page.

How to write good alt text

A helpful test is to imagine describing the page to someone over the phone. What would you say about this image so they did not miss anything important? Then apply these principles.

  • Be specific. Say what matters in the picture and not just the broad category.
  • Keep it concise. A sentence or short phrase is usually enough. Longer explanations belong in the page text.
  • Consider context. Describe what the image contributes to this page.
  • Skip the preamble. Avoid starting with image of or picture of, as screen readers normally announce that it is an image.
  • Include visible text. If words in the image carry meaning, include them in the description.
  • Write naturally. Use a keyword only where it is an honest description of the picture.

Why context changes the description

There is rarely one correct description for a photograph, as it depends on why the image is there. Imagine a photo of a florist arranging a bouquet in her shop.

PagePossible alt text
About pageShop owner arranging a bouquet at the counter of her florist shop
Wedding flowers pageFlorist tying a white rose and eucalyptus bridal bouquet
Workshop pageTutor showing how to bind stems when building a hand-tied bouquet

Each is accurate, and each focuses on what that page is about. This is why reusing a single description across a whole site is often a missed opportunity, depending on how your platform stores this information.

Examples by image type

Product photos

Include details that help someone decide: product type, colour, material, and the angle or feature being shown. Where a product has several images, make each one distinct.

  • Weak: Blue mug
  • Stronger: Navy stoneware mug with a speckled glaze, shown from the side
  • Second image: Close view of the rounded handle on the navy stoneware mug

Our guide to ecommerce product photography covers planning the images themselves.

Team and people photos

If the person is named nearby and their appearance is not relevant, a short description is plenty, such as naming the person and their role. Be careful when describing physical traits, and only do so where they matter to the content.

Logos

Give the name of the company, and add the word logo where it is useful. If the logo links to your homepage, the description should make that clear, for example with the business name followed by home.

Icons and linked images

When an image is a link or button, describe what it does and not how it looks. A magnifying glass icon that opens a search box should say Search and not Magnifying glass. A shopping bag icon should say something like View basket.

Charts and diagrams

A short description cannot usually carry a detailed chart. Use the alt text to state what the chart shows and its main point, then give the detail in the text around it or in a table. Our guide to tables on mobile may help if you present the data that way.

Images containing text

Offers, quotes and banners with text in the image need that text repeating in the alt text. Better still, use real text on the page where possible, as it can be resized, translated and read more reliably.

Screenshots

Focus on what the reader needs to learn. Do not describe every menu and button. You might say which screen it shows and point out the setting being discussed.

Decorative images

Background textures, dividers and flourishes add nothing a screen reader user needs. These can usually have empty alt text, which tells assistive technology to skip them. Leaving the alt attribute off entirely is different, and a screen reader might then read the filename instead.

Many platforms handle this by letting you leave the field blank, but behaviour differs. If accessibility is a priority for your site, ask your developer how your theme outputs empty descriptions. A photo that sits alongside text and adds no new information may also count as decorative, though this is a judgement call.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Keyword stuffing. A string such as plumber London emergency plumber cheap plumber is unhelpful to listeners and may look like spam to search engines.
  • Using the filename. Automated text such as DSC_0091 is meaningless.
  • Repeating the same text everywhere. Identical descriptions on a gallery of different pictures tell people very little.
  • Repeating the caption. If the caption says it all, the alt text can add something different or be kept brief.
  • Making unsupported claims. Alt text should describe the image, not advertise. Marketing lines belong in your copy.
  • Relying wholly on automatic descriptions. Generated text can be a helpful starting point, but it may miss context or get details wrong, so check it.

Adding alt text on common platforms

WordPress

In the block editor, select an image block and find the alternative text field in the block settings sidebar. You can also add text in the Media Library when viewing an image. Be aware that changing it in one place may not update copies of the image already inserted in pages, so check existing content separately. Our media library guide offers tips on managing files.

Shopify

Shopify lets you add alt text to product images from the product screen, and to files elsewhere in the admin. Our Shopify product media guide covers this in more detail.

Other website builders

Most builders have an option in the image settings, sometimes labelled alt text, image description or something similar. Check your platform's help pages if you cannot find it.

How to fix alt text across an existing site

Tackling a whole site at once can feel daunting, particularly if nobody has looked at this before. Breaking the task into stages keeps it manageable.

  1. Run a crawl or audit tool to list images with missing alt text.
  2. Prioritise your most important pages first: the homepage, key service pages, top products and contact details.
  3. Pay particular attention to linked images and buttons, as missing text there can stop people using the site.
  4. Agree simple house rules so everyone who uploads images writes in the same way.
  5. Add alt text to your publishing checklist so new content is covered from the start.
  6. Try a screen reader on a few pages, or ask someone who uses one for feedback.

This fits naturally alongside compression and resizing work, which our image optimisation guide explains, or a broader review such as a content audit.

FAQs

How long should alt text be?

There is no single official limit, but short is generally better. A concise sentence or phrase suits most images. If you need much more, put the detail in the page text.

Does every image need alt text?

Every meaningful image needs a useful description. Purely decorative images can usually have empty alt text so that screen readers skip over them.

Does alt text help SEO?

It can help search engines understand an image and may support image search visibility. It is one small factor among many, and it should be written for people first.

Should I include keywords in alt text?

Only where they naturally describe the image. If your page is about oak dining tables and the photo shows one, saying so is reasonable. Forcing in extra phrases is not.

Can AI write my alt text?

It can produce useful first drafts, especially for large catalogues. Review the results, as generated text may not reflect context or may describe details wrongly.

Is alt text a legal requirement in the UK?

Requirements depend on your organisation. Public sector websites have specific accessibility rules, and other businesses have wider equality duties. Take legal advice if you are unsure how this applies to you.
