Web Design in Leeds: Websites for Professional Services, Legal and Financial Firms

Web design for Leeds law firms, accountants and financial advisers: clear service pages, regulator details, fee information and enquiry routes that build trust.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Leeds professional services websites are judged on trust: clear expertise, visible regulation details, honest fee information and people a client can picture speaking to.
  • Regulated firms should check what their regulator expects on a website, such as price and complaints information, before a new site goes live.
  • Prism Works Studio works with Leeds businesses remotely, planning, writing and building sites over video calls and shared drafts.

Web design in Leeds for a law firm, accountancy practice or financial adviser has one central job: make a cautious, often stressed prospective client feel confident enough to get in touch. That depends less on striking visuals and more on clarity. Who are you, what do you specialise in, how are you regulated, roughly what will it cost and what happens after someone fills in the form? Prism Works Studio works with Leeds businesses remotely, so the planning, writing and build happen over video calls and shared documents rather than in a meeting room.

Leeds has a large professional and financial services community, from firms around Park Square, Wellington Place and the area near the station to practices in suburbs such as Headingley, Roundhay, Horsforth and Wetherby. Those firms range from sole practitioners to multi-office partnerships, but their websites face similar questions. This guide focuses on those questions rather than general web design advice.

Why trust is the whole brief

People rarely browse a solicitor's or adviser's website for fun. They arrive because something has happened: a house move, a divorce, a business sale, a tax letter, a pension decision. They want reassurance that you handle their kind of problem, that you are properly regulated and that contacting you will not commit them to anything.

A trust-first website usually gets these things right:

  • Specific practice areas, each with its own page, rather than one long list of everything the firm does.
  • Real people, with profiles that explain experience in plain English and photos that look like the person a client will meet.
  • Regulatory information that is easy to find, not hidden in tiny footer text.
  • Fee transparency where you can offer it, and an honest explanation of what affects cost where you cannot.
  • A low-pressure first step, such as a short enquiry form or call-back request with a clear note about what happens next.

Practice area pages that answer real questions

The page a prospective client lands on matters more than the homepage. Someone searching for help with a commercial lease or an inheritance tax question should reach a page that speaks directly to that issue. A useful structure for each practice area page is:

  1. A plain summary of who the service is for and the problems it solves.
  2. The main stages of the matter, explained without jargon.
  3. What affects cost and timescale, with a link to fee information where relevant.
  4. Who in the firm handles this work.
  5. Short, accurate answers to the questions clients tend to ask.
  6. A clear call to action.

Regulatory details belong in the design

Professional firms often treat regulatory text as something to add at the end. It is better to plan for it from the start, because it affects page layout, navigation and the content you need to gather.

Type of firmPoints to check with your regulator or compliance adviser
SolicitorsThe Solicitors Regulation Authority's transparency rules, which cover publishing price and service information for certain types of work, complaints procedures and display of regulatory status
Financial advisers and mortgage brokersFinancial Conduct Authority rules on financial promotions and status disclosures, plus how risk warnings appear on pages and forms
AccountantsRequirements from your professional body on describing membership and regulated services, and any anti-money laundering information you choose to explain
All firmsCompany details, privacy notice, cookie consent and accessible complaint information

This is a starting checklist, not legal advice. Rules change and apply differently depending on the services you offer, so confirm the specifics with your regulator's guidance or your compliance lead before launch. A designer's job is to make the required information clear and easy to find, not to decide what it should say.

Fee pages without fear

Many professional firms are reluctant to publish prices because every matter is different. Yet prospective clients often look for cost information early, and a page that explains how pricing works can filter out poor-fit enquiries and reassure good ones.

Options that tend to work, depending on your services and any regulatory requirements, include:

  • Fixed fees for well-defined services, with what is and is not included.
  • Price ranges with the factors that move a matter up or down the range.
  • Hourly rates by role, alongside an explanation of how time estimates are given.
  • A clear note on disbursements, VAT and third-party costs.

Whatever you publish, keep it current. Out-of-date prices create awkward conversations and can raise compliance questions.

Profiles that sound like people

Team pages are often the most visited pages on a professional services site, because clients want to know who they will deal with. Profiles written as a list of qualifications rarely help. A better profile explains the kinds of matters someone handles, the clients they work with and how they like to communicate, followed by qualifications and memberships.

Use recent, consistent photography and keep profiles up to date when people join, leave or change roles. Only mention accreditations, panel memberships or rankings the firm genuinely holds and is permitted to display.

SEO for Leeds professional firms

Search behaviour for professional services mixes local and topic-led queries. Someone might search for a family solicitor near them, or for an explanation of a specific tax rule and only later look for an adviser. A good site serves both.

  • Practice area plus location pages help with local searches, but only where you genuinely serve that area and have something useful to say.
  • Helpful guides on common client questions can attract people earlier in their research, as long as they are accurate and reviewed by a qualified person.
  • Author and reviewer details on guides support credibility for topics that affect people's money or legal position.
  • A consistent Google Business Profile for each office supports visibility in map results.

Enquiry forms and confidentiality

Prospective clients may share sensitive details in an enquiry form without being asked. Good form design helps manage that. Ask only for what you need to respond, add a short note asking people not to include confidential details at first contact, and explain how quickly someone will reply.

Think about where submissions go, who can see them and how long they are kept. Your privacy notice should explain this in line with UK GDPR guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office. Our article on website contact forms covers layout and wording that can reduce drop-off.

Multi-office and multi-service firms

Firms with offices across West Yorkshire or beyond need a structure that avoids duplicate pages. Rather than copying the same practice area page for every town, consider one strong page per service with office information linked from it, plus individual office pages that carry genuinely local detail such as parking, accessibility and which team members are based there. Our guide to multi-location SEO explains the approach.

Writing in plain English

Legal and financial writing can drift towards formality. A website is not a letter of engagement. Short sentences, everyday words and clear headings help clients understand their options and help search engines understand your pages. Our guide to website copywriting has practical tips for turning expert knowledge into readable pages.

Accessibility matters for client care

Clients of professional firms include older people, people with disabilities and people reading in a second language. Readable fonts, good colour contrast, descriptive links and forms that work with keyboards and screen readers are part of serving them well. Our website accessibility guide explains the basics.

FAQs

What should a law firm website in Leeds include?

Separate practice area pages, team profiles, clear regulatory and complaints information, any price information your regulator expects, and a simple enquiry route with a note on what happens next.

Do solicitors have to publish prices on their website?

For certain types of work, the Solicitors Regulation Authority's transparency rules require price and service information to be published. Check the current rules for the services you offer, as requirements depend on your practice areas.

Can a financial adviser write guides on their website?

Yes, and they can be useful for search visibility. Content that could count as a financial promotion should be checked against FCA rules, so involve your compliance lead before publishing.

How should we handle sensitive information in enquiry forms?

Ask for the minimum, invite people not to share confidential details at first contact, store submissions securely and explain your handling in a privacy notice.

Should every office have its own page?

If you have more than one office, yes, but give each page genuinely local information. Avoid duplicating practice area pages for every location.
