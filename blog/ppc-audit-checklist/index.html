PPC Audit Checklist: A Google Ads Audit with a Symptom-to-Fix Table

A PPC audit checklist reviews tracking, structure, keywords, bids, ads, landing pages and Shopping feeds. Use this Google Ads audit and symptom-fix table.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Audit conversion tracking first: every other finding depends on whether the numbers are right.
  • Work from symptoms to causes: a symptom-to-fix table stops you changing bids when the real problem is the landing page.
  • Run a light audit monthly and a full audit quarterly or before any change of agency or budget.

A PPC audit checklist is a structured review of a Google Ads account covering conversion tracking, account structure, keywords and search terms, bidding, ads, landing pages and, for retailers, the Shopping feed. Start with tracking, because if conversions are counted wrongly every other decision in the account is built on bad data.

This checklist is written for UK business owners and in-house marketers who want to check an account themselves or hold an agency to account. It complements our guides to PPC management costs and choosing a Google Ads agency in the UK.

Before you start the audit

  • Get admin or at least standard access to Google Ads, GA4, Google Tag Manager and Merchant Center.
  • Pick a review window: the last 90 days is usually enough, with a year-on-year comparison for seasonal businesses.
  • Gather your real numbers: orders, revenue, leads and margins from your store or CRM, so you can compare them with what Ads reports.
  • Write down the business goal: profitable sales, qualified leads, bookings. Audit against that, not against click-through rate.

PPC audit checklist: the eight areas

1. Conversion tracking

  • Primary conversion actions match real business outcomes (purchases, qualified leads), not page views or button clicks.
  • Purchase values are passed dynamically and match order totals.
  • No duplicate counting from both a GA4 import and a Google Ads tag.
  • Enhanced conversions and consent mode are configured in line with UK GDPR and PECR.
  • Phone calls and form submissions are tracked if they matter to you.

2. Account structure

  • Campaigns are split by meaningful goals (brand, generic, competitor, Shopping), not by habit.
  • Budgets reflect priority, and the best campaigns are not limited by budget while weak ones spend freely.
  • Location targeting is set to people in, not people interested in, your target areas.
  • Ad schedules and devices are reviewed against conversion data.

3. Keywords and search terms

  • The search terms report is reviewed and irrelevant queries are added as negatives.
  • Broad match is only used where smart bidding has enough conversion data to steer it.
  • Brand and generic terms are not mixed in the same campaign.
  • Negative keyword lists are shared across campaigns where sensible.

4. Bidding

  • The bid strategy suits the data volume: manual or maximise clicks for very new accounts, target CPA or target ROAS once conversions are steady.
  • Targets reflect margins rather than arbitrary numbers.
  • Campaigns are not in constant learning because of frequent changes.

5. Ads and assets

  • Each ad group has a responsive search ad with strong, varied headlines.
  • Sitelinks, callouts, structured snippets, images and call assets are in use.
  • Copy includes UK-relevant details: delivery, returns, pricing in £, locations served.
  • Claims follow the CAP Code and Google's policies.

6. Landing pages

  • Each ad points to the most relevant page, not the home page.
  • Pages load quickly on mobile and pass Core Web Vitals.
  • The offer, price and next step are clear above the fold.
  • Forms and checkouts work and are short.

7. Shopping and Performance Max (retailers)

  • Merchant Center has no unresolved disapprovals or policy warnings.
  • Feed titles and attributes are optimised; custom labels group products by margin or best seller status.
  • Brand exclusions are applied so brand searches do not inflate Performance Max results.
  • Asset groups are split by theme with full creative. Our Google Shopping ads guide covers the basics.

8. Reporting and ownership

  • You own the Google Ads account and are an admin, and the billing is in your name.
  • Reports show cost, conversions, value and cost per result tied to your goals.
  • There is a change history showing regular, purposeful work.

Symptom-to-fix table

Most audits find the same handful of problems. Use the symptom you see to find the likely cause before you change anything.

How to prioritise audit findings

An audit can easily produce fifty findings. Fixing them in the wrong order wastes time and can make results worse, so sort them before acting.

  1. Data integrity. Anything that makes the numbers wrong comes first: broken tags, duplicate conversions, missing purchase values, consent mode gaps. Until these are fixed, you cannot judge anything else.
  2. Money leaks. Spend that clearly cannot convert: irrelevant search terms, the wrong locations, disapproved products still sitting in budgets, campaigns pointing at broken pages.
  3. Structural issues. Brand mixed with generic, Performance Max cannibalising brand search, budgets spread across too many campaigns.
  4. Growth opportunities. Better ads and assets, new keyword themes, feed improvements, landing page tests.

Give each finding an owner, an estimated effort and a date. Re-check the account a month later to confirm the changes stuck and had the expected effect.

Questions to ask after an audit

  • Which three changes will make the biggest difference, and why?
  • What evidence supports each finding?
  • What could go wrong when we make the change, and how will we spot it?
  • How will we measure whether the fix worked?

Be wary of audits that recommend rebuilding everything at once. Large simultaneous changes reset learning across the account and make it impossible to know what helped. Steady, documented improvements usually beat a dramatic restructure.

How often to audit

A light monthly check covers search terms, budget pacing, disapprovals and conversion counts. A full audit using the whole checklist makes sense quarterly, before increasing budget, before changing agency, or after a website relaunch. Site changes are a common cause of broken tracking; our guide to a website redesign without losing rankings explains the organic side of the same risk.

DIY audit or agency audit?

You can complete most of this checklist yourself with access and a few hours. An outside audit adds value when the account is large, when tracking is tangled across Tag Manager, GA4 and platform integrations, or when you suspect an incumbent agency is not acting in your interest. Ask any auditor for written findings, prioritised fixes and evidence, not a sales pitch.

If the audit points to site issues (speed, product pages, checkout), our website and ecommerce development work can address them. If it points to broader channel planning, our guide to choosing an agency helps you compare options fairly.

Quick audit checklist

  • Primary conversions are real business outcomes with accurate values.
  • Reported conversions roughly match store or CRM data.
  • Search terms reviewed and negatives up to date.
  • Location set to presence, not interest.
  • Bid strategy suits conversion volume.
  • Ads have full assets and UK-specific copy.
  • Landing pages match the ad and load fast on mobile.
  • Merchant Center clean and feed optimised (retail).
  • Brand exclusions in Performance Max.
  • You own the account and billing.

FAQs

What should a PPC audit checklist include?

A PPC audit checklist should cover conversion tracking, account structure, keywords and search terms, bidding, ads and assets, landing pages, Shopping feeds where relevant, and reporting and account ownership.

How long does a Google Ads audit take?

A small account can be reviewed in a few hours. Large accounts with many campaigns, Shopping feeds and complex tracking take longer, especially if tracking needs untangling.

What is the most common problem found in a PPC audit?

Inaccurate conversion tracking is one of the most frequent issues, for example duplicate counting or non-sale actions set as primary conversions. It distorts bidding and reporting across the whole account.

How often should I audit my Google Ads account?

Do a light check monthly and a full audit quarterly, plus before budget increases, agency changes or website relaunches.

Can I audit my own Google Ads account?

Yes. With admin access to Google Ads, GA4 and Merchant Center you can work through this checklist yourself. Outside help is useful for large or complex accounts.

Should I switch agency after a bad audit?

Not automatically. Share the findings and ask for a fix plan with dates. If basics such as tracking and account ownership are wrong and do not get fixed, it may be time to compare options.
