How to Write Responsive Search Ads That Work for a Small Business

Learn how to write responsive search ads in Google Ads: planning headlines and descriptions, using pinning carefully, ad strength and testing for UK businesses.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Responsive search ads let you supply multiple headlines and descriptions, and Google mixes them to suit each search, so every line must make sense on its own.
  • Plan headlines by job (keyword match, offer, proof, location, call to action) rather than writing variations of the same sentence.
  • Use pinning sparingly, treat ad strength as a guide not a goal, and judge ads on conversions from accurate tracking.

To write responsive search ads well, give Google a varied set of headlines and descriptions that each stand alone, cover different reasons to choose you, and closely match what people search for. Google then tests combinations automatically, so your job is to supply strong, distinct building blocks rather than one perfect advert.

Responsive search ads are the standard text ad format in Google Ads search campaigns. For a UK small business, the quality of the ad copy affects both how often people click and what kind of people click. This guide explains how the format works, a simple way to plan your copy, how to use pinning and ad strength sensibly, and how to test and improve over time. Platform limits change, so check Google's current help pages for exact character counts and asset numbers.

How responsive search ads work

Instead of writing a fixed ad, you provide a list of headlines and a list of descriptions. Google assembles combinations, usually showing up to three headlines and up to two descriptions at a time, and learns which combinations perform best for different searches and devices.

That has some important consequences:

  • Any headline may appear next to any other, so none can rely on another to make sense.
  • Headlines may appear in any order unless you pin them.
  • Repetitive headlines give Google little to test and tend to reduce performance.
  • Not every headline will be shown often. Google favours what it expects to work.

Before you write: get the basics right

Good ad copy cannot fix a poor campaign. Check three things first.

  1. Tight ad groups. Each ad group should focus on a close set of keywords with the same intent, such as emergency boiler repair rather than every heating service at once. That lets your headlines match the search closely.
  2. A relevant landing page. The ad should send people to a page about exactly what they searched for. Our landing page design guide explains what that page needs.
  3. Working conversion tracking. Without it, you cannot tell which ads bring enquiries. See our Google Ads conversion tracking setup guide.

How to write responsive search ads: plan by job

The easiest way to produce varied headlines is to give each one a job. Aim for several headlines in each group below, adapted to your business.

Headline jobPurposeExample for a local electrician
Keyword matchMirror the search so people see you have what they wantElectrician in Leicester
Service detailName specific services within the themeFuse Board Upgrades and Rewires
Benefit or offerGive a reason to choose youFree, No-Obligation Quotes
Proof and trustShow credibility using facts you can back upRegistered and Fully Insured
Location or availabilityReassure about coverage or timingCovering Leicester and Nearby
Call to actionTell people what to do nextBook Your Electrical Check Today

Only use claims you can prove. If you mention registration with a trade scheme, a guarantee or same-day availability, it must be accurate and current. UK advertising rules under the CAP Code apply to online ads, and misleading claims can also breach Google's policies.

Writing descriptions

Descriptions have more room, so use them to expand on the most persuasive points. A practical mix is:

  • One description that summarises what you do and who for.
  • One that focuses on your main differentiator, such as experience in a particular type of job or flexible appointments.
  • One that reduces risk, such as clear quotes, guarantees or what happens after someone gets in touch.
  • One with a direct call to action.

Each description should read naturally if it appears alone or alongside any other.

Using keywords in your ads

Including the main keyword in a few headlines usually helps, because searchers notice their own words and Google may show matching text in bold. But stuffing it into every headline wastes space you could use for benefits and proof.

A common approach is two or three headlines with the core keyword or close variants, then the rest focused on other jobs. Keyword insertion and location insertion can help with relevance, but they need careful defaults and testing, and they can produce awkward wording. Check how they behave in the ad preview before relying on them.

It is also worth keeping irrelevant searches away from your ads in the first place. Our negative keywords guide explains how.

Pinning: when and how much

Pinning fixes a headline or description to a specific position. It is useful when something must always appear, for example a required disclaimer, a brand name or a phrase your compliance process insists on. Businesses in regulated areas should agree ad wording with whoever approves their financial promotions or health claims.

The downside is that pinning reduces the combinations Google can test. If you pin one headline to position one, every ad starts the same way. A middle path is to pin two or three similar headlines to the same position, so Google can still rotate between them.

As a rule, pin only what you need to, and review whether pinned ads perform better or worse than unpinned ones.

Ad strength: useful, but not the goal

Google shows an ad strength rating as you build an ad. It reflects things like the number of headlines, how distinct they are and whether they include popular keywords. It is a useful prompt to add variety, but it measures how well the ad is put together, not whether it brings profitable enquiries.

An ad rated average with strong conversion rates is better than an excellent-rated ad that attracts the wrong people. Use ad strength to catch obvious gaps, then judge performance on conversions and cost per conversion.

Assets that support your ads

Ads can also show assets (previously called extensions) such as sitelinks, callouts, structured snippets, call buttons, location details and images. They make ads larger and give searchers more reasons to click.

  • Sitelinks point to useful pages such as pricing, reviews or a specific service.
  • Callouts highlight short facts like free quotes or local engineers.
  • Call assets suit businesses where phone enquiries are common.
  • Location assets link to your Google Business Profile.

Google decides when to show assets, so add a sensible set and keep them accurate.

Testing and improving responsive search ads

Responsive search ads test combinations automatically, but you still need to review them.

  1. Give ads time. Small budgets gather data slowly. Avoid judging copy after a handful of clicks.
  2. Check asset reporting. Google shows how often each headline and description appears and may label performance. Replace consistently weak lines with new ideas rather than tiny rewordings.
  3. Review combinations. Look at the combinations report to see what people actually see.
  4. Read search terms. If ads attract the wrong searches, adjust keywords and copy together.
  5. Change one area at a time. Swapping everything at once makes it impossible to see what helped.

A regular audit catches problems early. Our PPC audit checklist includes ad copy checks alongside structure and tracking.

Common mistakes

  • Fifteen headlines that say the same thing in slightly different words.
  • Headlines that only make sense in a particular order.
  • Unprovable claims such as best or cheapest in the area.
  • Pinning every position, which turns the ad into a fixed ad.
  • Chasing ad strength instead of conversions.
  • Sending every ad to the homepage.

FAQs

How do I write responsive search ads in Google Ads?

Plan headlines by job, such as keyword match, offer, proof, location and call to action, write descriptions that each stand alone, add them to a focused ad group, then review asset performance and conversions over time.

How many headlines should a responsive search ad have?

Google allows a set maximum and recommends using a good number of distinct headlines. Check Google's current help pages for exact limits, and focus on variety rather than simply filling every slot.

Should I pin headlines?

Only when something must always appear, such as a brand name or required wording. Pinning reduces testing, so pin sparingly or pin several options to one position.

Does ad strength affect my results?

Ad strength is a guide to how well the ad is assembled, not a direct measure of results. Use it to spot gaps, but judge ads on conversions and cost per conversion.

How long should I leave an ad before changing it?

Long enough to gather meaningful data, which depends on budget and search volume. For small accounts that may be several weeks. Avoid frequent changes that reset learning.

Can I use the same ads across all my ad groups?

It is better not to. Ads written for each ad group's specific keywords and landing page are more relevant to searchers and usually perform better.
