Wedding Venue Website Design: Spaces, Capacities and Show-Round Bookings

Website design for UK wedding venues: ceremony and reception spaces, capacities, packages, availability enquiries, show-round booking and honest photography.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Couples shortlist venues on space, capacity, setting and rough cost, so make those easy to find before asking for an enquiry.
  • Whether a venue can host legal ceremonies depends on its approval with the local authority, so describe ceremony options accurately and hedge anything you are unsure of.
  • The main conversion goal is usually a date enquiry or show-round booking, and the form should ask for the details you actually need to reply well.

Choosing a wedding venue is often the first big decision a couple makes, and it shapes almost everything that follows: the date, the guest list, the style of the day and a large part of the budget. Couples typically research many venues online before visiting any, which means a venue's website does a great deal of the early selling. It needs to show the spaces properly, answer practical questions and make it easy to check a date or book a visit.

This article covers the website itself for venues such as country houses, barns, hotels with wedding suites, estates and unusual spaces. For promotion more broadly, including wedding fairs, directories, social media and advertising, see our guide to wedding business marketing in the UK. If your venue is primarily a hotel, our guide to hotel and B&B website design covers the accommodation side.

What couples look for first

When couples land on a venue site, they are usually checking whether it belongs on their shortlist. The questions tend to be:

  • Does it look like the kind of place we want?
  • Can it hold our number of guests, for the ceremony, meal and evening?
  • Can we get married here, or only celebrate here?
  • Roughly what does it cost, and what is included?
  • Is our date available?
  • Is there accommodation, and how far is it from where our guests live?

If the site answers these quickly, couples are more likely to enquire. If they have to dig, many will move on to the next venue on their list.

Structuring a wedding venue website

A sensible structure for many venues is:

  • Home, setting the scene with strong imagery and a clear route to enquire.
  • Spaces, with a page or section for each ceremony room, reception space, outdoor area and evening venue.
  • Weddings or Packages, explaining how weddings work at your venue and what is included.
  • Accommodation, if you have rooms on site or nearby arrangements.
  • Food and drink, covering in-house catering or approved caterers.
  • Real weddings or a gallery, showing the venue in use.
  • FAQs, Find us and Enquire.

Venues that also host parties, corporate events or private dining may want a separate section for those, so wedding visitors are not distracted and event bookers can find what they need.

Presenting each space properly

Every space deserves its own clear description. For each one, include:

  • What it is used for: ceremony, wedding breakfast, drinks reception, evening party or a mix.
  • Capacity in different layouts, such as seated ceremony, banquet dining and standing reception.
  • Features that matter on the day: natural light, dance floor, bar, access, heating, and what happens in bad weather if the space is outdoors.
  • Photos of the space empty and dressed for a wedding.

Capacity figures should be accurate and consistent with what you can safely and legally accommodate, including any fire safety limits. A floor plan or a simple diagram of how spaces connect helps couples picture the flow of the day.

Explaining ceremony options accurately

This is an area where care matters. In England and Wales, civil ceremonies can generally only take place at premises approved by the local authority, and the approval usually covers specific rooms or areas. Rules differ in Scotland and Northern Ireland, and the law on wedding locations has been under review, so arrangements can change. Religious ceremonies and celebrant-led ceremonies have their own rules too.

On your website:

  • Only say you are licensed or approved for ceremonies if that is currently the case, and be clear which spaces the approval covers.
  • If you only host receptions, or celebrant-led ceremonies that are not legally binding, say so plainly so couples understand they may need a separate legal ceremony.
  • Point couples to their local register office or relevant authority for the legal requirements, rather than trying to explain the law in full yourself.

Packages and pricing

Couples want a sense of cost before they enquire. Many venues are reluctant to publish prices because every wedding is different, but some guidance helps filter out mismatched enquiries and builds trust. Options include:

  • Showing a starting price or a range for venue hire, with a note on what affects it, such as season, day of the week and guest numbers.
  • Describing packages clearly, listing what each includes and what is extra.
  • Offering a downloadable brochure with fuller detail.

Whatever you publish, keep it up to date and make sure it matches what couples are told when they enquire. Out-of-date prices cause awkward conversations. Our guide to pricing page design covers how to present pricing more generally.

Brochure downloads

Brochures are common in the wedding industry. If you offer one, decide whether it should be freely downloadable or exchanged for contact details. Gating the brochure can generate leads, but it can also put off couples at the early research stage. Whichever you choose, make sure the brochure and the website say the same things, and that any personal data collected is handled in line with UK data protection law and your privacy notice.

Availability and enquiry forms

The main goal of most venue websites is an enquiry. Make the route obvious from every page, with a clear button to check availability or book a show-round. The enquiry form should ask for what you need to give a useful reply:

  • Names and contact details.
  • Preferred date or dates, and flexibility.
  • Approximate guest numbers for the day and evening.
  • Whether they want a ceremony on site.
  • Anything else they would like to tell you.

Keep it reasonably short; you can gather more detail in conversation. Some venues show a live availability calendar, but this needs careful upkeep, as provisional holds and confirmed bookings change constantly. An honest "enquire to check your date" can be better than an inaccurate calendar. Our guide to website contact forms has more on form design.

Show-round booking

Most couples want to visit before booking. If you hold scheduled open days or offer private show-rounds, explain how to arrange them. A booking tool that lets couples pick a slot can save back-and-forth, provided you keep it updated. List open-day dates on the site and remove them once they have passed.

Photography and real weddings

Imagery is central for wedding venues. Use a mix of the venue on its own, the spaces dressed for weddings, and real weddings in different seasons and styles. Photos of real weddings must be used with the permission of the couple and the photographer, and the photographer should usually be credited. Do not use stock images that imply they were taken at your venue.

Real wedding stories, written with the couple's agreement, show how flexible your venue is. Keep testimonials genuine and attributed in a way the couple is comfortable with.

Suppliers and catering

Couples want to know whether they can choose their own caterers, florists, bands and other suppliers, or must use your recommended or approved list. Be clear about this, including any corkage, supplier fees or restrictions on things like confetti, candles or amplified music. Clarity here prevents disappointment later.

Accommodation and getting there

Guests need to know how to get to the venue and where to stay. Include clear directions, parking information, nearby stations, and any on-site rooms or arrangements with local accommodation. Mention finishing times and any licensing restrictions on music or bars that affect the evening.

Speed, mobile and accessibility

Large galleries can make venue sites slow, especially on phones. Compress images and use lazy loading so pages load quickly. Ensure the site is accessible, with alt text on images and readable contrast. Describe step-free access, accessible toilets and accessible rooms if you have them, since this matters to many guests. See our guides to mobile website design and website accessibility.

Common mistakes on wedding venue websites

  • No capacity figures, or figures that differ between pages.
  • Unclear ceremony options that leave couples unsure whether they can legally marry there.
  • Galleries with no context about which space is which.
  • Enquiry forms that are hard to find or far too long.
  • Out-of-date open-day dates and prices.

FAQs

Should a wedding venue publish prices?

Some guidance usually helps, such as a starting price or range. Keep it current and consistent with what you quote.

How should I describe ceremony licensing?

Only claim approval you currently hold, specify which spaces it covers, and direct couples to their register office for the legal requirements.

Do I need a live availability calendar?

Not necessarily. An inaccurate calendar does more harm than good, so an enquiry route may be better.

Can I use photos from real weddings?

Yes, with permission from the couple and the photographer, and usually with a credit to the photographer.

Should weddings and other events share a website?

They can, but give weddings their own section so couples are not distracted by corporate or party content.
