Website Homepage Design: What Your Homepage Needs to Do

A practical guide to homepage design for UK businesses: the jobs a homepage does, what to put above the fold, section order, and common mistakes.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A homepage is a signpost, not a brochure: it should tell visitors what you do, who it is for and where to go next.
  • The opening screen matters most, so lead with a plain statement of what you offer rather than a slogan.
  • Build the page from your visitors' questions in the order they ask them, then cut anything that does not help.

A good homepage answers three questions within a few seconds: what is this business, is it for me, and what should I do next? It is rarely the page that closes a sale. Its real job is to orient people and send them to the page that will, whether that is a service page, a product range or a contact form.

What a homepage is actually for

Visitors arrive at a homepage in different states of mind. Some typed your name after a recommendation and want reassurance. Some are comparing a few businesses and want to know quickly whether you fit. Some are existing customers looking for a login, an order or a phone number. A homepage that serves all of them is mostly about clear routes, not lots of content.

It also helps to remember that many visitors never see your homepage at all. Search engines and ads often send people straight to inner pages, so the homepage does not need to carry every message on its own.

The opening screen

The part of the page visible before scrolling does the most work. Aim for:

  • A plain headline. Say what you do and for whom. "Bookkeeping for small UK construction firms" beats "Numbers made simple".
  • A supporting line. One sentence on what makes you a sensible choice, written in your customers' words.
  • One main action. A clear button such as "Get a quote" or "Shop the range", with perhaps one quieter secondary option.
  • An image that shows something real. Your product, your work or your setting tends to say more than generic stock photography.

Rotating carousels are best avoided for key messages. Most people only see the first slide, and moving content can be distracting and harder to use for some visitors.

A sensible section order

There is no single correct layout, but a useful way to plan one is to list the questions a new visitor asks and answer them in order.

Visitor questionSection that answers it
What do you do?Headline and short introduction
Do you do the thing I need?Services or product categories, each linking to its own page
Why you rather than someone else?A few specific reasons, backed by evidence where you have it
Can I trust you?Genuine reviews, examples of work, accreditations you actually hold
How does it work?A brief outline of how people buy from or work with you
What now?A repeated call to action and contact details

Service businesses and online shops will weight these differently. A shop's homepage usually leans towards categories, offers and delivery information; a professional services homepage leans towards expertise and reassurance.

Writing for the homepage

Homepage copy should be short and scannable, with each section linking to a deeper page rather than trying to explain everything. Avoid stacking vague claims such as "passionate", "innovative" or "quality-driven", which tell visitors nothing. Specific, checkable statements work better. Our website copywriting guide covers this in more depth.

Homepages and SEO

Your homepage often ranks for your brand name and sometimes for a broad description of what you do. Give it a descriptive title tag and a heading that includes your main service in natural language, and link from it to your most important pages so they are easy for search engines to find. Trying to make one page rank for every service usually works less well than giving each service its own page.

Common homepage mistakes

  • Opening with a mission statement instead of what you sell.
  • Too many competing buttons, so nothing stands out.
  • Large, uncompressed images or videos that slow the page down on mobile.
  • Out-of-date news, offers or blog feeds that make the site look neglected.
  • Hiding contact details, delivery information or prices that people expect to find quickly.
  • Designing on a large monitor and only checking the phone version at the end.

Testing your homepage

A simple test is to show the page to someone unfamiliar with your business for five seconds, then ask what you do and what they would click. If they cannot say, the opening screen needs work. In your analytics, look at where homepage visitors go next and whether they leave quickly. Our small business website checklist covers other basics worth checking.

Homepages for different kinds of business

Service businesses

For trades, consultancies and professional firms, the homepage is largely about reassurance and routing. Visitors want to know the services you offer, the area or type of client you serve, and how to get in touch. Listing services as clear cards, each linking to its own page, usually works better than long paragraphs. If you cover a defined area, say so early so people outside it do not waste their time, and those inside it feel confident they are in the right place.

Online shops

A shop homepage behaves more like a shop window. Returning customers often want to get straight to a category or search, so make both easy. New visitors want a quick feel for the range, the price level and practical details such as delivery and returns. Featured categories, a small selection of popular or new products, and a clear strip of delivery information tend to do more than a single large hero image. Keep promotional banners current; an expired offer is one of the quickest ways to make a shop look unattended.

Businesses with several audiences

If you serve distinct groups, such as trade and retail customers, or patients and referring professionals, the homepage can split people early with clearly labelled routes. Keep the split simple: two or three paths are manageable, but a long list of audience types becomes another menu to decode.

Evidence and trust signals

Visitors arriving at a homepage for the first time are quietly asking whether you are a real, competent business. Useful signals include genuine customer reviews, short case studies or examples of work, recognisable details such as a registered company name and contact information, and memberships or accreditations you genuinely hold. Use only what is true, current and permitted. Invented or exaggerated testimonials are not only risky under consumer protection rules, they also tend to read as hollow. If you are a newer business without much to show yet, a clear explanation of how you work and what customers can expect can do a similar job honestly.

Planning a homepage before design starts

It is tempting to begin with visuals, but homepages built from a blank design canvas often end up as a collection of attractive sections with no clear purpose. A more reliable approach is to start with words and structure:

  • Write down the main types of visitor and what each needs to do next.
  • List the pages you most want people to reach from the homepage.
  • Draft the headline and section headings in plain text, as if the page had no design at all.
  • Only then sketch layouts, starting with the phone version.

If a section cannot be justified in the plain-text version, it probably does not belong on the finished page either.

Performance and accessibility

Homepages are often the heaviest pages on a site because they collect hero images, sliders, embedded videos and animation. Each addition can slow loading on mobile connections. Compress images, avoid loading large videos by default and remove scripts that are no longer needed. Check that text has good contrast against background images, that buttons are large enough to tap and that the page can be used with a keyboard. FAQs

How long should a homepage be?

Long enough to answer a new visitor's main questions and route them onwards. For many small businesses that is a handful of short sections. Length matters less than whether every section earns its place.

Should prices go on the homepage?

If price is a big part of the buying decision, a starting price or a link to a pricing page can help visitors self-qualify. It depends on your business and how you price.

Is a video background a good idea?

It can look striking, but it adds weight to the page and can distract from your message. If you use one, keep it short, muted, compressed and paired with a clear headline.

Should the homepage have a contact form?

A short form or a prominent button linking to one can work well, especially for service businesses. What matters is that the route to contact you is obvious.

How often should I update my homepage?

Review it whenever your offer changes and at least once or twice a year. Remove anything dated, and check it still matches what customers come to you for.
