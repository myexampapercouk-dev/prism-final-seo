404 Page Design: How to Help Lost Visitors Find Their Way

How to design a helpful 404 error page for a business website or online store, plus how to find broken links, set up redirects and avoid soft 404 errors.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good 404 page explains what happened in plain language and gives visitors useful routes onwards.
  • The page should return a genuine 404 status code, not redirect everything to the homepage.
  • Regularly finding and fixing the broken links that cause 404s matters more than the design of the page itself.

Every website eventually sends someone to a page that does not exist. A product is discontinued, a page is renamed, someone mistypes a web address or an old link elsewhere on the internet points somewhere that has gone. When that happens, the visitor lands on a 404 error page.

Many sites show a bare default message that leaves visitors stranded. A well-designed 404 page does the opposite: it acknowledges the problem, keeps the visitor on your site and helps them find what they wanted. This guide covers what a 404 page is, how to design a useful one and how to reduce the number of people who see it in the first place.

What is a 404 page?

When a browser requests a page, the server responds with a status code. A 404 code means "not found": the server is working, but there is nothing at that address. The page shown alongside that code is the 404 page.

Related codes are worth knowing. A 410 means a page has been permanently removed on purpose. A 301 is a permanent redirect, sending visitors and search engines to a new address. Choosing the right response for each situation is part of keeping a site healthy.

Why 404 pages matter

A visitor who hits a dead end with no way forward is likely to leave, and may not come back. That is a lost enquiry or sale, and it can leave an impression of a neglected website. A helpful 404 page will not recover every visitor, but it gives them a reasonable chance to continue.

404s are a normal part of the web, and Google has said that they do not in themselves harm a site's rankings. However, broken links on your own site waste visitors' time and can stop search engines discovering important pages, and losing pages that had valuable links pointing to them can mean losing the benefit of those links.

What a good 404 page includes

A clear, plain explanation

Tell visitors simply that the page they were looking for could not be found. Avoid technical jargon and avoid blaming the visitor. Something like "Sorry, we cannot find that page" is enough, perhaps with a brief note that it may have moved or the address may be mistyped.

Your normal header and navigation

Keep the standard site header, menu and footer so the page feels part of your site and visitors can navigate as usual. A 404 page that strips everything away makes it harder to recover. Good navigation structure pays off here.

A search box

For sites with lots of content or products, a prominent search box is often the most useful element. The visitor already knew what they wanted; search lets them look for it directly. If your store has good site search, put it front and centre.

Helpful links

Offer a short selection of likely destinations, such as:

  • your homepage,
  • main service or product categories,
  • popular or best-selling items,
  • recent blog posts, and
  • your contact page.

Keep the list short and relevant. A wall of links is almost as unhelpful as none.

A way to get help

Include an easy way to contact you, particularly for service businesses where a lost visitor may be a potential client. Some sites also invite visitors to report the broken link, which can help you find problems.

Tone and personality

A 404 page is a low-stakes place to show a little personality. A touch of humour or an illustration in keeping with your brand can soften the frustration. Keep it brief, though; the visitor wants to get back on track, not read a joke. And make sure the tone suits your business. A playful message may suit a gift shop but feel out of place on a site for a law firm or healthcare provider.

404 pages for online stores

Ecommerce sites see 404s often because products come and go. A few extra considerations apply:

  • Show product categories and best-sellers so visitors can continue shopping.
  • Feature search prominently, since many visitors arrive looking for a specific product.
  • Handle discontinued products thoughtfully. Rather than deleting a popular product page outright, you may choose to keep it live with a clear message and links to alternatives, or redirect it to the closest equivalent.
  • Keep the basket accessible, so shoppers mid-session do not feel they have lost their progress.

Technical essentials

Return a real 404 status

The page must return a genuine 404 (or 410) status code. A common mistake is showing a "not found" message while the server reports success with a 200 code. Search engines call this a soft 404, and it can lead to empty pages being treated as real content. Google Search Console reports soft 404s it detects.

Do not redirect every missing page to the homepage

Sending all missing pages to the homepage might seem tidy, but it confuses visitors, who land somewhere unexpected without explanation, and search engines often treat such redirects as soft 404s anyway. Redirect a missing page only when there is a genuinely relevant replacement.

Keep it fast and simple

The 404 page should load quickly and work properly on mobile. Avoid heavy scripts or large images.

Platform settings

Shopify, WordPress and WooCommerce all provide a 404 template that themes can customise. Check what your theme provides before building something from scratch; often it is a case of improving the content and links rather than starting over.

Finding and fixing the causes of 404s

A great 404 page is a safety net, not a solution. It is better to reduce how often visitors see it. Practical steps include:

  • Check Google Search Console regularly for pages reported as not found, focusing on those that receive traffic or have links pointing to them.
  • Review your analytics for visits to the 404 page and the addresses people were trying to reach.
  • Crawl your own site with a site audit tool to find broken internal links, then update them to point to the correct pages.
  • Set up redirects when you change URLs. Whenever you rename, move or merge a page, add a 301 redirect from the old address to the most relevant new one.

Redirects are especially important during a site rebuild or platform migration, when large numbers of URLs may change. Our guide to a website redesign without losing rankings covers redirect mapping in detail, and ongoing website maintenance helps catch new broken links before they pile up.

Should every 404 be fixed?

No. Some 404s are perfectly correct. If someone mistypes an address, or a page has been removed with no sensible replacement, a 404 is the right response. Focus your effort on:

  • broken links within your own site,
  • missing pages that still receive meaningful traffic, and
  • missing pages that have links from other websites.

For these, either restore the content, update the link or redirect to a relevant alternative.

Measuring your 404 page

Track how many visitors land on the 404 page and what they do next. If most leave immediately, try improving the links or search placement. If many go on to search, look at what they search for; it may reveal content customers expect but cannot find.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A bare default error page with no navigation.
  • Returning a 200 status code for missing pages.
  • Redirecting all missing pages to the homepage.
  • Jokes that clash with your brand or go on too long.
  • Forgetting redirects when URLs change.
  • Never checking which 404s visitors actually hit.

FAQs

Do 404 errors hurt SEO?

A normal number of 404s for pages that genuinely no longer exist is not usually a problem. Issues arise with broken internal links and lost pages that had valuable links or traffic.

What is a soft 404?

A soft 404 is a page that tells visitors nothing was found but returns a success status code, or a page search engines judge to be empty. Google Search Console reports these.

Should I redirect 404 pages to my homepage?

Generally no. Redirect only to a genuinely relevant replacement page; otherwise let the page return a 404 with a helpful message.

What should a 404 page say?

Explain briefly that the page could not be found, then offer search, helpful links and a way to contact you, in a tone that suits your brand.

Can I customise the 404 page on Shopify or WordPress?

Yes. Both include a 404 template that can be customised through your theme or with development work.
