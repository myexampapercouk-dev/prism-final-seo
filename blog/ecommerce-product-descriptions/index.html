How to Write Ecommerce Product Descriptions That Help Shoppers Buy

A UK guide to writing ecommerce product descriptions: structure, benefits versus features, tone, specifications, scannable layouts and avoiding duplicate copy.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good product description answers the questions a shopper would ask if they could hold the product or speak to someone in a shop.
  • Lead with what the product does for the customer, then back it up with clear, accurate specifications.
  • Original descriptions written for your own customers are more useful, and more distinctive, than copy pasted from a supplier.

On an online shop, the product description does the job a knowledgeable shop assistant would do in person. The shopper cannot pick the item up, try it on or ask a question across the counter, so the words on the page have to fill the gap. Yet product descriptions are often an afterthought: a line copied from a supplier feed, a list of measurements, or a paragraph of vague praise that could apply to anything.

This guide explains how to plan, structure and write ecommerce product descriptions that help shoppers decide. It focuses on the writing itself. For the search side, such as title tags, structured data and URL structure, see our product page SEO guide.

What a product description is for

A product description has a few overlapping jobs. It should help the right shopper recognise that this is the product they need, reassure them about the details that matter, and remove doubts that might stop them buying. It should also help the wrong shopper realise the product is not for them, which can reduce returns and disappointment.

It is not there to impress with clever wording. Shoppers tend to scan rather than read, looking for specific facts such as size, material, compatibility or care instructions. Description copy that hides those facts inside long paragraphs makes the shopper work harder than they need to.

Start with the customer, not the product

Before writing, think about who buys this product and why. A walking boot might be bought by a weekend rambler, a serious hill walker or someone buying a gift. Each cares about different things. If you sell to one main group, write for them. If several groups buy the same item, make sure the description covers the questions each is likely to have.

Useful sources of customer questions include:

  • Questions customers send by email, phone or live chat before buying.
  • Reasons given for returns, which often point to information that was missing or unclear.
  • Reviews on your own site, which show the words customers use and what they valued.
  • Searches made on your own site, which reveal the terms shoppers use. Our ecommerce site search guide explains how to use this data.

Collecting these questions for each product type gives you a checklist of what every description in that category should answer.

Features and benefits

A feature is a fact about the product. A benefit is what that fact means for the customer. "Merino wool lining" is a feature. "Keeps feet warm without overheating on long walks" is the benefit. Shoppers usually need both: the benefit explains why they should care, and the feature gives them confidence the claim is grounded in something real.

A simple approach is to take each important feature and ask "so what?" until you reach something the customer cares about. Keep benefits honest and specific. Avoid claiming outcomes you cannot stand behind, and be careful with words that have legal or regulatory meaning, such as "waterproof", "organic", "hypoallergenic" or health-related claims. If you are unsure whether a claim is allowed for your product category, it is worth checking the relevant guidance or taking advice before publishing.

A practical structure for product descriptions

There is no single correct template, but many shops find a consistent structure helps shoppers and makes writing quicker. A common pattern is:

  1. An opening line or short paragraph that says what the product is and who it is for, with the main benefit.
  2. Key benefits as a short list of three to five points, each tying a feature to an outcome.
  3. Detail and context for shoppers who want more, such as how it is made, how it fits or how it compares with similar items you sell.
  4. Specifications in a clear list or table: dimensions, weight, materials, compatibility, capacity and so on.
  5. Care, use or setup information where relevant.
  6. Practical information such as what is in the box, or a link to your delivery and returns information.

The order can change depending on the product. For technical items, shoppers may want specifications near the top. For fashion or gifts, the look and feel may matter more. What matters is that the structure is predictable across your shop, so shoppers learn where to find things.

Make descriptions easy to scan

Most shoppers skim product pages, particularly on mobile. Some simple formatting habits help:

  • Keep paragraphs short, ideally two or three sentences.
  • Use bullet points for benefits and specifications.
  • Use subheadings or tabs on longer descriptions, so shoppers can jump to what they need.
  • Put the most important information first in each section.
  • Bold sparingly, for genuinely key facts rather than whole sentences.

Be careful with tabs and accordions. They keep the page tidy, but information hidden inside them is easy to miss. Essential details such as size or compatibility are often better kept visible. Our mobile website design guide covers layout considerations for smaller screens.

Write in your brand's voice

Product descriptions are one of the places where your brand voice shows up most often, because there are so many of them. A playful brand can be playful here; a technical brand should be precise. The important thing is consistency, so that the shop feels like one business rather than a patchwork of supplier copy. If you have not defined your voice yet, our tone of voice guide explains how.

Whatever the voice, clarity comes first. Avoid filler phrases such as "high quality", "premium" or "the perfect choice" unless you explain what makes them true. Concrete details are more persuasive than adjectives: "double-stitched seams and a reinforced base" tells the shopper more than "built to last".

Use sensory and practical detail

Because shoppers cannot touch the product, words can help them imagine it. Describe texture, weight, sound, scent or how something feels to use where it is relevant. For clothing, explain fit and how the fabric behaves. For furniture, help the shopper picture the size in a real room. For food and drink, describe taste and serving suggestions.

Words work best alongside good imagery. Descriptions can point out details that photographs might not make obvious, such as the weight of a fabric or the finish on a surface. Our ecommerce product photography guide covers the visual side.

Specifications: accuracy matters most

Specifications are where errors cause the most trouble. A wrong measurement or missing compatibility note can lead to returns, complaints and lost trust. Some good habits:

  • Use consistent units across the shop, and consider showing both metric and imperial where your customers expect it.
  • State exactly what is and is not included.
  • Be clear about variations, such as how sizes differ between colours or styles.
  • Store specifications as structured product data where your platform allows, so they can be used for filters and comparisons as well as display.
  • Review specifications whenever a supplier changes a product.

Structured attributes also make category filters work properly, which our ecommerce category page design guide discusses.

Avoid duplicate and supplier copy

Many retailers receive product descriptions from manufacturers or wholesalers. Using them unchanged is quick, but the same text may appear on many other shops. That makes it harder for your product pages to stand out, both to shoppers and in search results, and the copy is rarely written with your customers in mind.

Rewriting every description at once may not be realistic for a large catalogue. A practical approach is to prioritise: start with your best-selling products, products with high return rates and products in your most important categories. Supplier information is still useful as a source of facts; the aim is to present those facts in your own words, structured for your customers.

Duplication within your own shop matters too. Products that differ only by colour or size often share near-identical descriptions. Where variants are genuinely the same product, presenting them as options on one page is often simpler than creating many near-duplicate pages.

Using AI tools for product descriptions

AI writing tools can help draft descriptions at scale, particularly for large catalogues. They can be useful for producing a first draft from structured product data, or for suggesting variations. However, they can also introduce errors, invent features or produce generic copy. Any AI-assisted description should be checked by someone who knows the product, especially specifications and claims. Treat the tool as a drafting aid rather than a replacement for product knowledge.

Descriptions and search

Well-written descriptions tend to support search visibility naturally, because they use the words customers use and answer the questions they ask. Include the terms shoppers actually search for where they fit naturally, but avoid stuffing keywords into the copy. Writing for people first usually produces better results than writing for search engines first. For keyword planning, see our keyword research guide.

Common mistakes

  • Copying supplier descriptions without adapting them.
  • Listing features without explaining why they matter.
  • Using vague praise instead of concrete detail.
  • Hiding key facts such as size or compatibility.
  • Writing long blocks of text that are hard to scan on mobile.
  • Making claims that are not accurate or not permitted.
  • Letting descriptions go out of date when products change.

A simple process for improving descriptions

If your shop has many products, improving descriptions can feel overwhelming. Breaking it down helps:

  1. Choose a priority group of products.
  2. Gather customer questions, return reasons and review language for that group.
  3. Agree a structure and checklist for the category.
  4. Write or rewrite descriptions, checking facts with whoever knows the products best.
  5. Review performance indicators you already track, such as conversion and returns, over time.
  6. Move on to the next group.

This keeps the work manageable and lets you learn what works for your customers before applying it across the shop.

FAQs

How long should a product description be?

Long enough to answer the questions a shopper is likely to have, and no longer. Simple products may need only a short paragraph and specifications; complex or expensive products usually need more.

Can I use the manufacturer's description?

You can use it as a source of facts, but rewriting it for your customers usually produces more useful and distinctive copy. Check any supplier terms that apply to their content.

Should every variant have its own description?

Not necessarily. If variants differ only by size or colour, one description with clear options is often simpler for shoppers.

Do product descriptions affect returns?

They can. Clear, accurate information about size, fit, materials and compatibility helps shoppers choose correctly, which may reduce avoidable returns.

Is it worth rewriting old descriptions?

Often, yes, particularly for best-selling products or items with frequent questions or returns. Start with a priority group rather than the whole catalogue.
