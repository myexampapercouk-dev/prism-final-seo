How to Brief a Shopify Developer So You Get What You Need

How to brief a Shopify developer: what to include, from goals and store data to apps, design files and acceptance criteria, so quotes and builds match.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good Shopify brief explains the business goal, the current store set-up and what done looks like, not just a list of features.
  • Share access, data and design files in an organised way, and say which apps, integrations and markets the store must support.
  • Written acceptance criteria and a clear change process help both sides agree when work is finished.

A Shopify developer can only build what they understand. A vague request such as "make the product page better" leaves the developer to guess, and guesses lead to quotes that are hard to compare, work that misses the point and arguments about what was agreed. A clear brief fixes most of that before any code is written.

This guide explains how to brief a Shopify developer, whether you are planning a new store, a theme change or a single feature. It works for freelancers and agencies alike, and it covers the information that helps anyone scope Shopify work. If you are still choosing who to work with, our guide to Shopify agencies in the UK covers that decision.

Start with the problem, not the feature

The most useful sentence in any brief describes what is wrong or what you want to change in business terms. Compare these two requests:

  • "Add a size guide pop-up to product pages."
  • "Customers keep emailing to ask which size to order, and returns for wrong sizes are eating our margin. We want size information visible before they add to basket."

The first tells the developer what to build. The second tells them why, which lets them suggest a better solution, spot related issues and judge whether a pop-up is the right answer at all. Always include the why. You can still say what you think the solution is; just label it as a suggestion.

What to include in a Shopify developer brief

Use the headings below as a template. Not every project needs every section, but leaving one out should be a deliberate choice.

1. About the business

A short paragraph on what you sell, who buys it and where. Mention if you sell to trade customers as well as consumers, if you ship outside the UK, or if you have seasonal peaks the developer should plan around. A developer who knows your busiest weeks can avoid scheduling risky changes during them.

2. Current set-up

List the facts the developer will otherwise have to dig out:

  • Your Shopify plan, and whether you use Shopify Markets for other countries or currencies.
  • The theme you use and whether it has been heavily customised before.
  • The apps installed and which ones matter to the business.
  • Integrations with other systems, such as stock, accounting, fulfilment, email marketing or point of sale.
  • Roughly how many products, variants and collections you have.
  • Anything known to be fragile or previously broken.

If you are moving to Shopify from another platform, say which one and what data has to come across. Our WooCommerce to Shopify migration guide and Etsy to Shopify guide list the data worth planning for.

3. Goals and how you will judge success

State what should be different after the work. Where you can, tie it to something you can check in Shopify reports or Google Analytics, such as fewer abandoned checkouts, more sign-ups or fewer support emails about a specific issue. Avoid promising yourself a number; just name the measure, so everyone knows what to look at after launch. Our GA4 setup guide explains how to make sure that data is being collected.

4. Scope: what is in and what is out

Write the work as a list of clear items, then add a short list of things that are explicitly out of scope. The out-of-scope list is often as useful as the in-scope one, because it stops assumptions on both sides. For example: "Redesign the collection page filters. Out of scope: changing the navigation menu, product photography, copywriting."

5. Pages, templates and user journeys

Shopify themes are built from templates: home, collection, product, cart, pages, blog and so on. Say which templates are affected and whether you need new ones, such as a separate product template for bundles. Describe the journey you want a customer to take, step by step, including what happens on mobile. If the change touches checkout, say so early, as what can be customised in checkout depends on your Shopify plan.

6. Design files and brand assets

If you have designs, share them with notes on desktop and mobile behaviour, hover states and what happens with long product names or missing images. If you do not have designs, say whether you expect the developer to design, to follow the existing theme style, or to work from reference screenshots of your own site. Include your logo files, brand colours and fonts, and confirm you have the licence to use any fonts or images supplied.

7. Content and data

Be clear about who provides product descriptions, images, metafield values and page copy, and when. Developers often build a feature that relies on product data, such as filters or size charts stored in metafields. If that data does not exist yet, someone has to create it, and the brief should say who.

8. Apps and integrations

For each app or integration involved, note whether it is staying, being replaced or being removed, and whether you are open to custom code instead. Apps can add monthly costs and can affect page speed, as explained in our Shopify speed optimisation guide. A developer can only weigh those trade-offs if they know which apps you are attached to.

9. Technical requirements

Include anything that must be true when the work is done. Typical items include:

  • Works on current versions of major browsers and on mobile.
  • Meets reasonable accessibility standards, such as usable keyboard navigation and sufficient colour contrast.
  • Does not noticeably slow down key pages.
  • Keeps existing URLs working, or adds redirects where they change.
  • Theme settings are editable by your team without code where practical.

10. Budget, timing and constraints

Giving a budget range helps a developer suggest an approach that fits it, rather than quoting the most complete version and leaving you to cut it back. If there is a fixed date, such as a product launch, say so and explain whether it is truly fixed. For Prism Works Studio pricing, see our pricing page.

Access: what to share and how

Shopify lets you add collaborators and staff accounts with limited permissions, so there is no need to share your own login. Give the developer only the access the job needs, and remove it when the work is finished. If they need access to other tools, such as your domain registrar or an email platform, use each service's own user or permission features where possible rather than sharing passwords.

Ask the developer to work on a duplicate, unpublished copy of your theme, so changes can be previewed before they go live. Keeping the original theme as a fallback means you can switch back quickly if something goes wrong after publishing.

Write acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria are short statements that must be true for an item to count as done. They turn a fuzzy feature into something both sides can check. For the size guide example, they might read:

  • On every product with a size chart metafield, a "Size guide" link appears below the size selector.
  • Clicking it opens the chart without leaving the page, on desktop and mobile.
  • Products without a size chart show no link.
  • The chart can be edited in the Shopify admin without code.

Writing these takes a few minutes and saves a lot of back and forth later. If you are not sure how to phrase them, ask the developer to draft them from your brief and then check they match what you meant.

Agree how changes and questions are handled

Requirements change once people see work in progress. That is normal. What causes friction is not agreeing how changes are handled. In your brief, ask how the developer handles requests that fall outside the agreed scope, how they will flag them and how they are priced. Name one person on your side who can make decisions, so the developer is not caught between conflicting instructions.

Gaps worth checking in a Shopify brief

These areas are easy to leave unclear, and they are worth checking before you send yours:

  • Mobile behaviour. Designs are often reviewed on a desktop screen while plenty of shoppers browse on a phone.
  • Edge cases. Sold-out variants, products with one image, very long titles, discounts and gift cards.
  • Who owns content. Features that depend on data nobody has created yet.
  • Testing. Who tests, on which devices, and who signs off.
  • Handover. Whether you need documentation or a walkthrough so your team can manage the result. Our guide to website ownership and handover covers what to ask for.

Freelancer or agency: does the brief change?

The content of the brief stays the same. What changes is how much of it the other side will help you write. Some developers prefer a detailed specification they can quote against; others prefer a short brief followed by a discovery call. Neither is wrong, but be clear which you are offering. FAQs

How long should a Shopify brief be?

Long enough to answer the questions in the template above. For a small change that might be a page; for a full store build it will be longer. Clarity matters more than length.

Should I give my Shopify password to a developer?

It is safer not to. Shopify offers collaborator and staff accounts with limited permissions, which you can remove when the work is done.

Do I need designs before briefing a developer?

No, but say what you expect instead: the developer designs it, follows your existing theme, or works from reference screenshots of your own store.

Should I include a budget in the brief?

Including a range usually helps, because it lets the developer propose an approach that fits rather than quoting a version you then have to cut down.

What are acceptance criteria?

Short, checkable statements that must be true for a piece of work to count as finished. They help both sides agree when an item is done.
