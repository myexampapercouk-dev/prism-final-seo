Multilingual Website Design: A Buyer's Guide for European Businesses

Multilingual website design explained: URL structure, translation workflow, language switchers, SEO and upkeep, so your site works in every market you serve.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A multilingual website is a set of properly structured language versions, each with its own URLs, metadata and content, not a translation widget bolted onto one site.
  • The biggest decisions come before design: which languages and markets, which URL structure, who translates and who keeps every version up to date.
  • Budget for ongoing upkeep as well as the build, because every new page, offer or policy change has to be published in every language you support.

Multilingual website design means building a site that serves each language as a complete, findable version, with its own URLs, navigation, metadata and content, rather than translating text on the fly. For a business selling across Europe, that is the difference between a site that ranks and converts in each market and one that only looks translated.

We are a UK-based studio and we work remotely with businesses across Europe, including Ireland, the Netherlands, Germany, France, Spain and the Nordics. This guide covers the decisions a buyer should make before commissioning a multilingual build, what good looks like, and how to avoid the problems we most often see when a second or third language is added late.

Multilingual website design: what it actually involves

A multilingual site is usually several parallel versions of the same website. Each version needs content written or adapted for its readers, a clear way for visitors to switch language, and technical signals that tell search engines which version suits which audience. The design work is not only visual. It covers structure, templates, content workflow and how editors will maintain everything after launch.

The main components are:

  • Language and market plan. Which languages you need, and whether any language serves several countries, such as German for Germany, Austria and parts of Switzerland.
  • URL structure. Subfolders, subdomains or separate country domains.
  • Templates that flex. German and Finnish words can be long, French text often runs longer than English, and buttons, menus and headings must cope.
  • Translation and localisation workflow. Who writes each version, who reviews it, and how changes flow through.
  • Search signals. Hreflang annotations, translated titles and descriptions, and a sitemap that lists every version.
  • Legal and trust content per market. Privacy notices, cookie information, terms, delivery and returns, reviewed for each country.

Choosing a URL structure

The structure you choose affects SEO, cost and how easy the site is to manage. There is no single right answer, but most small and mid-sized businesses start with subfolders because everything stays on one domain and one platform.

Subfoldersyoursite.com/de/One domain builds authority; simplest to run on one platformWeaker local signal than a country domain

Language versus country

One of the first questions is whether you are targeting languages or countries. A Dutch-language page might serve the Netherlands and Flanders. An English version might serve Ireland, the Nordics and international buyers who browse in English. A French version might need small differences for France, Belgium and Switzerland in delivery terms, prices or legal wording.

Start with the smallest set that covers your real customers. It is far easier to add a language later than to maintain five half-finished versions. Where pricing, delivery or regulation differ by country, you may need country-specific versions even when the language is the same.

Translation is not localisation

Machine translation has improved, and it can be a useful first draft. On its own, though, it often misses tone, idiom, product terminology and the phrases people actually type into search. A localised page uses the words your customers search for in that market, local formats for dates, numbers and addresses, and examples that make sense to the reader.

A sensible workflow looks like this:

  1. Finalise the source content first, so you are not translating drafts.
  2. Research keywords in each language rather than translating English keywords directly.
  3. Use a professional translator or fluent reviewer for key pages, especially sales, legal and checkout content.
  4. Keep a glossary of product names and brand terms so every version stays consistent.
  5. Agree how updates are triggered, so a change to an English page creates a task for every other language.

Good source copy makes translation cheaper and better. If your English content needs work first, our guide to website copywriting is a practical place to start.

Design details that matter in several languages

Most multilingual problems are small layout issues multiplied across every page. A few to plan for:

  • Text expansion. Leave room in buttons, navigation and cards. Avoid fixed-height boxes that clip longer translations.
  • Language switcher. Make it visible in the header and footer, label languages in their own names (Deutsch, Français, Nederlands), and avoid using flags for languages.
  • No forced redirects. Suggest a language based on browser settings if you like, but let people choose and remember their choice. Forced redirects frustrate travellers and can stop search engines seeing every version.
  • Fonts. Check your typefaces support accented characters used in Nordic, German, French and Spanish text. Our colour and typography guide covers choosing type that works.
  • Images with text. Avoid text baked into images, because it cannot be translated easily or read by screen readers.
  • Navigation. Keep the same structure across languages where you can, so editors and analytics stay manageable. See our guide to website navigation structure.

Hreflang and other search signals

Hreflang tells search engines that pages are equivalents in different languages or regions, so they can show the right version to the right searcher. Each language version should reference itself and every alternative, and the references must be reciprocal. Mistakes here are common, particularly when pages are added in one language but not others.

Each version also needs its own translated title tag and meta description, translated image alt text, and a correct language declaration on the page. A sitemap that lists all versions helps search engines find them. If you are relaunching an existing site in several languages, plan redirects carefully so you do not lose existing rankings; our guide to redirects when relaunching explains the process.

Legal, privacy and accessibility in each market

Across the EU, data protection is governed by the GDPR, and cookie consent is shaped by ePrivacy rules as applied in each country, with national regulators issuing their own guidance. The European Accessibility Act also brings accessibility requirements for many consumer-facing digital services, including a lot of online shops. These rules are detailed and can change, so check current official guidance in each country you sell to and take legal advice where needed.

In practice, your privacy notice, cookie banner and terms should be available in each language you trade in, and your consent tool should behave correctly in every version. Our guides to cookie banner design and website accessibility cover the design side of both.

Choosing a platform

WordPress, Shopify and several other platforms support multiple languages, but they do it differently. WordPress usually relies on a multilingual plugin or a multisite network. Shopify supports several languages and markets within one store, with options that can depend on your plan and apps. The right choice depends on how much content you publish, whether you sell online, and who will edit each language.

Ask any supplier to show you how an editor would add a new page in every language, update a price, and check which pages are missing translations. If that process is awkward, it will be neglected after launch.

What to prepare before you speak to an agency

  • The countries and languages you sell in today, and those you plan to add.
  • Who will translate and review content, and who signs it off.
  • Your current platform, domain and any rankings you want to protect.
  • Any country-specific pricing, delivery or legal differences.
  • Examples of competitor or peer sites in your target markets that you think work well.

FAQs

How much does multilingual website design cost?

Our small websites start at £1,000 to £2,000 and ecommerce sites at £1,500 to £3,500. Extra languages add set-up, templates and testing, so we quote multilingual projects after discovery. Translation is usually costed separately. Prices are in pounds, and other currencies are quoted on request.

Should I use subfolders or country domains?

Many businesses start with subfolders on one domain because it keeps authority in one place and is simpler to run. Country domains can send a stronger local signal but cost more to maintain. The right choice depends on your markets, current rankings and resources.

Can I use machine translation for my website?

It can be a useful first draft, but key pages should be reviewed by a fluent speaker. Sales, legal and checkout content in particular needs accuracy, and keywords should be researched in each language rather than translated directly.

Do I need hreflang tags?

If you have equivalent pages in different languages or for different countries, hreflang helps search engines show the right version. The tags must be correct and reciprocal on every version, so they are worth checking after launch and whenever pages are added.

Can you work with businesses outside the UK?

Yes. We are a UK-based studio and work remotely with clients across Europe, using video calls, shared documents and project boards. We write in UK English and work with your translators or a translation partner of your choice for other languages.

How do I keep every language up to date?

Agree a workflow before launch: every change to the main language creates a task for each other version, with a named person responsible. Choose a platform that shows missing translations clearly, and review all versions at least every quarter.
