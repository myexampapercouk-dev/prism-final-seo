Shopify Customer Segments: Grouping Customers for Better Marketing

How Shopify customer segments work, which groups are worth building first, and how to use them for email, discounts and reporting without overcomplicating it.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Customer segments group shoppers by what they have bought, how they behave and what they have agreed to receive.
  • A handful of clear, purposeful segments is more useful than dozens nobody looks at.
  • Only market to customers who have given the right consent, and check current UK guidance on direct marketing rules.

Not every customer is the same. Someone who has bought from you five times over two years has different needs from someone who signed up last week and has not ordered yet. Shopify customer segments let you group customers based on their data so you can send more relevant messages, offer more targeted discounts and understand your customer base more clearly.

This guide covers what segments are, which ones are usually worth creating first, how to use them in practice and the privacy points to keep in mind. Shopify updates its features regularly, so check the current admin for exact filter names and options.

What is a customer segment?

A customer segment is a saved group of customers who match a set of conditions. The conditions might relate to orders, such as the number placed or the amount spent, to dates, such as when they last bought, or to other details like location, tags and email subscription status.

Segments in Shopify are dynamic. As customers place orders or their details change, they move in and out of segments automatically. That means a segment such as "customers who have not ordered in a while" stays current without you having to update it by hand.

Shopify includes some ready-made segment templates, and you can build your own using filters. Segments can then be used in Shopify's own email and marketing tools, for customer-specific discounts, and for reviewing and exporting customer lists.

Why segments matter

Sending the same message to everyone is simple, but it rarely suits everyone. Segmentation lets you match messages to where a customer is in their relationship with your store. A welcome message for new subscribers, a thank-you for repeat buyers and a gentle reminder for lapsed customers all make more sense than one generic newsletter.

Segments also help with understanding. Seeing how many customers have bought only once, or how many come from a particular region, can inform decisions about products, shipping and marketing priorities.

Segments worth building first

Start with a small set that maps to clear actions. Each one below has an obvious use.

  • Subscribers who have not purchased. People who joined your list but have not ordered may respond to helpful information about your products, delivery and returns.
  • First-time customers. A follow-up after a first order can encourage a second purchase, for example by suggesting complementary products.
  • Repeat customers. Customers who have bought several times are worth recognising, whether through early access to new products or simply a thank-you.
  • Lapsed customers. Customers who bought before but not recently can be reminded of what is new.
  • Customers by location. Useful if delivery options, events or offers differ by region.
  • Customers who bought a particular product or range. Helpful for refills, accessories, care guides or related launches.

Decide what "repeat" and "lapsed" mean for your store. A business selling consumables that run out regularly will set different time windows from one selling furniture. Base the thresholds on your own order patterns rather than general rules of thumb.

Building a segment

The process is broadly the same for any segment.

  1. Define the purpose. Write one sentence describing who should be in the segment and what you will do with it.
  2. Choose the conditions. Combine filters such as order count, last order date, amount spent, location or subscription status.
  3. Check the result. Look at the number of customers and open a few records to confirm they match what you expect.
  4. Name it clearly. A name like "Bought twice or more, last order within six months" is clearer than "VIP".
  5. Review it later. Revisit segments periodically to make sure they still reflect your intentions.

Keep conditions as simple as possible. Very narrow segments can end up with only a handful of customers, which may not justify a separate campaign.

Using segments for email marketing

Segments are most often used to decide who receives which email. Shopify's own email tools can send to segments, and automations can be triggered by customer behaviour. Typical uses include welcome sequences, post-purchase follow-ups and win-back messages.

Relevance is the goal. A repeat customer does not need a basic introduction to your brand, and a new subscriber may not yet care about loyalty perks. Matching content to the segment respects the customer's time and can make your emails more welcome. Our guide to ecommerce email marketing covers planning campaigns more broadly.

Segments also support recovery flows. Customers who started checkout but did not finish can receive a reminder, which we cover in our article on abandoned cart recovery.

Using segments for discounts

Shopify lets you limit some discounts to specific customer segments. That can be useful for rewarding loyal customers or encouraging lapsed customers to return without offering the same discount to everyone.

Be thoughtful about discounting. Regularly discounting to the same group can train them to wait for offers. A clear plan for when and why you discount helps protect margins. See our discount code strategy guide for more on this.

Segments, tags and other customer data

Customer tags can be used as segment conditions, which is handy for groups that cannot be defined by order data alone, such as trade customers or event attendees. Keep tagging consistent: decide on a short list of tags and how they are written, and avoid creating near-duplicates.

The quality of your segments depends on the quality of your data. Duplicate customer records, inconsistent tags or missing subscription statuses all reduce accuracy. A periodic tidy-up pays off. Similar principles apply to product data, which we cover in our guide to Shopify product tags.

Privacy and consent

Segmentation uses personal data, so it must be handled responsibly. In the UK, sending marketing emails is generally subject to rules about consent and the right to opt out, alongside wider data protection law. The detail can depend on your circumstances, so check current guidance from the ICO or take advice if you are unsure.

Some practical habits help:

  • Only send marketing emails to customers whose subscription status shows they can receive them.
  • Make unsubscribing easy and honour it promptly.
  • Avoid segmenting on sensitive information you do not need.
  • Explain in your privacy policy how you use customer data for marketing.
  • Limit who on your team can export customer lists, and store exports securely.

Our guide to Shopify staff accounts covers controlling who can access customer information.

Common segmentation mistakes

  • Building segments with no plan to use them. A segment that never receives a message or informs a decision is just clutter in the admin.
  • Overlapping groups without rules. A customer can belong to several segments at once. If two campaigns target overlapping groups in the same week, the same person may receive both. Decide which message takes priority.
  • Ignoring segment size. Check how many customers a segment holds before planning a campaign around it.
  • Forgetting to update thresholds. As your range and buying patterns change, the definitions of repeat or lapsed customers may need to change too.
  • Relying on incomplete data. Guest checkouts, imported lists and duplicate records can all leave gaps, so treat segment counts as a guide rather than an exact figure.

Measuring whether segments help

Segments are only worthwhile if they lead to better decisions. Track simple measures for each campaign or automation, such as orders and revenue attributed to it, unsubscribes and how many customers move from one segment to another over time. Compare results with your previous, unsegmented approach where you can.

Be cautious about drawing conclusions from small numbers. A segment with a few dozen customers can produce results that swing widely from one send to the next. Look at trends over several campaigns before changing direction.

FAQs

Do Shopify customer segments update automatically?

Yes. Segments are based on conditions, so customers move in and out as their data changes.

How many segments should a store have?

There is no set number. Start with a few segments that each support a clear action, and add more only when you have a specific use for them.

Can I give a discount to one segment only?

Shopify allows some discounts to be limited to specific customer segments. Check the current discount settings in your admin for the options available.

Can I email everyone in a segment?

Only customers who have agreed to receive marketing should get marketing emails. Check subscription status and current UK guidance on direct marketing.

What is the difference between a segment and a customer tag?

A tag is a label attached to a customer record. A segment is a group defined by conditions, which can include tags along with order and other data.
