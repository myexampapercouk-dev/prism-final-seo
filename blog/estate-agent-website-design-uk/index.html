Estate Agent Website Design: Listings, Valuations and Local Trust

Estate agent website design for UK agencies: what to include, property feeds, listing pages, valuation requests, compliance basics and choosing a designer.

By the Prism Works Studio team · Updated 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • An estate agent website serves several audiences at once: buyers, tenants, sellers and landlords, and each needs a clear route through the site.
  • Property portals handle much of the searching, so an agent's own site often earns its keep by winning valuations and instructions.
  • Listing feeds, material information and fee disclosure have practical and regulatory implications, so plan them carefully and check current guidance.

Good estate agent website design gives buyers and tenants fast property search, and gives sellers and landlords a clear reason to book a valuation. Many people first see your properties on a portal, so your own site is not always where the search starts. But it is often where sellers and landlords decide who to trust with their home, and where buyers check out the agent behind a listing.

This guide covers website design for UK estate agents and letting agents, including independent high street agencies, hybrid and online agents, and lettings and property management firms. It focuses on the website itself. For wider promotion, such as local search, social media and advertising, see our guide to estate agent marketing.

Four audiences, four journeys

Most agency sites need to serve at least four groups:

  • Buyers who want to search properties, book viewings and understand the buying process.
  • Tenants who want to find rentals, check requirements and apply.
  • Sellers who are choosing an agent and may want a valuation.
  • Landlords who are comparing letting and management services.

Clear navigation helps each group find their path quickly. A common pattern is a main menu with Buy, Rent, Sell and Landlords, plus a prominent property search on the homepage. Avoid making sellers and landlords wade through listings to find out how you can help them. Our guide to website navigation covers the principles.

Property search and listing feeds

Most agents manage listings in property software that can feed properties to the website automatically. This is usually far better than adding listings by hand, because it keeps prices, status and photos in step with your portals. When planning the site, check:

  • How your software provides the feed, and how often it updates.
  • Which search filters are available, such as location, price, bedrooms and property type.
  • Whether sold and let properties can remain visible as evidence of your track record, and whether that is appropriate.
  • How the feed handles features such as floor plans, virtual tours and brochures.

Search should work well on a phone. Filters that are fiddly on a small screen, or results that load slowly, can push people back to the portals. See our guide to mobile website design.

Listing pages

A good property page gives buyers or tenants what they need to decide whether to view. It usually includes:

  • A large, swipeable photo gallery.
  • Price, tenure and key facts near the top.
  • A floor plan and, where available, a video or virtual tour.
  • A clear description that is accurate rather than overblown.
  • Location information and a map.
  • A simple way to book a viewing or ask a question.

Accuracy matters. Agents are subject to consumer protection rules on misleading information, and National Trading Standards has published guidance on material information that should be included in property listings, such as council tax band, tenure and price. Requirements and guidance in this area have been developing, so check the current position and make sure your feed and templates can display the required fields.

Valuation requests: the page that wins instructions

For many agencies, the most commercially important page is the valuation page. A seller who requests a valuation is a potential instruction. Make it easy:

  • A clear "Book a valuation" call to action in the header and on key pages.
  • A short form asking only what you need to get started, such as address, contact details and timescale.
  • An explanation of what happens next, and whether the valuation is in person or remote.
  • Reassurance on why a seller might choose you, using factual points about your service.

Some agents offer an instant online valuation estimate. If you do, make clear it is an estimate based on data and not a substitute for an in-person valuation. Our guide to website contact forms covers how to keep forms short and useful.

Seller and landlord service pages

Sellers and landlords are comparing agents. Your service pages should explain what you actually do, in plain English:

  • How you market a property and what is included.
  • How viewings and feedback are handled.
  • For landlords, the difference between let-only, rent collection and full management.
  • Fees, or a clear way to find them.

Letting agents in England are generally required to display their fees and details of their client money protection and redress scheme membership on their website, and similar requirements apply in other parts of the UK. Membership of an approved redress scheme is also required for estate agents. Check the current rules for your nation and make sure this information is easy to find, not tucked away. Don't promise outcomes such as guaranteed sale prices or timescales.

Area guides and local knowledge

Local expertise is one of the strongest things an independent agent can show. Area guides covering the neighbourhoods you work in can help buyers and sellers alike. Consider including:

  • A genuine overview of each area and the types of property found there.
  • Transport links, schools, shops and green spaces.
  • Links to current listings in that area.

Write these from real knowledge rather than generic descriptions, and be careful with anything that may change, such as school catchments or performance, by linking to official sources. Original, useful area content can also help the site in local search. See our guide to local SEO.

Building trust

Choosing an agent involves trusting them with one of the biggest transactions in someone's life. The website can support that trust with:

  • An about page that introduces the real people in the branch.
  • Clear office address, opening hours and contact details.
  • Genuine reviews, linked to their source where possible.
  • Professional body and redress scheme memberships you actually hold.

Avoid vague claims such as "the area's leading agent" unless you can back them up. Our guide to website trust signals covers more ideas.

Tenant applications and portals

Letting agents often need tenants to register interest, apply for a property or report maintenance issues. Your property software may provide online forms or portals for this. Link them clearly from the website and make sure the experience on mobile is reasonable. Where personal data is collected, the site should have a clear privacy notice and handle data in line with UK GDPR. Referencing and right to rent checks involve sensitive information, so take care over which systems hold it.

Guides for buyers, sellers and landlords

Many people moving home or letting a property for the first time are unsure how the process works. Short, plain-English guides, such as what happens after an offer is accepted, what a seller needs to prepare before marketing, or what a landlord's main responsibilities are, can position your agency as helpful and knowledgeable. Keep legal and regulatory points general, date the guides, and link to official government sources for detail, because rules on lettings in particular change often and vary across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. Link each guide to the relevant service page and valuation call to action so readers have a natural next step.

Platform and integration choices

Many agencies use a website provided by their property software, while others build on WordPress or a similar platform with the listing feed integrated. Software-provided sites can be quick to set up but may limit design and content. A custom-built site gives more flexibility for area guides, valuation journeys and branding, but the feed integration needs care. See our guides to website builders vs custom websites and redesigning without losing rankings if you are replacing an existing site.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A homepage that only serves buyers and ignores sellers and landlords.
  • Listings that are out of sync with portals.
  • Valuation forms that ask for too much.
  • Hidden or missing fee and redress information for lettings.
  • Slow, image-heavy pages that frustrate mobile users.

Choosing an estate agent website designer

The feed integration and valuation journey are where these projects most often go wrong, so raise them early. Before you commit, ask:

  • Can you work with our property software, and have you confirmed how its feed works?
  • What happens to a property page, and its search listing, when a home is sold or let?
  • Can our team add area guides and service pages without developer help?
  • How will you protect our current search rankings if this replaces an existing site?
  • Can the listing template show the material information we need to display?
  • Will we own the domain, website content and analytics accounts?
  • What is included in the price, and which feed or software costs are separate?

FAQs

What should an estate agent website include?

Property search, detailed listing pages, a valuation request, seller and landlord service pages, area guides, clear contact details, and the fee and redress information that applies to your agency.

How much does an estate agent website cost?

It depends on the feed integration, search features and number of pages, so ask for a written scope. Our published website prices are on our pricing page.

Do estate agents still need a website if they use property portals?

Portals help buyers find listings, but your own site is where sellers and landlords judge your service and request valuations. The two work together.

Can listings update automatically on an estate agent website?

Usually, yes. Most property software can provide a feed that keeps the website in step with your listings. Check how your provider supports this.

What must letting agents show on their website?

In England, letting agents are generally required to show fees and details of client money protection and redress scheme membership. Rules vary across the UK, so check current guidance.

Should an estate agent offer an online valuation tool?

It can attract enquiries, but make clear it is an estimate and encourage a proper valuation for accurate advice.

What makes a good property listing page?

Strong photos, accurate key facts, a floor plan, location details and an easy way to book a viewing, with required material information included.
