WordPress Reading and Discussion Settings: What Each Option Does

A plain guide to WordPress Reading and Discussion settings: homepage display, posts page, search visibility, comments, moderation and avatars.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Reading settings decide what your homepage shows, where your blog lives and whether search engines are discouraged from indexing the site.
  • Always check the search engine visibility box is unticked on a live site, especially after launching from a staging copy.
  • Discussion settings control comments, moderation and notifications, so set them to match how much time you can give to replies.

Two screens in the WordPress Settings menu quietly shape how a site behaves: Reading and Discussion. Reading decides what visitors see on the homepage, how the blog is laid out and whether search engines are asked to stay away. Discussion controls comments, moderation and the small notices WordPress sends between sites.

Most of these options are set once and forgotten. That is fine when they are right, but a single wrong tick can hide a site from search results or open a blog up to a flood of unwanted comments. This guide walks through each option in plain terms and suggests sensible choices for a UK business site.

Where to find these settings

Log in to the dashboard and open Settings in the left-hand menu. Reading and Discussion are separate screens. You need an administrator account to change them. If your site uses a block theme, some display choices also live in the site editor, but the core options covered here still sit under Settings.

Reading settings

Your homepage displays

The first choice is whether the homepage shows your latest posts or a static page.

  • Your latest posts. The homepage becomes a running list of blog posts. This suits sites where the blog is the main product, such as a publication or a personal journal.
  • A static page. You pick a page to act as the homepage, and optionally a separate page to hold the list of posts. This is what most business sites need: a designed homepage that explains what you do, with the blog living at its own address.

When you choose a static page, you select two pages from drop-downs. The homepage should be a real page with your content. The posts page should be an empty page, often called Blog or News. WordPress fills it automatically with your posts, and anything you type into that page's content area is usually ignored by the theme.

For more on what a business homepage should contain, see our website homepage design guide.

Blog pages show at most

This sets how many posts appear on each page of the blog list, and on category and tag archives. There is no single right number. A smaller number keeps pages lighter and quicker to scan. A larger number means fewer pages to click through. Choose something that suits your theme's layout, for example a number that fills complete rows if posts appear in a grid.

Syndication feeds show the most recent

WordPress produces an RSS feed that feed readers and some tools use to pick up new posts. This option sets how many recent items the feed includes. The default is fine for most sites.

For each post in a feed, include

You can include the full text of each post or just an excerpt. Full text is convenient for readers using feed readers. An excerpt encourages them to visit the site. Some site owners prefer excerpts to reduce the chance of content being copied wholesale from the feed, although that is not a guarantee.

Search engine visibility

This is the most important box on the screen. Ticking "Discourage search engines from indexing this site" asks search engines not to index it. Search engines usually respect the request, so a live site with this box ticked can drop out of results or never appear.

The box is often ticked on purpose during a build or on a staging copy, which is sensible. The risk is that the setting gets carried over when the site goes live. After any launch, migration or restore from a staging copy, check this box is unticked. It is one of the first things we look at in a technical SEO audit.

Note that this setting is a request, not a lock. It does not stop people visiting the site. If you need to keep a staging site private, use password protection from your host as well.

Discussion settings

The Discussion screen is longer and can look intimidating. It splits roughly into default settings for new posts, rules for who can comment, moderation, notifications and avatars.

Default post settings

Three options at the top control the default behaviour for new posts:

  • Attempt to notify any blogs linked to from the post. When you link to another WordPress site, yours may send it a notice, called a pingback.
  • Allow link notifications from other blogs. This lets other sites send pingbacks and trackbacks to you. These have long been a source of spam, and many business sites turn them off.
  • Allow people to submit comments on new posts. This sets whether comments are open by default. You can still change it on individual posts.

These defaults only affect posts created after you change them. Existing posts keep their own settings, which you can change in bulk from the posts list using bulk edit.

Should a business site allow comments at all?

It depends on whether anyone will read and reply. Comments can add useful questions and show that real people engage with your content. They also need moderation, attract spam and can sit unanswered, which looks worse than having no comments. If you do not have time to respond, turning them off is a reasonable choice. Visitors with questions can still use your contact form.

Other comment settings

If you keep comments on, these options are worth reviewing:

  • Comment author must fill out name and email. Usually left on.
  • Users must be registered and logged in to comment. Useful for membership sites, unnecessary for most others.
  • Automatically close comments on older posts. Closing comments after a set period reduces spam on old content that nobody is watching.
  • Show comments cookies opt-in checkbox. This lets commenters choose whether their details are remembered. Keeping it on is a sensible privacy default, but check current ICO guidance on cookies and consent for your own situation.
  • Enable threaded comments. Allows replies to replies, up to a depth you choose.
  • Break comments into pages. Helpful only if posts get very long comment threads.

Notifications

You can ask WordPress to email you when anyone posts a comment, or when a comment is held for moderation. These emails go to the post author and the site admin address. If nobody acts on them, they just become noise, so make sure they reach someone responsible.

Moderation rules

This section decides what happens before a comment appears:

  • Comment must be manually approved. Every comment waits for approval. This is the safest option for a business site.
  • Comment author must have a previously approved comment. Returning commenters skip the queue.
  • Hold a comment if it contains a number of links. Spam often contains several links, so a low number catches a lot of it.
  • Comment moderation and disallowed keys. Lists of words, names, email addresses or IP addresses that send a comment to moderation or to the bin.

For a fuller approach to unwanted comments, see our WordPress comment spam guide.

Avatars

WordPress can show a small picture next to each commenter, pulled from a global avatar service linked to their email address. You can switch avatars off, set a maximum rating and pick a default image for people without one. Loading avatars from an outside service means each visitor's browser makes extra requests, so some site owners turn them off for speed or privacy. Check your privacy notice reflects whatever you choose.

A sensible starting point for a UK business site

Every site is different, but this is a reasonable baseline that you can adjust:

  • Homepage set to a static page, with a separate empty page for the blog.
  • Search engine visibility box unticked on the live site, ticked on staging along with password protection.
  • Pingbacks and trackbacks off unless you have a reason to use them.
  • Comments either off, or on with manual approval, link limits and automatic closing on older posts.
  • Notifications going to someone who will act on them.
  • Avatars on or off depending on your design and privacy approach.

When settings get changed by accident

Themes, imports and site migrations can change these settings without anyone noticing. A homepage that suddenly shows a list of posts, or a site disappearing from search results, are classic signs. Recording your chosen settings somewhere simple, such as a site handover document, makes it quick to check them after any big change.

FAQs

Why is my WordPress site not showing in Google?

There can be many reasons, but check the search engine visibility box in Settings, then Reading first. If it is ticked, search engines are being asked not to index the site.

Why does my blog page ignore the content I added?

When a page is set as the posts page, WordPress fills it with your post list and most themes ignore the page's own content. Put introductory text in the theme's archive settings or template instead.

Should I turn off comments on my business blog?

If nobody has time to moderate and reply, turning them off is often better than leaving comments unanswered. You can still invite questions through a contact form.

Do Discussion settings change existing posts?

The default post settings only apply to new posts. Use bulk edit in the posts list to open or close comments on existing posts.

What are pingbacks and trackbacks?

They are notices one site sends to another when it links to it. They are rarely useful for business sites and often attract spam, so many owners switch them off.

Is the search visibility setting enough to hide a staging site?

No. It is a request to search engines, not access control. Use password protection or your host's privacy tools as well.
