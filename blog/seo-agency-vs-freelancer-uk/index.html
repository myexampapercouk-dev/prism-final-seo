SEO Agency vs Freelancer vs In-House: Which Is Right for Your UK Business?

SEO agency vs freelancer in the UK comes down to scope, risk and budget. Compare agencies, freelancers and in-house hires to find the right fit for you.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Freelancers suit focused, well-defined SEO needs; agencies suit broader scopes needing several skills; in-house suits sustained, large-scale programmes.
  • Compare total cost and continuity risk, not just the monthly fee: holidays, illness, staff turnover and management time all have a price.
  • Whichever route you choose, insist on clear scope, access to your own accounts and reporting tied to enquiries and revenue.

Choosing between an SEO agency and a freelancer in the UK comes down to scope, risk and budget: freelancers suit focused, well-defined work, agencies suit broader programmes that need several specialist skills, and in-house teams suit large sites with sustained, daily SEO needs. The best choice is the one that covers the skills your site actually needs without paying for capacity you will not use.

This guide compares the three options side by side, looks at cost and continuity risk, and gives you a checklist for deciding. It is written for UK business owners and marketing leads weighing up their first serious SEO investment or reviewing an existing arrangement.

SEO agency vs freelancer: the key differences

The biggest difference is breadth. A good freelancer may be excellent at technical SEO or content strategy, but modern SEO also involves digital PR, analytics setup, structured data, development support and, increasingly, visibility in AI search. An agency spreads those skills across a team; a freelancer either covers them personally or subcontracts.

The second difference is continuity. With one person, a holiday or illness pauses the work. Agencies have more cover, though you should still ask who will actually work on your account and how often that changes.

The third is accountability and process. Agencies tend to have documented processes, contracts and reporting templates. Freelancers can be more flexible and often give you direct access to the person doing the work, which many businesses value.

Neither is automatically better. A specialist freelancer can outperform a large agency where the agency assigns junior staff, and a small, focused agency can offer the personal attention of a freelancer with more depth behind it.

It also helps to be honest about what you want from the relationship. Some businesses want a strategic partner who challenges priorities and joins up channels. Others want a reliable pair of hands to execute an agreed plan. A freelancer is often ideal for the second; an agency is usually better placed for the first, particularly where SEO needs to work with paid search, website changes and content production at the same time.

Finally, consider how much of the work will need development support. Many SEO recommendations, from template fixes to structured data and site speed improvements, depend on someone being able to change the site. If neither you nor your provider can make those changes, recommendations stall. Ask every option how fixes will actually be implemented, who does it and how long it typically takes.

When a freelancer makes sense

  • You need a specific, well-defined piece of work, such as a technical SEO audit or a migration review.
  • Your site is small and your local market is not highly competitive.
  • You have someone internally who can implement changes and manage priorities.
  • You want to fill a specialist gap alongside an existing team.
  • Your budget is limited and you prefer to pay for discrete projects.

When an agency makes sense

  • You need technical, content and authority work joined up in one plan.
  • You want SEO connected to paid media, website development or digital PR.
  • You lack internal time to manage several specialists.
  • You want continuity of service through holidays and staff changes.
  • You operate in a competitive market, such as ecommerce, finance or SaaS, where several disciplines need to move together.

When in-house SEO makes sense

An in-house hire works best for large sites with frequent changes, such as big ecommerce catalogues, publishers or marketplaces, where SEO decisions come up daily and need to be built into product and development workflows. An in-house specialist understands the business deeply and can influence decisions early.

The trade-offs are cost and coverage. One hire rarely covers every skill, so many businesses with in-house SEO still use an agency or freelancer for digital PR, technical projects or specialist audits. Recruitment also takes time, and when the person leaves, knowledge can leave with them unless processes are documented.

Questions to ask in a first meeting

Whichever route you lean towards, a first conversation should tell you a lot. Ask who will do the work day to day, how they would approach your first three months, which of your pages they would prioritise and why, and how they report progress. Ask how they handle disagreement about priorities, and what they need from you to succeed. Good providers will ask you plenty of questions in return about your margins, your best customers and your capacity to handle more enquiries, because SEO only matters if it brings the right business.

For a business running an online shop, it is also worth asking about platform experience. Shopify, WooCommerce and custom builds each have their own quirks, and someone who already knows them will move faster.

Risks to watch with any option

  • Account ownership: Google Search Console, GA4, Google Business Profile and your domain should always be owned by you, with access granted to the provider.
  • Guarantees: no freelancer, agency or employee can guarantee rankings. Treat promises of first-page positions with suspicion.
  • Risky link building: cheap link packages can harm your site. Our guide to digital PR and link building explains safer approaches.
  • Long lock-ins: check notice periods and what happens to work in progress if you leave.
  • Vague reporting: rankings alone are not enough; reports should connect to traffic, enquiries and revenue.

Decision checklist

Work through these questions to decide which route fits:

  1. Is the work a defined project or an ongoing programme?
  2. How many disciplines does it need: technical, content, links, analytics, development?
  3. Who internally will implement changes and manage the provider?
  4. How competitive is your market and how large is your site?
  5. What would a two-week pause in the work cost you?
  6. Do you own all your accounts and data?
  7. Have you compared total cost, including management time and tools?
  8. Are the scope, deliverables, reporting and notice period written down?
  9. Is SEO likely to need joining up with paid media or website work in the next year?

FAQs

Should I choose an SEO agency vs freelancer in the UK?

Choose a freelancer for focused, well-defined work or a specialist gap, and an agency when you need several disciplines joined up with less management from you. Your scope, budget and internal capacity should drive the decision.

Are SEO freelancers cheaper than agencies?

Often the headline rate is lower, but compare total cost: cover for absence, tools, management time and any subcontracted work. For broad programmes, the difference may be smaller than it first appears.

When should a business hire an in-house SEO?

When SEO decisions come up daily, typically on large ecommerce, publishing or marketplace sites, and the business can support recruitment, tools and line management.

Can I combine in-house SEO with an agency?

Yes. Many businesses keep a generalist in-house and use an agency or freelancer for specialist areas such as digital PR, technical audits or migrations.

How long before SEO shows results?

Whichever option you choose, SEO results typically show over three to six months, depending on your starting point, competition and how quickly changes are implemented.

What should be in an SEO contract?

Scope, deliverables, reporting frequency, account ownership, notice period and what happens to work and data when the arrangement ends.
