Painter and Decorator Website Design: Portfolios, Quotes and Finish Quality

Painter and decorator website design for UK businesses: before-and-after portfolios, quote requests, interior and exterior pages, and honest detail on finishes.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Painting and decorating is judged on finish, so a well-organised portfolio of your own before-and-after photos is usually the most persuasive part of the site.
  • Help people describe the job in a quote form, with room types, surfaces, condition and photos, so you can give a realistic estimate.
  • Explain preparation, timescales and what happens in an occupied home honestly, without promising results or durations you cannot guarantee.

Painting and decorating looks simple from the outside, which is part of the challenge for decorators online. Customers often compare quotes without understanding why one is higher than another, and they may not appreciate how much preparation affects the finish. A decorator's website has a chance to show what good work looks like, explain what goes into it, and make it easy for the right customers to ask for a quote.

This guide is about the website itself. For broader promotion, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK. If you also offer full interior design or styling, our guide to interior designer website design covers that side, which is different from presenting a decorating trade.

Know which customers you want

Decorators vary widely. Some focus on quick, affordable repaints for landlords and letting agents. Others specialise in high-end finishes, period properties, wallpaper hanging or kitchen cabinet painting. Others mainly do commercial work. Your website should make it obvious within seconds which kind of decorator you are, because that shapes everything from the photos to the wording. A headline that says what you do and where, plus a line about the kind of work you are known for, does more than a generic "quality painting and decorating".

A portfolio organised by job type

Customers want to see work like theirs. A single long gallery of photos is less helpful than a portfolio organised by type:

  • Interior rooms, such as living rooms, bedrooms, hallways and stairwells.
  • Exterior work, such as render, masonry, woodwork and windows.
  • Wallpaper hanging, including feature walls and patterned papers.
  • Specialist finishes, such as kitchen cabinet painting or period features, if you offer them.
  • Commercial projects, such as offices, shops or communal areas.

Before-and-after pairs are especially persuasive because they show the transformation, not just the result. Short captions explaining the job, such as the condition of the surfaces and the preparation involved, help visitors understand the work behind the photo. Use your own photos, take them in good light, and get permission before showing identifiable homes or naming clients.

Keep image file sizes under control so the gallery does not slow the site. Our guide to website speed optimisation explains how.

Service pages for interior, exterior and specialist work

Separate service pages help visitors and search engines. Interior and exterior decorating are different jobs with different questions. Exterior pages might cover weather, access and the condition of render or woodwork, while interior pages might cover furniture, occupied homes and drying times. Specialist pages, such as wallpapering or cabinet spraying, can attract specific searches from customers who are ready to book.

If you use spraying equipment, explain what that means for the customer, such as masking and ventilation, without making claims about speed or durability that you cannot support. If exterior work depends on access equipment, say whether you arrange it.

Explaining preparation

Preparation is where much of the difference in quality and price comes from, yet customers rarely see it. A page or section explaining your approach, such as filling, sanding, priming and protecting floors and furniture, helps customers understand why quotes differ. Keep it factual and describe what you generally do rather than promising a fixed process for every job. This kind of explanation also gives you useful content for search, because people do search for questions about decorating preparation.

Quote requests that capture the right detail

A decorating quote depends on room sizes, surfaces, the condition of walls and woodwork, ceiling heights, colour changes and whether the home will be occupied. A quote form can make this easier by asking in plain language:

  • Which rooms or areas need decorating, and whether interior, exterior or both.
  • Surfaces involved, such as walls, ceilings, woodwork, doors or wallpaper.
  • Approximate condition, such as cracks, damp or peeling paint.
  • Preferred timing and whether the home will be lived in during the work.
  • Photos of the rooms.

Make clear whether the form leads to a visit, a remote estimate from photos or both. Our guide to website contact forms covers form length, spam and privacy.

Pricing information

Decorating prices vary with size, condition and finish, so fixed price lists are uncommon. You can still help visitors by explaining what affects price, whether you charge by the job or by the day, whether materials are included and whether figures include VAT. If you publish example prices, keep them accurate and current. Our guide to pricing page design looks at presenting pricing clearly.

Paint, colours and materials

Customers often want advice on paint types and finishes. A short guide to finishes, such as matt, eggshell and gloss, and where each tends to suit, can be genuinely useful. Explain whether you supply materials or whether customers can choose their own. Avoid naming specific paint brands unless you have a reason to, and do not suggest any manufacturer endorsement you do not have. Describe paint durability in general terms rather than promising how long a finish will last, since that depends on conditions and use.

Older properties can raise particular issues, such as old paint that may contain lead. If you mention this, keep it general and point customers to official health and safety guidance rather than giving definitive advice.

Working in occupied homes

Many decorating jobs happen while people are living in the house. A section explaining how you usually handle this, such as protecting furniture, keeping areas tidy, timings and ventilation, reassures customers and sets expectations. Only describe what you actually do. If there are things customers need to do beforehand, such as clearing rooms or removing pictures, say so clearly.

Landlords, letting agents and commercial clients

Repeat customers such as landlords, letting agents, property managers and small businesses can provide steady work, but they want different things from a homeowner. They usually care about turnaround between tenancies, consistent standards across several properties, clear invoicing and someone who answers the phone. If this work suits you, a short page aimed at these clients can explain the types of property you handle, how to request work, how access and keys are usually arranged and whether you can work outside normal hours to reduce disruption to a business. Keep it to what you genuinely offer, and avoid promising turnaround times you cannot meet at busy periods. A separate route for these clients also stops the homeowner pages becoming cluttered with trade detail.

Credentials and guarantees

If you belong to a trade association or have completed recognised training, say so accurately and follow any rules on logo use. State whether you hold public liability insurance if you do. If you offer a guarantee on your work, explain exactly what it covers and for how long, and any conditions. Vague promises such as "guaranteed perfect finish" are best avoided; consumer law already gives customers rights, and overstated guarantees can create problems.

Reviews and local presence

Reviews are especially valuable for decorators because customers want reassurance about tidiness, reliability and finish. Show genuine reviews and link them to relevant portfolio items where you can. Our guide to a testimonials page explains how to present them well. Never invent or misleadingly edit reviews. Keep your business details consistent with your Google Business Profile, and list the areas you actually cover.

Mobile experience

Customers often browse decorators on their phones in the evening, comparing portfolios. The site should load quickly, let photos be viewed comfortably on a small screen, and keep the quote button easy to reach. Our guide to mobile website design explains what matters.

Platform choice

A decorator's website is usually portfolio-led. Squarespace and Wix handle image galleries well and suit smaller businesses that want to update photos themselves. WordPress gives more flexibility for many service pages, area content and guides. Either way, the portfolio should be easy to update after each job.

FAQs

What is the most important part of a decorator's website?

Usually the portfolio. Customers want to see real before-and-after photos of work like theirs.

Should I publish decorating prices?

Fixed prices are rarely practical, but explaining how you charge and what affects the price helps.

Can I quote from photos?

Some decorators give rough estimates from photos and confirm on a visit. Explain on your site how you handle it.

Should I mention paint brands?

It is not necessary. Explaining finishes and whether you supply materials is usually enough.

Should I offer a guarantee?

Only if you can explain clearly what it covers, for how long and under what conditions.
