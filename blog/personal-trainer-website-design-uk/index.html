Website Design for Personal Trainers in the UK: Pages, Booking and Responsible Claims

Website design guide for UK personal trainers: training packages, booking, online coaching, health data, honest claims and platforms.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A personal trainer's website works best when it speaks to a specific type of client and makes booking a first session simple.
  • Clear packages, honest descriptions of qualifications and responsible wording about results build trust with people who may be nervous about starting.
  • Health information collected through forms needs particular care under UK GDPR, and results claims need to be realistic and supportable.

Personal training is a trust-based service. Many people considering a trainer are nervous: about their fitness, about being judged, about whether they will stick with it, or about an injury or health condition. They want to find someone who understands their situation and who they will feel comfortable working with. A personal trainer's website cannot replace that first session, but it can give people the confidence to book it.

This guide is for UK personal trainers, whether you train clients in a gym, at home, outdoors, online or a mix. It focuses on the website itself: who it is for, the pages to include, packages, booking and payments, online coaching, health information, honest claims and choosing a platform. If you run a gym or studio and are looking at marketing more broadly, our gym marketing guide covers memberships, local promotion and advertising.

Be specific about who you train

"Helping you get fit" describes nearly every trainer. A website is usually more effective when it speaks directly to a particular person, for example:

  • Beginners who have never trained before and feel intimidated by gyms.
  • People returning to exercise after having a baby.
  • Older adults wanting to stay strong and mobile.
  • Runners or cyclists wanting strength training to support their sport.
  • Busy professionals who need efficient sessions around work.

You may train more than one group, and that is fine. Consider a short section or separate page for each, written in the words those clients would use. If you work in a specialist area that needs additional qualifications, such as training people with particular medical conditions or during pregnancy, only describe it if you hold the relevant training.

The pages a personal trainer's website needs

Home page

Say who you help, where or how you train, and what the first step is, usually a free consultation or an introductory session. Follow with how training with you works, a short introduction to you, genuine testimonials if you have them, and the call to action again. Our hero section guide covers the first screen in detail.

About page

Clients want to know who they will be training with. Share your background, why you became a trainer, your training style and your qualifications. Personal training is not a protected title in the UK, so describe your qualifications and any professional memberships accurately and avoid implying credentials you do not hold. Good photos of you, ideally taken where you train, help clients feel they know you before they meet.

Training options and packages

Explain the ways clients can work with you, covered below.

How it works

Walk through what happens from first contact to regular sessions. Explain what a first session involves, what to bring and what to expect. This is especially reassuring for beginners.

Testimonials or client stories

Real words from real clients, with permission. More on this below.

Contact and booking

An easy way to book or get in touch, with your phone number and location or training areas.

Policies

A privacy notice, terms of service and your cancellation policy.

Presenting packages and pricing

Many trainers find it clearer to offer a small number of defined options rather than a long list. These might include single sessions, blocks of sessions, monthly packages, small group or partner training, and online coaching. For each, explain who it suits, how often sessions happen, how long they last and what is included. Only list inclusions you genuinely provide, such as programme design or check-ins.

Some trainers show prices on their site; others discuss them during a consultation. Showing prices or starting points can help visitors judge fit before getting in touch. Whichever you choose, be clear about any minimum commitments, how block bookings expire and what happens if a client wants to stop. Our pricing page design guide explains the options.

Booking and payments

Trainers spend a lot of time arranging and rearranging sessions. An online booking system can reduce that by letting clients see availability, book and reschedule within your rules, and receive automated reminders. Useful features to look for include:

  • Bookings that sync with your own calendar.
  • Support for packages and blocks of sessions.
  • Payment or deposits at the time of booking, if you want them.
  • Automated reminders.
  • Cancellation rules that match your policy.

Our online booking systems guide covers the options in more detail. Make your cancellation policy clear at the point of booking, and check that your terms are fair and consistent with consumer law, particularly for prepaid packages and subscriptions. Our article on subscriptions covers recurring payment considerations.

Online coaching

Many trainers now offer online coaching, with programmes, check-ins and support delivered remotely. If you do, your website should explain clearly how it works, what clients receive, how often you are in contact, which app or platform you use for delivery and how to sign up. Online coaching can broaden your reach beyond a local area, so the site may need to work for people who will never meet you in person. Be realistic about the level of support included, and only describe features you actually provide.

If you sell digital products such as training plans, you will need a way to take payments and deliver files. Squarespace, Wix and Shopify all offer options for this.

Health information and privacy

Trainers often collect health information before working with a client, such as through a pre-exercise questionnaire. Health data is treated as special category data under UK GDPR, which means it needs extra care.

  • Only collect the information you genuinely need, and explain why you need it.
  • Use forms and tools that store information securely.
  • Make sure your privacy notice explains what you collect, why, how long you keep it and who can access it.
  • Think about whether you need an appropriate lawful basis and condition for processing health data.

The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for small businesses on UK GDPR and special category data. Check what applies to you, as this guide cannot cover your specific situation. Our contact forms guide covers form design, and our cookie banner guide covers consent for cookies and tracking.

Honest claims about results

Before and after photos and dramatic results claims are common in fitness marketing, but they need care.

  • Avoid guarantees. Results depend on many factors outside your control. Promises such as a set amount of weight loss in a set time are difficult to support.
  • Use real client stories with permission. Never invent testimonials or use images of people who are not your clients. If you use before and after photos, get clear written consent and consider noting that results vary.
  • Be careful with health claims. Avoid claiming that training will treat or cure medical conditions. Encourage clients with health concerns to speak to a medical professional.
  • Nutrition advice. If you offer nutrition guidance, stay within your qualifications and be careful about claims, especially around supplements.

UK advertising rules generally require marketing claims to be truthful and supportable, and the Advertising Standards Authority has published guidance relevant to weight loss and health claims. Check it before publishing results-focused content. Our testimonials page guide explains how to gather and present genuine client feedback.

Getting found locally

If you train people in person, local search matters. A complete Google Business Profile, clear information about where you train, and genuine reviews can all help people nearby find you. Our local SEO guide and Google Business Profile guide explain the essentials. Helpful content, such as advice for beginners starting strength training, can also support visibility; keep it accurate and within your expertise.

Choosing a platform

Squarespace and Wix are popular with personal trainers who want a professional site they can manage themselves, with built-in booking and payment options. WordPress offers more flexibility for trainers who plan to publish lots of content, sell programmes or build membership features. Our comparisons of Wix vs WordPress and Squarespace vs WordPress explain the differences. Whatever you choose, make sure the site works well on a phone, as many people will find you that way. FAQs

What should a personal trainer's website include?

A clear home page, an about page with accurate qualifications, your training options, an explanation of how it works, genuine testimonials, easy booking and a privacy notice.

Should personal trainers show prices online?

Many do, as it helps visitors judge fit. Others discuss prices in a consultation. Either way, be clear about commitments and cancellation terms.

Can I use before and after photos on my website?

Only of real clients who have given clear consent. Avoid implying typical results and check Advertising Standards Authority guidance on health and weight loss claims.

Do I need to protect clients' health information?

Yes. Health data is special category data under UK GDPR, so collect only what you need, store it securely and explain its use in your privacy notice.

Which platform is best for a personal trainer's website?

Squarespace and Wix suit trainers wanting a simple site with booking. WordPress suits those planning lots of content, digital programmes or membership features.
