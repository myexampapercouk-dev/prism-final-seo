Ecommerce Transactional Emails: Order Confirmations, Shipping Updates and More

A guide to ecommerce transactional emails for UK online shops: order confirmations, dispatch and delivery updates, refunds, design, deliverability and rules.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Transactional emails are triggered by something a customer did, such as placing an order, and they are among the most opened messages a shop sends.
  • Each email should answer the customer's likely question at that moment: what did I order, when will it arrive, what happens next.
  • Keep them clearly service-led; heavy marketing content in transactional emails can raise consent questions and reduce trust.

When someone places an order on your online shop, the next thing they usually see from you is an email. Order confirmations, dispatch notices, delivery updates and refund confirmations are known as transactional emails: messages triggered by an action the customer took, rather than campaigns you send to a list. They are easy to overlook because every ecommerce platform sends them by default. But default templates are generic, and these emails shape how customers feel about the purchase they have just made.

This guide looks at the transactional emails an online shop typically sends, what each should contain, how to design and write them, how to make sure they actually arrive and where the line sits between service messages and marketing. It focuses on UK shops using platforms like Shopify and WooCommerce. For newsletters and automated marketing flows, see our separate guide to ecommerce email marketing.

Why transactional emails matter

Customers actively look for these emails. They want reassurance that the order went through, they want to know when it will arrive and they want something to refer back to if there is a problem. That makes transactional emails a key part of the post-purchase experience. A clear, well-branded confirmation reassures; a confusing or missing one prompts anxious "where is my order?" messages to your customer service inbox.

They also matter for trust. Phishing emails often pretend to be order or delivery notifications, so customers are rightly cautious. Emails that look consistent, come from a recognisable address on your own domain and contain specific order details are easier to trust.

The core transactional emails

Order confirmation

Sent immediately after checkout. It should include:

  • A clear subject line, such as "Your order #1234 is confirmed".
  • The order number and date.
  • Each item with image, name, variant (size, colour), quantity and price.
  • Subtotal, delivery cost, any discount, VAT where relevant, and total paid.
  • Delivery address and chosen delivery method, with an expected dispatch or delivery window if you can give one reliably.
  • Billing summary, without showing full card details.
  • What happens next, and how to contact you.
  • Links to your delivery and returns information.

Under UK consumer rules, online sellers generally need to give customers certain information and confirm the contract in a durable medium, which an email can provide. What exactly needs to be included depends on what you sell, so it is worth checking current guidance or taking advice rather than assuming a default template covers everything.

Dispatch or shipping confirmation

Sent when the order leaves you. Include what has been sent (especially if an order is split across parcels), the carrier, a tracking link and an expected delivery date if available. If part of the order is still to come, say so clearly.

Delivery updates

Some shops send "out for delivery" and "delivered" messages, either themselves or via the carrier. Avoid duplicating the carrier's notifications so heavily that customers receive a flood of near-identical emails; decide which messages you send and which you leave to the carrier.

Order changes and cancellations

If an order is edited, delayed, partly out of stock or cancelled, tell the customer promptly and plainly, with what it means for them and what they need to do, if anything.

Returns and refunds

Confirm when a return request is received, when the returned item arrives and when the refund is processed, including the amount and how long it may take to appear. These emails are often neglected but reduce a lot of follow-up contact.

Account and security emails

If you offer customer accounts, you will also send account creation, password reset and possibly email change confirmations. These should be short, functional and secure, with links that expire. Our guide to customer accounts and wishlists covers accounts more widely.

Gift card and digital delivery emails

If you sell gift cards or digital products, the email may be the product itself. Make the code or download obvious and explain how to use it. See our gift cards guide for more.

Writing transactional emails

Each email should answer the question the customer has at that moment. Lead with it:

  • Confirmation: "We've got your order."
  • Dispatch: "Your order is on its way."
  • Refund: "Your refund of £X has been processed."

Keep the tone consistent with your brand, but prioritise clarity. A warm thank you is welcome; a long paragraph before the order details is not. Use short sentences, plain language and specific dates rather than vague phrases like "soon". Avoid internal jargon such as fulfilment status codes that mean nothing to a customer.

Subject lines should be descriptive, include the order number and make the email easy to find later by searching an inbox. Clever or teasing subject lines belong in marketing campaigns, not here.

Designing transactional emails

Good transactional email design is simple and consistent:

  • Branding. Your logo, colours and fonts (with safe fallbacks) so the email is clearly from you.
  • Mobile-first layout. Many people read emails on their phones. Use a single column, readable text sizes and buttons large enough to tap.
  • Clear hierarchy. The key message and order number at the top, then items, then totals, then addresses and help.
  • Product images. Small thumbnails help customers confirm they ordered the right thing, but the email should still make sense if images are blocked.
  • Accessible. Sufficient colour contrast, real text rather than text baked into images, and meaningful link text.
  • Consistent across all emails. The confirmation, dispatch and refund messages should feel like one family.

Shopify lets you edit notification templates in the admin, and WooCommerce emails can be customised through settings, templates or plugins. Test changes carefully, because template edits can break layouts in some email clients.

Making sure emails arrive

A beautifully designed confirmation is no use if it lands in spam or never arrives. Deliverability depends on several things:

  • Send from your own domain. Use an address like orders@yourdomain.co.uk rather than a generic platform address where possible.
  • Authenticate the domain. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC records so receiving mail servers can verify the emails are genuinely from you. Our guide to business domain and email setup explains these.
  • Use a reliable sending service. Hosted platforms like Shopify handle sending for you. WordPress and WooCommerce sites often send through the web server by default, which can be unreliable; routing email through a dedicated transactional email service is commonly recommended.
  • Keep transactional and marketing separate. Some businesses send them from different addresses or subdomains, so problems with marketing emails do not affect order confirmations.
  • Allow replies. Avoid "no-reply" addresses where you can. Customers will reply to order emails, and those replies should reach someone.

Place test orders regularly and check emails arrive promptly in major inbox providers, not just your own.

Transactional versus marketing content

It is tempting to add product recommendations, discount codes or newsletter promotions to order confirmations because they are so widely opened. Some light, relevant content can be acceptable, but there are reasons for care.

Under PECR and UK GDPR, marketing emails generally need appropriate consent or must meet the conditions of the "soft opt-in". Purely service messages, such as an order confirmation, are treated differently. If a transactional email becomes primarily promotional, it may start to look like marketing, with the consent obligations that come with it. The ICO publishes guidance on direct marketing, and it is sensible to check it or take advice if you plan to include promotional content.

From a customer experience point of view, too much promotion also gets in the way. Someone checking their delivery date does not want to scroll past three banners. If you include anything beyond the service content, keep it below the essential information and relevant to the purchase, such as care instructions for the item bought.

Connecting emails to the rest of the shop

Transactional emails work best when they match what the website says:

  • Delivery estimates in emails should match those on product pages and at checkout.
  • Returns links should point to an up-to-date returns page. Our guide to delivery and returns pages covers what to include.
  • Tracking links should open a page that works on mobile.
  • Contact details should match your website and reach a monitored inbox.

Inconsistencies, such as an email promising next-day delivery when the checkout said three to five days, create confusion and complaints.

A transactional email checklist

  • Order confirmation, dispatch, refund and account emails all customised and branded.
  • Subject lines are descriptive and include the order number.
  • Each email leads with the key information.
  • Layout works on mobile and with images turned off.
  • Emails send from your own authenticated domain.
  • Replies reach a real, monitored inbox.
  • Promotional content, if any, is light, relevant and considered against marketing rules.
  • Details match the website's delivery and returns information.
  • Test orders are placed after any template or platform change.

FAQs

What is the difference between transactional and marketing emails?

Transactional emails are triggered by a customer's action, such as placing an order, and deliver service information. Marketing emails promote products or offers and generally need consent.

Can I edit the default order confirmation email?

Yes. Shopify and WooCommerce both let you customise notification emails, though the method differs. Test after any change.

Why are my order emails going to spam?

Common causes include sending from an unauthenticated domain, unreliable server email on self-hosted sites and spam-like content. Setting up SPF, DKIM and DMARC and using a reliable sending service often helps.

Should I use a no-reply address?

Generally it is better not to. Customers reply to order emails with questions, and those messages should reach someone who can help.
