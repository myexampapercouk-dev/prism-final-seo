Digital PR for Link Building: How UK Businesses Earn Quality Backlinks

How digital PR earns quality backlinks for UK businesses: campaign types, finding stories, pitching journalists, measuring results and what to avoid.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Digital PR earns links by giving journalists something genuinely worth covering: data, expertise, a timely angle or a useful resource.
  • Relevance beats volume: a few links from respected sites in your sector or region are worth more than many from unrelated blogs.
  • Measure coverage, links, referral traffic and branded search, and judge campaigns over months rather than days.

Digital PR is the practice of earning coverage and links from online publications by giving journalists and editors stories they want to publish. For SEO, it is one of the most reliable ways to build the authority that helps pages rank, and the brand mentions it creates also feed the sources AI assistants rely on.

This guide explains how digital PR works for UK businesses, the campaign types that tend to succeed, how to pitch, how to measure results and what to avoid.

Why links still matter

Search engines use links from other websites as one signal of trust and authority. Not all links are equal: a link from a respected national newspaper, an industry publication or a well-known local news site carries far more weight than one from a directory nobody visits. Links also send referral traffic and introduce your brand to new audiences.

Technical fixes and content can only take a site so far. In competitive markets, authority is often what separates page one from page three. That is why our SEO and GEO service works hand in hand with digital PR and content.

Types of digital PR campaign that work

Data-led stories

Journalists love new data. A survey, an analysis of public datasets or insight from your own anonymised data can produce headlines such as regional comparisons or trend stories. The data must be robust, the method clearly explained and the findings genuinely interesting.

Expert commentary

Journalists regularly need expert quotes on topics in the news. Responding quickly with useful, quotable comment through journalist request services, or directly to reporters who cover your sector, can earn high-quality links with relatively little effort.

Reactive news

When something happens in your industry, a fast, well-informed reaction can earn coverage. This needs a team that monitors the news and can turn around comment within hours.

Useful resources and tools

Calculators, guides, templates and checklists that people want to reference can earn links over time, especially from bloggers, trade bodies and educators.

Local and regional stories

For businesses serving particular areas, local news sites, regional business publications and community organisations are valuable and often more accessible than national titles. Local relevance also supports local SEO.

Finding a story worth covering

The hardest part of digital PR is the idea. A good story usually has at least one of these:

  • Newness: data or insight nobody has published before.
  • Relevance: it matters to the publication's readers.
  • Timeliness: it connects to something happening now, such as a season, a policy change or a news event.
  • Emotion or surprise: it challenges an assumption or provokes a reaction.
  • Authority: it comes from someone with genuine expertise.

Start with what you already have: expertise in your team, data from your business, and questions customers ask repeatedly. Often the best stories are hiding in plain sight.

How to pitch journalists

  1. Target carefully. Build a list of journalists and publications that actually cover your topic. A small, relevant list beats a mass email.
  2. Lead with the story. The subject line and first sentence should contain the headline finding. Journalists are busy and skim.
  3. Keep it short. A few short paragraphs, the key data points, a quote and an offer of more detail.
  4. Make it easy. Provide images, methodology and a spokesperson who is available.
  5. Follow up once. A polite follow-up is fine. Repeated chasing is not.
  6. Respect embargoes and corrections. Accuracy builds long-term relationships.

Where to link: getting the most from coverage

Coverage often links to your homepage, but a link to the page that hosts your research or resource is usually more natural. That page should then link to your important service pages, passing authority through your site. Strong internal linking turns one good piece of coverage into a boost for many pages.

Not every article will include a link. Unlinked mentions still help brand awareness and AI visibility, and it is reasonable to politely ask a journalist to add a link to the source of the data they used.

An example campaign, step by step

Imagine a UK online homeware retailer that wants links to its category pages. Here is how a modest data-led campaign might run.

  1. Idea. The team notices customers in different regions favour different colours and styles. Using anonymised order data, it can show regional preferences, which is the kind of light, visual story lifestyle and regional editors like.
  2. Validation. Before investing, the team checks the angle has not been covered recently and that the data is robust enough to stand up to scrutiny.
  3. Asset. A page on the retailer's site presents the findings with a simple map, a short method note and links to relevant category pages.
  4. Outreach. Tailored pitches go to interiors journalists, regional news desks and lifestyle bloggers, each leading with the angle most relevant to their readers.
  5. Follow-through. Coverage is tracked, journalists who used the data without linking are politely asked to credit the source, and the findings are reused in social posts and email.

The result is not guaranteed, but a well-validated idea with a clear regional hook gives editors a reason to cover it, and gives the retailer relevant links to pages that matter commercially.

Building relationships, not just campaigns

The most consistent digital PR results come from relationships. Journalists who know you respond quickly with accurate, useful comment come back to you. Keep a simple record of who covered what, follow the reporters who write about your sector, and offer help even when it does not lead directly to a link. Over time, this turns outreach from cold pitching into conversations.

Measuring digital PR

Useful measures include:

  • Number and quality of pieces of coverage, and the relevance of each publication.
  • Links earned, and whether they are followed or tagged as sponsored or nofollow.
  • Referral traffic from coverage.
  • Changes in rankings for target pages over the following months.
  • Growth in branded search in Search Console.

Results arrive in waves: an initial burst of coverage, then a gradual effect on rankings. Judge campaigns over months, not days.

Budgeting for digital PR

Digital PR is labour-intensive. The cost depends on the type of campaign: ongoing expert commentary is lighter, while a data-led national campaign needs research, design and extensive outreach. Most businesses get the best value from a steady rhythm of smaller activities plus occasional larger campaigns, rather than one big launch a year. What to avoid

  • Buying links or paying for "guest posts" on sites that exist only to sell links.
  • Misleading data: small, biased samples presented as national findings damage credibility.
  • Irrelevant coverage on sites with no connection to your business or audience.
  • Spamming journalists with untargeted pitches; it gets your domain ignored.
  • Guaranteed link packages: nobody can guarantee what editors will publish.

Digital PR for different sectors

The approach varies. B2B companies often succeed with trade publications and expert commentary. SaaS businesses can use product data to tell industry stories. Retailers in the ecommerce sector do well with consumer trends and seasonal angles. Regulated sectors need extra care with claims, which we always check with you first.

Begin by listing your expertise, your data and the publications your customers read. From there, a simple plan of expert commentary plus one data or resource campaign a quarter is a strong start. If you would like help shaping that plan, book a free discovery call.

FAQs

What is digital PR in SEO?

It is the practice of earning online coverage and links from publications by giving journalists newsworthy stories, data or expert comment, which builds the authority that helps pages rank.

How many backlinks does a digital PR campaign get?

It varies widely with the story and the sector. Nobody can guarantee a number, because editors decide what to publish; focus on relevance and quality rather than volume.

Is digital PR better than buying links?

Yes. Earned links are what search engines intend links to represent, while paid links that pass ranking value can breach search guidelines and put your rankings at risk.

How long does digital PR take to affect rankings?

Coverage can arrive within days of a campaign launching, but the effect on rankings usually builds over the following weeks and months.

Can small businesses do digital PR?

Yes. Local news, trade publications and expert commentary are accessible and effective for small businesses, and often more relevant than national coverage.
