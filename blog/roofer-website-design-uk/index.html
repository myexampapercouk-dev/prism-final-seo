Roofer Website Design: Showing Work People Cannot See From the Ground

Roofer website design for UK roofing contractors: storm repair pages, flat and pitched roof services, project galleries, survey requests and trust details.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Customers usually cannot see the roof problem or the finished work themselves, so clear photos and plain explanations do much of the selling.
  • Separate pages for repairs, flat roofs, pitched roofs and chimneys or guttering help visitors find the right service and help search engines understand what you do.
  • Guarantees, insurance and trade body membership should only be shown where you genuinely hold them, described exactly as they apply.

Roofing has an unusual problem: the customer rarely sees the work. A homeowner notices a damp patch on a bedroom ceiling or a few slipped tiles on the drive after a windy night, but they cannot climb up and judge the problem or, later, the quality of the repair. That means they rely heavily on how a roofer explains things and on evidence of past work. A roofer's website has more explaining to do than most trade sites.

This guide focuses on the website: structure, pages, photos and enquiry forms for UK roofing contractors. For promotion beyond your own site, such as review platforms, directories and local advertising, see our guide to tradesman marketing in the UK.

Why roofing sites need to explain more

Many roofing customers start with a symptom rather than a service. They search for "water coming through ceiling", "tiles blown off roof" or "flat roof leaking", not "EPDM installation". Your website can meet them there by explaining common problems in plain English and then pointing them to the right service and next step.

It also helps to be clear about the difference between a repair and a replacement, and how you decide which to recommend. Customers are often wary of being told they need a whole new roof. A calm explanation of how you assess a roof, and that you will show them photos of what you found, can build more confidence than any slogan.

Storm damage and urgent repairs

Bad weather can bring a sudden spike in calls. If you offer urgent or temporary repairs, such as making a roof watertight with a tarpaulin until a full repair is possible, a dedicated page helps. It should cover:

  • Whether you offer urgent call-outs and roughly what hours.
  • Areas you cover for urgent work.
  • A tap-to-call button that stays visible on mobile.
  • Brief, sensible safety advice: do not climb onto the roof, keep clear of loose tiles or chimney debris, and move belongings away from leaks.

Many people will also be thinking about home insurance. You can say you are happy to provide photos and a written description of the damage to support a claim, if that is something you do, but avoid implying you can tell people what their insurer will cover. Each policy is different, so suggest customers check with their insurer.

A banner you can switch on yourself during busy periods, explaining that you are prioritising emergency weatherproofing and that quote visits may take longer, sets fair expectations.

Service pages for roofing

Roofing covers a wide range of work, and a single services list does it no justice. Typical pages might include:

  • Roof repairs. Slipped or broken tiles and slates, leaks, ridge and hip repairs.
  • Pitched roofs. Re-roofing, tile and slate replacement, battens and membranes.
  • Flat roofs. The systems you install, such as felt, rubber or fibreglass, explained in plain terms with honest pros and cons.
  • Chimneys and leadwork. Repointing, flashing, chimney removal if you offer it.
  • Fascias, soffits and guttering. Often a separate search in its own right.
  • Commercial roofing. If you work on commercial buildings, a separate page with the information facilities managers and contractors need.

Each page should say what the job involves, signs that someone might need it, how you approach quoting and a few real project photos. Avoid stuffing pages with town names or repeating the same paragraphs on every page.

Project galleries and photo evidence

Photos matter more for roofers than for almost any other trade, precisely because customers cannot see the work themselves. Useful types of image include:

  • Before, during and after shots of the same roof.
  • Close-ups of details such as leadwork, ridge tiles and valleys.
  • Photos showing scaffolding and site tidiness, which reassure people about disruption.
  • Drone or elevated photos, if you use a drone and operate it in line with current UK drone rules.

A gallery organised by type of work is easier to use than one long scroll. Short captions describing the problem and the solution make each project more useful and give search engines meaningful text. Only show your own work, and ask customers before showing images that identify their home. Compress images so they load quickly; our guide to website speed optimisation explains why this matters on mobile.

It is also worth explaining that you will share photos of what you find during a survey or repair. If that is genuinely part of how you work, it addresses one of the biggest worries roofing customers have.

Survey and quote requests

Most roofing jobs need a visit or at least photos before you can quote. A well-designed survey request form can save wasted trips. Consider asking for:

  • Type of property and roof, if known (pitched, flat, mixed).
  • The issue, in the customer's own words.
  • Postcode.
  • Whether the issue is urgent, for example an active leak.
  • Photos from the ground or inside, such as a ceiling stain.
  • Preferred times for a visit.

Make clear what happens after they submit: whether you call back, and roughly when. See our guide to website contact forms for more on keeping forms short and effective, and make sure your privacy notice covers any photos and personal data you collect.

Guarantees, insurance and memberships

Roofing customers often ask about guarantees and whether a firm is insured. Your website should answer clearly, but accurately:

  • If you offer a workmanship guarantee, explain what it covers and for how long, and how it works if you are no longer trading, if you have arranged any insurance-backed cover.
  • If materials come with manufacturer guarantees, explain that these are separate and depend on the manufacturer's terms.
  • Only display trade association or scheme logos where you are a current member, and follow each body's rules on using their marks.
  • State the insurance you hold in plain terms rather than vague phrases.

Some roofing work may involve building regulations, for example when replacing a significant part of a roof covering, and in some cases permissions such as planning or conservation area consent. Rather than giving detailed rules, you can say that you will advise whether approvals are likely to be needed and suggest customers check with their local authority. Keep this hedged and avoid making promises on behalf of anyone else.

Explaining prices without listing figures

Roofing prices vary widely with access, materials, scaffolding and roof condition. Rather than publishing figures that quickly go out of date, a pricing explanation page can describe what affects cost, whether quotes are written and itemised, whether scaffolding is included, and whether prices include VAT. This helps people understand your quote without inviting comparisons on the wrong basis.

Domestic and commercial visitors

If you work for both homeowners and commercial clients, separate their paths. Homeowners want reassurance and clear explanations. Commercial clients, property managers and main contractors often want to know your capacity, health and safety approach, insurance levels and how you handle documentation. A separate commercial page, or a short section of the site, lets each audience find what they need without wading through the other.

Local visibility

Roofing is a local search, especially after storms. An areas covered page, consistent contact details and a well-maintained Google Business Profile help. Our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile cover this. Adding real project write-ups that mention the area naturally tends to be more useful than thin location pages.

Choosing a platform

Most roofers do not need anything complicated. WordPress, Squarespace or Wix can all handle service pages, galleries and forms. What matters more is that you can add new projects and switch on a storm banner yourself without help. If you are unsure, our comparison of Wix and WordPress may help.

FAQs

What pages should a roofer's website have?

Typically a homepage, separate pages for repairs, pitched roofs, flat roofs, chimneys and guttering, a project gallery, an areas covered page, an about page and a survey or quote request page.

Should I have a storm damage page?

If you offer urgent or temporary repairs, yes. It helps people find you quickly when they need help most. Be clear about hours and coverage.

Can I say I help with insurance claims?

You can say you provide photos and written reports if you do, but avoid implying what an insurer will pay. Customers should check their own policy.

Should roofing prices be on my website?

Explaining what affects price is usually more useful than listing figures, since roofing jobs vary so much.

Are drone photos worth using?

They can show roof work far better than ground shots. Make sure any drone use follows current UK rules.
