Web Design in Derby: Websites for Rail and Aerospace Supply Chain Businesses

Web design for Derby rail and aerospace suppliers: approvals, tender-ready content, supplier portals, document control and sites that support bids.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Rail and aerospace buyers often check a supplier's website during pre-qualification, so treat it as part of your tender evidence.
  • Long programme lifecycles mean obsolescence, spares and through-life support content can matter as much as new-build capability.
  • Document control discipline belongs on the website too: dated, versioned downloads and accurate approvals.

Web design in Derby has a particular flavour because of the city's long connection with rail and aerospace engineering. Around the large train builders and aero engine work sits a broad supply chain of smaller firms: precision machinists, cable and harness makers, test houses, interiors specialists, software and signalling consultancies, inspection services and maintenance contractors. Many of these businesses win work through formal tenders and supplier qualification rather than through casual enquiries, and that changes what their websites need to do. This guide takes a different angle: websites that support procurement, tenders and very long product lifecycles, which are characteristic of rail and aerospace work.

Your website as tender evidence

When a buyer or bid team at a larger organisation shortlists suppliers, they may look at the supplier's website before or alongside a formal pre-qualification questionnaire. They are checking that the business is real, established, appropriately approved and capable of the scope being tendered. A thin or outdated site does not necessarily lose a bid, but it can raise questions that a well-organised site would have answered.

It helps to think about the site as a public summary of the evidence you would put in a bid:

  • Who you are. Company details, registered name, how long you have traded and the scope of work you take on.
  • Approvals and certifications. Only those you genuinely hold and that are current. In aerospace that might include AS9100 or specific customer approvals; in rail it might include RISQS registration or relevant product acceptance. Describe them accurately, and if you are unsure how a scheme may be referenced publicly, check its rules.
  • Capability statements. Clear descriptions of processes, materials, size ranges and inspection capability.
  • Policies. Health and safety, environmental, quality and modern slavery statements where relevant. Buyers frequently ask for these, and linking to current versions can save back and forth.
  • Contacts for commercial and technical questions. Bid teams appreciate knowing who to ask.

A downloadable capability statement, kept in step with the website, is often useful for buyers who need to attach something to an internal approval.

Long lifecycles: spares, obsolescence and through-life support

Trains and aircraft stay in service for decades. That creates a steady need for spares, repairs, re-engineering of obsolete parts, overhauls and modifications. For many Derby suppliers, this aftermarket and through-life work is as important as new-build contracts, yet their websites focus only on manufacturing capability.

If this is work you want, give it dedicated pages. Explain the kinds of problems you solve: reverse engineering a part where drawings no longer exist, finding alternatives for discontinued components, repairing rather than replacing assemblies, or supporting fleets through refurbishment programmes. Fleet engineers and maintenance planners search for these problems in plain language, so write pages that use the words they would use.

Be careful with specifics. Naming fleets, aircraft types or operators may be fine with permission, but many contracts restrict what can be said. Describe the type of work and the outcome in general terms unless you have clear agreement.

Document control on a public website

Rail and aerospace businesses live by document control internally, yet their websites often contain old brochures, expired certificates and data sheets with no revision information. For an audience that values traceability, that is an avoidable credibility problem.

Practical habits that help:

  • Show the issue date or revision on every downloadable document.
  • Replace files rather than adding new versions alongside old ones, and redirect old file addresses where they might be bookmarked.
  • Set a review date for certificates and policies so someone checks them before they expire.
  • Keep one owner responsible for website documents, ideally the same person who manages them internally.

A content management system such as WordPress can handle this well if the media library is organised and the site is set up so non-technical staff can replace a file safely. Our website maintenance guide covers keeping a site current over time.

Supplier and customer portals

Some suppliers want more than a brochure site: a secure area where customers can download controlled drawings, certificates of conformity or test reports, or where approved distributors can find pricing and stock information. This can be valuable, but it adds responsibility.

Before building a portal, consider whether your existing systems already provide a secure way to share documents, who will manage user accounts, and what the security expectations of your customers are. Some larger customers ask suppliers about schemes such as Cyber Essentials during onboarding; whether it applies depends on the customer and the contract, so check rather than assume. In many cases a simple, well-secured request route is more appropriate than a full portal at first.

Small specialist firms and consultancies

Not every Derby supplier is a factory. The rail and aerospace sectors also rely on small consultancies and specialists: systems engineers, safety assurance consultants, software developers, technical authors, trainers and inspectors. Their websites need to sell expertise rather than equipment.

For these firms, the strongest content is usually explanatory: what a particular type of assurance or analysis involves, when a client needs it, what the deliverables look like and who will do the work. Biographies of the people delivering the work, with their qualifications and professional memberships, matter far more than they do for a machine shop. Our engineering consultancy website guide goes into this in more detail.

Recruiting engineers and apprentices

Skilled people are in demand across the sector, and smaller suppliers compete with large, well-known employers. A careers section can help a smaller firm stand out by being specific: the type of work, the variety compared with a large organisation, training and apprenticeship routes, chartership support where offered, and honest information about hours and location.

Some roles may require security clearance or other checks. Say so where relevant, without going into detail that should not be public. Photography of real staff, with consent, does more than stock images ever can.

Search visibility for procurement-led businesses

Buyers in these sectors search for processes, standards and problems more than for company names. Dedicated pages for each capability, written with the technical vocabulary buyers use, give search engines a clearer picture of what you do. A page about a specific inspection technique or material, for instance, is more likely to be found than a single generic services page.

Local search also has a role. Some buyers prefer suppliers within a reasonable distance for visits, audits and quick turnaround, so a clear location, a complete Google Business Profile and consistent company details help. Redesigning an established supplier site

Many established Derby suppliers have websites that have grown piece by piece over many years. When redesigning, it is worth auditing which pages and documents bring in visitors and enquiries before deleting anything. Old capability pages can still rank for useful terms, and removing them without redirects can lose that visibility. Our guide to redesigning without losing rankings explains the process.

Photography that is cleared for publication

Workshop and test-facility photographs can accidentally reveal customer parts, drawings, screens or labels. Agree a simple clearance step before any image goes live, ideally signed off by whoever manages customer confidentiality, and keep a record of what has been approved.

FAQs

Do buyers really look at supplier websites during tenders?

Some do, particularly at the shortlisting stage. A clear, current site that matches what you say in a bid avoids raising doubts.

How should we show rail or aerospace approvals?

List only approvals you currently hold, describe them accurately and keep them up to date. Check any rules the scheme has about how it may be referenced.

Is it worth having pages about obsolescence and spares?

If that work matters to you, yes. Maintenance planners and fleet engineers search for these problems, and a general manufacturing page may not be found.

Should we put certificates and policies on the website?

Many suppliers do, with dates or revision numbers shown. Set review dates so expired versions are not left online.

Do we need a customer portal?

Not always. Consider security, account management and what customers actually need first; a simple request route may be enough to start.
