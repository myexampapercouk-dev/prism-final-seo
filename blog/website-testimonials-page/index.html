Website Testimonials: How to Collect, Display and Use Genuine Customer Feedback

How to collect genuine testimonials for your website, ask for permission, present them on a testimonials page and around your site, and stay within the rules.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Specific, attributable testimonials from real customers are far more persuasive than vague praise.
  • Ask at the right moment, make it easy and always get permission to publish names and details.
  • Never invent, edit the meaning of or selectively hide feedback; honesty is both a legal requirement and the whole point.

Testimonials are one of the simplest ways to show visitors that other people have trusted you and been glad they did. A few genuine words from a real customer can do more to reassure a hesitant visitor than any amount of carefully written sales copy. Yet many business websites either have no testimonials at all or show a handful of vague, anonymous quotes that persuade nobody.

This guide explains how to collect genuine testimonials, how to present them on a dedicated page and around your site, and the rules you need to follow to keep everything honest.

Why testimonials matter

Visitors to your site are usually trying to answer a few questions: can this business do what I need, will it be a good experience and can I trust them? You can make claims about yourself, but visitors naturally discount them. Testimonials are different because they come from people with nothing to gain from praising you.

They sit alongside other trust signals such as clear contact details, credentials and case studies. Together, they help a visitor feel confident enough to enquire or buy.

What makes a good testimonial

Not all testimonials are equally useful. Compare "Great service, highly recommend" with a quote that explains what the customer needed, what the experience was like and what difference it made. The second is far more persuasive because it is specific and believable.

Strong testimonials tend to have these qualities:

  • Specific: they mention a particular product, service or outcome.
  • Attributable: they include a name and, where relevant, a business, role or location, with permission.
  • Relevant: they address concerns your prospective customers actually have.
  • In the customer's own words: natural language reads as genuine.
  • Recent: current feedback reflects how you work now.

How to collect genuine testimonials

Ask at the right moment

Timing matters. The best moment is usually shortly after a customer has experienced the value of what you provide: when a project is signed off, a product has been in use for a little while or a customer has thanked you. Asking too early means they have little to say; asking months later means the detail has faded.

Make it easy

Many happy customers are willing to help but do not know what to write. Make the process as simple as possible:

  • Send a short, personal request rather than a generic mass email.
  • Offer a few prompts, such as what they needed, what the experience was like and what has changed since.
  • Let them reply by email, a short form or even a quick conversation you write up and send back for approval.
  • Keep it optional and do not chase repeatedly.

Use prompts, not scripts

Prompts help customers give useful detail without putting words in their mouths. Avoid sending a pre-written testimonial for them to sign off; it will read as artificial and undermines the honesty you are aiming for.

Use public reviews too

Reviews on platforms such as your Google Business Profile are another source of genuine feedback. Encourage customers to leave reviews there as well, and check each platform's guidelines on how reviews can be requested and reused.

Permission and accuracy

Always get clear permission before publishing a testimonial, and confirm exactly what you can show: full name or first name only, business name, job title, location and photo. Keep a record of that permission.

If you tidy a testimonial, for example to correct a typo or shorten it, do not change its meaning, and ideally send the final version to the customer for approval. Respect requests to remove a testimonial later.

Personal data rules apply to names, photos and other details, so handle testimonial information with the same care as any other customer data and check your privacy notice covers it.

Staying within the rules

Testimonials and reviews are covered by UK consumer protection law. Broadly, you must not publish fake reviews or testimonials, misrepresent who wrote them or present feedback in a misleading way, for example by only showing positive reviews while implying they represent all feedback. Incentivised reviews that are not disclosed can also be a problem.

The rules in this area have been strengthened in recent years and guidance continues to develop, so check the current position or take advice if you are unsure. The safe principle is simple: only publish genuine feedback from real customers, presented honestly.

That also means no placeholder testimonials during a website build that are left in place at launch. If you do not yet have testimonials, leave the space out rather than filling it with invented quotes.

Designing a testimonials page

A dedicated testimonials page gives visitors who want more reassurance a place to read further. To make it useful:

  • Open with context: a short introduction explaining what the page shows.
  • Organise by relevance: group testimonials by service, product type or customer type so visitors can find feedback that matches their situation.
  • Show attribution clearly: name, business or location and a photo if the customer has agreed.
  • Keep it scannable: highlight a key phrase from each testimonial, with the full quote beneath.
  • Link to more detail: where a testimonial relates to a case study, link to it.
  • End with a next step: a clear call to action to get in touch or browse services.

Video testimonials can be powerful because they are hard to fake and show real people, but only use them where customers are comfortable. A short, natural clip is better than a polished script.

Testimonials around your site

A testimonials page is useful, but many visitors will never visit it. Testimonials often do more work placed in context, close to the point where someone is deciding:

  • Homepage: one or two strong, broad testimonials to build early trust.
  • Service pages: feedback specific to that service.
  • Product pages: customer reviews that address fit, quality or use.
  • Landing pages: testimonials that answer the main objection a visitor might have.
  • Near forms: a short reassuring quote beside a contact or quote form.

Match the testimonial to the page. A quote about fast delivery belongs near delivery information; a quote about clear communication suits a service page for complex projects.

Testimonials and SEO

Testimonials are primarily for people rather than search engines, but they can help in supporting ways. Genuine customer language often includes the words and phrases prospective customers use, and fresh content keeps pages relevant. Be cautious with review structured data: Google has rules about which reviews are eligible for rich results, and self-serving reviews about your own business shown on your own site are generally not eligible. Check current guidance before adding markup.

Responding to less positive feedback

Not every customer will be delighted, and that is normal. If you receive critical feedback, treat it as useful information rather than something to hide. Where it appears publicly, a calm, constructive reply shows prospective customers how you handle problems, which can be reassuring in itself. Use the feedback to improve how you work, and never try to suppress genuine reviews.

Keeping testimonials fresh

Old testimonials can make a site feel neglected, and feedback about services you no longer offer can mislead. Build collecting testimonials into your normal routine, such as at the end of every project or after a set period following a purchase. Review the page from time to time and remove anything outdated.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Anonymous quotes such as "J, London" that look invented.
  • Generic praise that does not address real concerns.
  • Editing testimonials in ways that change their meaning.
  • Publishing without permission.
  • Leaving placeholder testimonials on a live site.
  • Hiding testimonials on a single page nobody visits.

FAQs

How many testimonials should a website have?

There is no set number. A few specific, genuine testimonials placed well are more useful than a long list of vague ones.

Can I edit a customer's testimonial?

Minor tidying is generally fine, but never change the meaning. Ideally, send the edited version to the customer for approval.

Do I need permission to use a testimonial?

Yes. Always get clear permission, including for names, photos and business details, and keep a record of it.

Can I use Google reviews on my website?

Many businesses display reviews from their Google Business Profile. Check the relevant guidelines and show them accurately, without implying they are a complete picture if they are not.

What if I do not have any testimonials yet?

Leave the section out until you do, and start asking recent customers. Never use invented or placeholder testimonials on a live site.
