Website Call to Action Buttons: Wording, Design and Placement

How to write, design and place call to action buttons on a UK business website so visitors know exactly what to do next, without pressure or clutter.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good call to action says what happens next in plain words, such as Book a call or Get a quote.
  • Give each page one main action and make it visually stronger than everything else on the page.
  • Place the button where the visitor has enough information to decide, not only at the very top.

A call to action button is the element on a page that asks a visitor to do something: get in touch, book, buy, download or sign up. It is often the smallest part of a page, yet it is the point where interest turns into an enquiry or a sale. When buttons are vague, hidden or competing with each other, visitors hesitate. When they are clear and well placed, the next step feels obvious.

This guide covers how to word call to action buttons, how to design them so they stand out, where to place them, and how to avoid the common traps that make a site feel pushy. It applies to service websites, online shops and landing pages alike.

What a call to action actually does

A call to action, often shortened to CTA, answers one question for the visitor: what should I do now? Every page on your site has a job. A service page should lead to an enquiry. A product page should lead to adding an item to the basket. A blog article might lead to a related guide or a newsletter sign-up. The button is how the page completes its job.

That means you cannot design a good button in isolation. Before you choose the wording or colour, decide what the page is for and who is reading it. A visitor comparing agencies wants different reassurance from someone who already knows they want to buy. If you have not yet mapped the purpose of each page, our guide to website navigation structure is a useful starting point.

How to word a call to action button

The words on the button matter more than its colour. Visitors scan, and a button label is one of the few things nearly everyone reads.

Say what happens next

Generic labels such as Submit, Click here or Learn more tell the visitor nothing about the outcome. Compare these:

  • Submit versus Send my enquiry
  • Learn more versus See our Shopify services
  • Click here versus Download the price guide
  • Buy versus Add to basket

The second label in each pair sets an expectation. The visitor knows what they will get and roughly what commitment they are making.

Match the commitment to the stage

Someone who has just landed on your homepage is rarely ready to sign a contract. A low-commitment action such as View our work or Read how we approach projects may suit them better than Book a consultation. Further down the page, or on a pricing page, a stronger action makes more sense. Think of buttons as a ladder of small steps rather than one big leap.

Use the visitor's language

Write labels from the visitor's point of view. Get my free quote often reads more naturally than Request a quote from us. Avoid internal jargon or clever wordplay that needs a second read. If a label needs explaining, it is not clear enough.

Keep it short

Two to five words is usually enough. Long labels wrap awkwardly on mobile and become harder to scan. If you need to add reassurance, put it in a short line of supporting text beside or below the button, for example No obligation, we reply by email.

Designing buttons that stand out

A button should look like a button. Visitors have learned what clickable elements look like, so avoid styles that blur the line between a link, a label and a decoration.

Contrast and colour

Your primary button should have the strongest contrast on the page. That does not mean it has to be red or orange. It means it should be clearly different from the background and from other elements around it. Choose a colour from your palette that is reserved for primary actions, and use it consistently across the site. Our guide to website colour and typography covers how to build a palette that supports this.

Colour contrast is also an accessibility issue. Text on the button must be readable against the button colour for people with low vision or colour blindness. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines set out contrast expectations, and our website accessibility guide explains why this matters for UK businesses.

Primary and secondary buttons

Many pages need more than one action, such as Book a call and View pricing. Make the hierarchy clear. The primary action gets the solid, high-contrast button. The secondary action gets a lighter style, such as an outline or a text link. If two buttons look identical, the visitor has to stop and choose, and some will choose neither.

Size and tap targets

Buttons need to be large enough to tap comfortably with a thumb, with enough space around them that visitors do not hit the wrong thing. This is especially important on mobile, where many UK visitors will first meet your site. See our mobile website design guide for more on designing for small screens.

States and feedback

A well-built button changes appearance when hovered, focused with a keyboard or pressed. After a click, it should show that something is happening, for example a loading state on a form submission. Without feedback, visitors click again and may send duplicate enquiries or orders.

Where to place call to action buttons

There is a popular idea that the main button must always sit at the very top of the page. That works when the visitor already understands the offer. When the offer needs explaining, a button at the top is useful as a shortcut, but the important placements are where the visitor has gathered enough information to decide.

  • Near the top for returning visitors or people who arrived ready to act.
  • After key sections, such as once you have explained a service, shown examples or answered objections.
  • At the end of the page, so a visitor who has read everything is not left at a dead end.
  • In the header, as a persistent but modest route to contact or the basket.

Repeating the same action several times down a long page is fine, as long as the wording and destination stay consistent. What causes confusion is a different action in every section.

Sticky buttons on mobile

On mobile, a slim sticky bar with one action, such as Call us or Add to basket, can help visitors act without scrolling back up. Keep it small so it does not cover content, and make sure it does not clash with cookie banners or chat widgets.

Calls to action on different page types

Service pages

The main action is usually an enquiry or a booking. Support it with a secondary path for visitors who are not ready yet, such as seeing examples of work or reading a related guide. Link the primary button to a short, focused form rather than a generic contact page where possible. Our guide to website contact forms covers how to make that form easy to complete.

Product pages

Add to basket should be the dominant element once a visitor has chosen options such as size or colour. Keep it close to the price, the variant selectors and delivery information, so the decision and the action sit together. Secondary actions such as Add to wishlist should be visually quieter.

Landing pages

A landing page built for a campaign should usually have a single action and minimal distractions. Removing the main navigation is a common choice for paid traffic landing pages, though it depends on your audience. Our landing page design guide goes into this in more detail.

Blog articles

Readers of a guide are often early in their research. A gentle action, such as a related article, a downloadable checklist or a soft link to your services, usually fits better than a hard sell. Put it where it naturally follows from the content.

Mistakes that weaken calls to action

  • Too many competing buttons. When every element shouts, nothing stands out.
  • Vague labels. Submit and Learn more give no reason to click.
  • Buttons that do not look clickable. Flat text with no visual cue is easy to miss.
  • Mismatched destinations. A button saying Get a quote that opens a general contact page breaks trust.
  • Pressure tactics. Fake countdowns or guilt-tripping decline links, sometimes called confirmshaming, can annoy visitors and may raise consumer protection concerns. If you use urgency, it should be genuine.
  • Ignoring keyboard users. Buttons should be reachable and usable without a mouse.

Testing and improving your buttons

You do not need complex software to improve calls to action. Start by watching how real people use your pages. Ask someone unfamiliar with your business to find out how to get in touch or buy a product, and note where they hesitate.

Set up conversion tracking in Google Analytics so you can see which buttons are clicked and which forms are completed. Once you have a steady flow of visitors, you can test variations of wording or placement, changing one thing at a time so you know what made the difference. Our guide to Shopify conversion rate optimisation covers testing in more depth for online shops.

Be wary of copying what another site does just because it looks polished. Your visitors, offer and brand voice are different. What matters is whether your own buttons are clear to your own audience.

FAQs

What is the best colour for a call to action button?

There is no single best colour. What matters is contrast with the background and surrounding elements, consistency across the site and readable text on the button. Reserve one colour for primary actions so visitors learn what it means.

How many calls to action should a page have?

Aim for one main action per page, with perhaps one secondary action for visitors who are not ready yet. You can repeat the main action down a long page, but avoid introducing a different action in every section.

Should the button say Submit on a form?

It is better to describe the outcome, such as Send my enquiry or Request my quote. This reassures visitors about what will happen and makes the form feel less formal.

Is a link the same as a call to action?

A text link can be a call to action, particularly for secondary or low-commitment actions. For the main action on a page, a styled button is usually clearer because it stands out from body text.

Do urgency messages on buttons cause problems?

Genuine urgency, such as a real sale end date, can be helpful. Fake or misleading urgency may damage trust and could raise concerns under UK consumer protection rules. If you are unsure, it is sensible to take advice or check guidance from the Competition and Markets Authority.
