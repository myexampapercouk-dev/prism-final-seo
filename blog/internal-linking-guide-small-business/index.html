Internal Linking for Small Business Websites: How to Link Your Pages Properly

Internal linking helps visitors and search engines find your most important pages. Learn how to plan links, write anchor text and fix orphan pages.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Internal links tell search engines which pages matter most and help visitors move from general information to the page where they can buy or enquire.
  • Plan links around a few priority money pages, link to them from relevant blog posts and service pages, and use descriptive anchor text rather than click here.
  • Review links whenever you publish, redesign or remove pages, and fix orphan pages and broken links so no useful page is left unreachable.

Internal linking for small business websites means connecting your own pages to each other with clear, relevant links so visitors and search engines can find your most important content. Done well, it helps your service and product pages rank, keeps people moving towards an enquiry, and costs nothing but a little planning.

Most small business sites grow in bursts. A few service pages at launch, then a blog post here and there, then a new product range or a location page. Without a plan, the links between them become patchy: new posts link to nothing, older pages are buried, and the pages that bring in revenue receive no support from the rest of the site. This guide explains how to put that right.

Why internal links matter for SEO and for visitors

Search engines discover pages largely by following links. A page with no internal links pointing to it may be found slowly, or not at all, even if it is in your sitemap. Links also pass signals between pages. When many relevant pages on your site link to your bathroom fitting service page, for example, that is a strong hint that the page is important and what it is about.

For visitors, internal links answer the question "where next?". Someone reading a blog post about choosing a boiler is a natural candidate for your boiler installation page. If the post does not link there, many readers will simply leave. Good internal linking is as much about conversion as about rankings.

It also supports newer kinds of search. AI-driven results tend to draw on sites that cover a topic clearly and connect related pages logically, which is part of what our guide to generative engine optimisation describes.

The main types of internal link

Link typeWhere it appearsWhat it is good for
Main navigationHeader menu on every pageReaching top-level services, shop categories and contact
Footer linksBottom of every pageSecondary pages, policies, locations
Contextual linksInside the body textStrongest relevance signal and natural next steps
BreadcrumbsAbove the page titleShowing hierarchy on larger sites and shops
Related content blocksEnd of posts or product pagesKeeping readers on the site
Calls to actionButtons and bannersMoving people to an enquiry or purchase

Navigation and footer links are set once and shared across the site, so they matter for structure. Our guide to website navigation structure covers menus in detail. Contextual links inside your content are where most of the day-to-day work happens, and they are the focus of the rest of this guide.

How to plan internal linking for small business websites

You do not need software to do this well on a site of a few dozen pages. A simple spreadsheet is enough.

  1. List every page. Export your sitemap or copy the list from your CMS. Note the page type: home, service, product, category, location, blog post, other.
  2. Pick your priority pages. These are the pages that make you money: core service pages, main shop categories, your pricing or booking page. For most small businesses that is somewhere between three and ten pages.
  3. Group pages by topic. Put each blog post and supporting page next to the priority page it relates to most closely. A post about garden drainage belongs with your landscaping service; a post on caring for wool jumpers belongs with your knitwear category.
  4. Map the links. Every supporting page should link to its priority page at least once, ideally early in the content where it fits naturally. Priority pages can link back to the most useful supporting articles.
  5. Add sideways links where they help. Related posts within the same topic can link to each other when a reader would genuinely want the second article.

This hub-and-spoke pattern is often called a topic cluster. It keeps relevance tight and makes sure the pages you care about receive the most internal support. If you are still deciding which topics to write about, our content strategy guide and keyword research guide help with that first step.

Writing good anchor text

Anchor text is the clickable wording of a link. It tells both readers and search engines what to expect on the other side.

  • Be descriptive. "Our emergency plumbing service in Leeds" is far more useful than "click here" or "read more".
  • Keep it natural. Write the sentence first, then link the words that best describe the destination. Forcing an exact keyword into awkward sentences reads badly.
  • Vary it a little. Linking to the same page with slightly different wording from different posts is normal and reads naturally.
  • Do not use the same anchor for different pages. If "kitchen fitting" links to two different URLs on your site, you are sending mixed signals about which page should rank.
  • Keep links accessible. Screen reader users often navigate by a list of links, so each one should make sense on its own. Our website accessibility guide covers this and more.

How many internal links should a page have?

There is no fixed number. A useful rule of thumb is that every link should help a reader. A blog post of around 1,500 words might sensibly include somewhere in the region of five to ten contextual links to related pages, while a short service page may need only a few. Pages crammed with links in every sentence become hard to read and dilute the value of each link.

More important than the count is the pattern across the site. Your priority pages should receive the most internal links from relevant content, and no useful page should receive none at all.

Finding orphan pages and broken links

An orphan page is a page that no other page on your site links to. It might be reachable through the sitemap or an old email campaign, but visitors browsing the site will never find it, and search engines may treat it as unimportant.

To find orphan pages on a small site, compare your full list of pages against the pages that appear as link destinations. A website crawler can produce the link list for you, and Google Search Console's links report shows which pages receive the most internal links. Pages near the bottom of that list, or missing from it, deserve a look. Our Google Search Console setup guide explains how to get access if you have not already.

Broken internal links are the other common problem. They usually appear after pages are deleted, renamed or moved during a redesign. Fix them by updating the link to the correct live page rather than relying on a redirect, and set up redirects for any old URLs that external sites or bookmarks may still use. Our guide to website redirects when relaunching covers that side.

Platform tips

Most platforms make contextual linking straightforward, but each has its own habits, so check the platform's current help pages for the exact steps.

  • WordPress: the block editor lets you search for existing posts and pages when adding a link, which reduces typos. Some SEO plugins suggest internal links as you write.
  • Shopify: product descriptions, collection descriptions and blog posts all accept links. Linking blog content to relevant collections is an easy win for many stores.
  • Website builders: link tools are usually in the text editor toolbar. Use the option to link to an internal page rather than pasting a full address where possible, so links update if a page is renamed.

A simple internal linking routine

Internal linking is not a one-off job. Build it into how you publish:

  • Every new post or page links to at least one relevant priority page.
  • After publishing, add a link to the new page from two or three older, related pages.
  • Each quarter, check for orphan pages and broken links.
  • Before any redesign or migration, export your links so nothing important is lost.

A periodic website content audit is a good moment to review links alongside the content itself.

When to get help

On a small site, internal linking is very manageable in house. It becomes harder on shops with hundreds of products, sites with many locations, or sites with years of unplanned blog posts. FAQs

What is internal linking for small business websites?

It is the practice of linking your own pages to each other so visitors and search engines can find important content. For small businesses, that usually means linking blog posts and supporting pages to the service, product or booking pages that generate enquiries and sales.

Do internal links help SEO?

Yes. They help search engines discover pages, understand what each page is about and judge which pages matter most. They are one of the few ranking signals entirely within your control.

Should internal links open in a new tab?

Generally no. Internal links usually work best opening in the same tab, which is what most visitors expect. New tabs are better kept for documents such as PDFs or for situations where leaving the page would lose a visitor's progress.

What is an orphan page?

An orphan page has no internal links pointing to it from elsewhere on your site. Visitors cannot reach it by browsing, and search engines may treat it as low priority. Either link to it from relevant pages or remove it if it no longer serves a purpose.

Is it bad to link to the same page many times?

Linking to a priority page from many different relevant pages is good. Linking to the same page several times within one article adds little, so one well-placed link per destination in each piece of content is usually enough.

Should I use nofollow on internal links?

Usually not. Nofollow on internal links rarely helps and can stop search engines following paths you want them to use. If a page should not appear in search results, a noindex instruction on that page is normally the better tool.
