Website Ownership and Handover: Domains, Logins, Code and Content

Who owns your website? A UK guide to domains, hosting, platform accounts, code, content and analytics, plus a handover checklist to agree before you start.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Paying for a website does not automatically mean you control every part of it, so agree ownership of domains, accounts, code and content in writing.
  • Keep key accounts such as the domain, hosting, platform and analytics in your business's name, with admin access you control.
  • A clear handover, with logins, documentation and backups, makes it far easier to change provider or bring work in-house later.

Many business owners assume that once they have paid for a website, they own it. In practice, a website is made of several separate pieces: a domain name, hosting, a platform account, design files, code, content, images, plugins or apps, and analytics. Each can be registered to, licensed by or controlled by different people. If those pieces sit in someone else's name, you may find it difficult to make changes, move provider or even keep the site running if a relationship ends. This guide explains what each piece is, who should control it and what a good handover looks like.

It is general guidance, not legal advice. Ownership of intellectual property depends on your contract and the specific circumstances, so take independent legal advice if you have concerns or a dispute.

Domains: the most important thing to control

Your domain is the foundation of your online presence. It points visitors to your website and usually routes your business email. If a former designer registered it in their own name, you may struggle to regain control. Make sure the domain is registered to your business, with an account you can log into, and that renewal reminders go to an email address you monitor. Our guide to business domain and email setup explains how domains and email fit together.

For UK domains ending in .uk, the registry has its own rules on who the registrant is. Check that the registrant details show your business, not your designer.

Hosting and platform accounts

For hosted platforms like Shopify, Squarespace and Wix, the platform account is effectively the website. If you are building on one of these, it is generally sensible for the account to be created in your business's name and paid for by you, with your designer given staff or collaborator access. That way, if you change designer, you simply remove their access.

For WordPress and WooCommerce sites, the hosting account matters because it holds the files and database. If your designer hosts the site on their own account, ask what happens if you leave: can they provide a full backup, and will they help you move it? Our guide to WordPress hosting covers what to look for in a hosting provider.

Some designers offer hosting as part of a package, which can be convenient. That is not necessarily a problem, but it is worth agreeing in advance how you would retrieve the site if you wanted to move.

Design, code and intellectual property

This is where ownership gets more nuanced. Under UK law, the person who creates a work is generally its first owner of copyright, unless they are an employee creating it in the course of employment or the rights are assigned in writing. This means that when you commission an external designer or agency, you do not necessarily own the copyright in the design or code just by paying for it. Your contract should set out what you get: an assignment of rights, a licence to use, or a mix.

In practice, many websites combine:

  • Bespoke work created specifically for you, which can often be assigned to you.
  • Themes, frameworks and plugins owned by third parties, which you use under their licence terms.
  • A designer's reusable tools or code, which they may licence to you rather than assign.

None of this is unusual. What matters is that it is written down and that the licence lets you use, modify and move the site as you reasonably need to. If the wording is unclear, ask for clarification before signing, and consider independent legal advice for significant projects. Our article on marketing agency contracts covers terms worth checking in any agency agreement.

Content and images

Text you write yourself is yours. Copy written for you by a designer or copywriter should be covered by the contract in the same way as design work. Images need particular care. Stock photos are licensed, not owned, and licences can be limited to specific uses or to the person who purchased them. If your designer bought images on their own account, ask whether the licence covers your business and whether you can continue to use them if you part ways. Photography commissioned for you should also be covered by a written agreement.

Analytics, search tools and tracking

Analytics data builds up over time and is valuable for understanding performance. Google Analytics properties, Google Search Console and tag management containers should ideally be created under your business's own Google account, with your designer or agency added as a user. If they were set up under someone else's account, ask for ownership-level access to be transferred. Our GA4 setup guide and Google Business Profile guide explain how these tools work.

Ecommerce: orders, customers and payments

For online shops, the platform account holds orders, customer records and often payment settings. Payment gateway accounts should be in your business's name, because funds are paid to that account. Customer data also brings data protection responsibilities: under UK data protection law, your business is usually the controller of its customers' personal data, and the Information Commissioner's Office provides guidance on what that involves. Make sure you, not a third party, hold the primary account.

A website handover checklist

Whether a project is finishing or you are changing provider, a handover should leave you able to run the site independently. Agree what will be handed over before the project starts. A checklist might include:

  • Domain registrar account access, with the business as registrant
  • DNS settings documented, or access to where they are managed
  • Hosting account access, or a full backup of files and database
  • Platform account ownership, with admin or owner access for the business
  • A list of themes, plugins and apps used, with licence details and renewal dates
  • Google Analytics, Google Search Console and tag manager access at the highest level
  • Copies of design files, if agreed in the contract
  • Source code or theme files for custom work, if agreed
  • Image and font licence information
  • Login details for any form, email or integration services
  • Brief documentation of how the site is set up and any custom functionality
  • Confirmation of when the designer's access will be removed

Store logins securely, using a password manager, and avoid sharing passwords over email. When someone leaves, change passwords and remove their user accounts rather than relying on them to stop logging in.

What to do if you do not control your website

If you discover that your domain or site is in someone else's name, start with a polite, written request to transfer ownership or provide access. Many issues are resolved this way, particularly if the original arrangement was informal. Check any contract or emails that set out what was agreed. For domains, registrars and registries have processes for transfers and disputes. If you cannot reach agreement, take independent legal advice rather than attempting workarounds.

Building ownership into a new project

The simplest way to avoid problems is to agree ownership at the start. Before signing, ask who will register the domain, which accounts will be set up in your name, what rights you receive in the design and code, and what a handover will include. FAQs

Do I own my website if I paid for it?

Not automatically in every respect. You may own some elements and licence others. Your contract should set out which, and you should control the key accounts. Take legal advice if ownership is disputed.

Should my web designer register my domain?

They can help, but the domain should be registered to your business, in an account you can access.

Can I move my Shopify store to a new designer?

If the Shopify account is in your name, you can remove one collaborator and add another. If it is in the designer's name, ask for ownership to be transferred.

What if my WordPress site is on my designer's hosting?

Ask for a full backup of files and database, and for details of plugins and licences. A new host or developer can then restore the site elsewhere.

Who owns the analytics data?

The account holder controls the property. Ideally it should be your business's Google account, with others added as users.
