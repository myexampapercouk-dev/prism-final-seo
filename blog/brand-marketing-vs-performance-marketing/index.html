Brand Marketing vs Performance Marketing: How to Balance Your Budget

Performance marketing drives measurable sales now; brand marketing builds the recognition that makes future sales easier. Learn how to balance the two.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Performance marketing targets an immediate, measurable action such as a sale or enquiry; brand marketing builds familiarity and preference over time.
  • Relying only on performance marketing can mean rising costs, because you keep paying to reach people who have never heard of you.
  • Most businesses need both, with the balance shifting according to their stage, sales cycle and how well known the brand already is.

Performance marketing is activity aimed at an immediate, measurable action, such as a click, enquiry or sale, while brand marketing builds awareness, trust and preference so people choose you when they are ready to buy. Performance marketing harvests demand that exists today; brand marketing grows the pool of people who will look for you tomorrow.

UK business owners often feel pushed to choose between them, especially when budgets are tight and every pound needs to show a return. In practice the two work best together. This guide explains brand marketing vs performance marketing, what each looks like, how to measure them and how to decide on a sensible balance for your business.

What brand marketing involves

Brand marketing starts with strategy: who you serve, what you stand for, why you are different and how you sound. Those decisions shape your identity, messaging and tone of voice, which then run through everything from your website to your ads. Our guide to brand strategy vs marketing strategy explains how the strategic layer differs from the plan for reaching customers.

The activity itself is broad. It can include video and display campaigns aimed at reach, sponsorship of local events, digital PR and media coverage, useful content that people remember, and a consistent look and voice across every channel. The aim is to create memories and associations so that when a need arises, your name comes to mind first or feels familiar on a results page.

Digital PR is a good example of work that does both jobs: it builds recognition and also earns links that help SEO. Our guide to digital PR and link building explains how.

What performance marketing involves

Performance marketing focuses on actions you can track and attribute. Common channels include Google search and Shopping ads, conversion-focused campaigns on Meta and TikTok, affiliate programmes and remarketing to people who have already visited your site. Budgets are adjusted based on results, often weekly, and success is judged by cost per lead, cost per sale or return on ad spend.

Its strengths are clear. You can see what works, cut what does not and scale winners quickly. That makes performance marketing appealing to small businesses and anyone who needs to justify spending. The catch is that it mainly reaches people who are already looking, and that pool is limited.

Why performance marketing alone tends to plateau

When a business relies only on performance channels, a familiar pattern often appears. Early campaigns capture the easiest customers, the ones already searching. As the business tries to grow, it has to bid on broader terms and colder audiences, where people do not know the brand and convert less often. Costs per sale rise, and competitors bidding on the same terms push them higher still.

Brand familiarity changes that picture. People who recognise your name are more likely to click your ad, choose you over an unknown competitor and search for you directly. Branded searches are usually cheaper and convert better than generic ones. In other words, brand marketing can make performance marketing more efficient, even though its own effect is harder to measure.

Why brand marketing alone is risky too

The opposite mistake is also common, particularly after a rebrand. A business invests in a new identity, a beautiful website and awareness campaigns, but has no plan to capture the interest it creates. People hear about the brand, search for it later and find a competitor's ad first, or arrive at a site with no clear next step.

Brand activity needs performance activity to turn attention into revenue: search campaigns that cover your brand and key services, landing pages that convert, and tracking that shows what happened. For smaller businesses with limited cash, some performance activity is usually needed to fund the longer-term work.

How to balance your budget

There is no single correct split. The right balance depends on several factors:

  • Stage of business. New businesses often lean towards performance to generate cash and learn what sells, then add brand activity as they grow.
  • Sales cycle. Longer, considered purchases benefit more from brand familiarity, because buyers research and compare over time.
  • Market competition. In crowded markets where click costs are high, brand recognition can be a real advantage.
  • Existing awareness. A well-known local business may need less awareness work than a newcomer.
  • Cash flow. If you need sales this month, performance has to come first, with brand work built in steadily.

A practical approach is to protect a performance budget that covers your core, high-intent searches and remarketing, then set aside a smaller, consistent amount for brand activity and review its effect over a longer period. Keep messaging consistent so both kinds of activity reinforce each other.

Whatever split you choose, write it down along with what you expect each part to achieve and when you will review it. Brand work in particular is easy to cut at the first quiet month, yet it only pays off when it runs consistently. Agreeing a review point in advance, such as every quarter, helps you judge it fairly rather than on a few weeks of data.

Channels that can do both

Some channels can serve brand and performance goals depending on how they are set up. YouTube can run reach campaigns that build familiarity or action-focused campaigns that drive sales; our guide to YouTube ads covers both. Meta and TikTok can run awareness or conversion objectives. SEO builds visibility for people ready to buy while also making your name familiar to researchers. What matters is being clear about the goal of each campaign so it is judged on the right measure.

FAQs

What is the difference between brand marketing vs performance marketing?

Brand marketing builds awareness, trust and preference over time, while performance marketing aims for an immediate, measurable action such as an enquiry or sale. Brand grows future demand; performance captures demand that exists now.

Is performance marketing better than brand marketing?

Not on its own. Performance marketing is easier to measure, but it mainly reaches people already looking. Without brand activity, costs often rise as you try to reach colder audiences who do not know you.

How should a small business split budget between brand and performance?

There is no fixed split. Many small businesses start mostly with performance to generate cash and learn, then add a steady, smaller brand budget. Review the balance as the business grows and as results come in.

How do you measure brand marketing?

Look at branded search volume, direct and returning visitors, reach, and whether conversion rates improve over time. These signals move slowly, so judge them over months rather than weeks.

Is SEO brand marketing or performance marketing?

It can be both. SEO captures people searching for what you sell, which is performance-like, and it also makes your name familiar to researchers, which supports your brand. Results usually take 3-6 months to build.

Do I need a brand strategy before running ads?

You can run ads without a formal brand strategy, but clear positioning and messaging usually make ads more effective. Even a short piece of work on who you serve and why you are different helps.
