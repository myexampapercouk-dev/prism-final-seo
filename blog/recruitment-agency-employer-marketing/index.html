Marketing for Recruitment Agencies: How to Win More Employer Clients

Practical marketing for recruitment agencies seeking more employer clients: positioning, sector pages, hiring content, LinkedIn, referrals and tracking.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Employer-focused marketing works best when an agency can clearly say who it recruits for, which roles it fills and why a hiring manager should choose it.
  • Useful sector pages, honest hiring content and a consistent presence on LinkedIn give employers evidence of knowledge before they make contact.
  • Track employer enquiries separately from candidate activity, because job applications can make marketing look busy while saying little about new client work.

Marketing for recruitment agencies aimed at employers should do three jobs: make clear which hiring problems you solve, show evidence that you understand that market, and make it easy for a hiring manager to start a conversation. In practice, that usually means a focused position, dedicated sector and role pages, useful hiring content, a consistent presence on LinkedIn, a planned approach to referrals, and tracking that separates employer enquiries from candidate traffic.

Recruitment agencies have two audiences, and candidate marketing often gets the most visible attention because job adverts need filling. This guide concentrates on the other side: winning and keeping employer clients. For a wider view covering both audiences, read our guide to recruitment agency marketing in the UK.

Why employer marketing deserves its own plan

Candidates and employers want different things. A candidate may be asking whether a job is right for them. A hiring manager is asking whether you can reduce a business problem: a role that has stayed open, a team that is stretched, or a market they do not know well. A single set of messages will rarely serve both.

Employer buying decisions can also involve more people. A line manager may spot the need, HR may run the process and finance may question the fee. Some employers will use preferred supplier lists or have procurement rules. Your marketing should therefore give different people the reassurance they need, not simply repeat that you are passionate about people.

Start with a position an employer can repeat

Generic claims are easy to ignore. Many agencies describe themselves as trusted partners who find the best talent, and that tells a hiring manager very little. A stronger position answers plain questions:

  • Which sectors do you know well?
  • Which types of role do you fill?
  • Which locations or markets do you cover?
  • Do you handle permanent roles, contract roles, temporary staffing or a mixture?
  • What is different about how you work?

Try writing one sentence that someone could repeat to a colleague. It should describe a real focus you can back up, rather than an aspiration. If your agency works broadly, you might still choose a small number of sectors to market first, since it is easier to create convincing content for a defined audience. For help thinking this through, see our guide to the difference between brand strategy and marketing strategy.

Build pages for employers, not just job listings

Many recruitment sites are organised around vacancies. That makes sense for candidates, but employers may struggle to find anything meant for them. Our guide to recruitment agency website design covers the wider site, and these are the employer-facing pages to think about.

A clear page for employers

Explain how you work: how a brief is taken, how candidates are assessed, how you keep clients informed, and what the next step looks like. Explain the fee model in general terms if you are comfortable doing so, or state that terms are agreed upfront. Clarity can filter out poor-fit enquiries and reassure good ones.

Sector pages

Each sector page should prove that you understand that market, covering the roles you fill, the hiring challenges you see and any relevant qualifications or checks. Avoid swapping one sector name into the same template. A hiring manager in that sector will spot generic text quickly.

Role and location pages, where justified

Pages for particular roles or places can help where you really work in that area and have something specific to say. Thin pages that simply repeat a template are unlikely to help anyone, and may weaken the quality of your site overall.

Evidence

Case studies and testimonials can be persuasive when they are genuine and permitted. Describe the hiring challenge, what you did and what happened, using only details the client has agreed you can share. Our guide to the website testimonials page offers practical advice. Avoid claiming placement or retention results you cannot substantiate.

A simple way to make contact

Give employers a direct route that does not begin with a candidate registration form. A short form asking about the role, location and timing may be enough, along with a phone number and named contact where appropriate. Our guide to website contact forms has more detail.

Show up for hiring-related searches

Employers looking for help may search for a type of recruiter, a sector, a place, or a hiring problem. SEO for this audience is similar to other business-to-business search work: find the terms your likely clients use, match each one to a useful page and strengthen those pages over time. Our guide to B2B SEO explains the approach, and our keyword research guide can help you begin.

Be realistic about competition. Large job boards and national firms can dominate broad searches, so narrower sector and location terms may be a more sensible place to start. Local visibility can also matter if you work with employers in a particular area, and our Google Business Profile guide covers the basics.

Publish content hiring managers can use

Good content for employers helps them make better hiring decisions, whether or not they use you immediately. Ideas include:

  • How to write a brief for a hard-to-fill role.
  • What a sensible interview process looks like in your sector.
  • Commentary on skills and availability, based on what you are genuinely seeing.
  • Questions to ask when choosing between recruiters.
  • Practical onboarding advice for new starters.

Salary guides and market reports can be useful, but only if the information is sound. Explain where your data comes from and how much of it there is, and do not present a handful of conversations as a national trend. If you discuss employment law, keep it general, note that rules can change and suggest that readers take professional advice. Our guide to content strategy can help you plan this work.

Use LinkedIn with a clear purpose

LinkedIn is an obvious place for recruiters, but activity alone does not win clients. Posting vacancies all day speaks mainly to candidates. To reach employers, consider sharing observations from your sector, explaining mistakes that slow hiring and showing how you approach difficult roles.

Encourage consultants to post in their own voice as well as using the company page, as people may respond more readily to someone they recognise. Keep messaging respectful, too. Copy-and-paste outreach is easy to spot and can damage the reputation you are trying to build.

Paid LinkedIn campaigns may be useful where you have a clear audience and a good reason for someone to respond, though it is worth testing carefully. Our guide to LinkedIn ads for B2B lead generation explains how to approach this.

Make referrals and repeat business deliberate

Existing relationships can be an important source of new work, yet they are often left to chance. A simple approach might include:

  • Checking in after a placement, at sensible intervals, to see how things are going.
  • Staying in touch when a hiring manager moves to a new employer.
  • Asking satisfied clients whether they would be willing to give a testimonial or an introduction.
  • Sending occasional useful updates, not constant promotion.
  • Reviewing past clients who have gone quiet and deciding who to contact.

Keep a record of this in your CRM so it does not depend on one consultant's memory. If a consultant leaves, the relationship history should not disappear with them.

Use outreach and email carefully

Direct outreach is part of recruitment, but it should be planned and lawful. Rules on electronic marketing and personal data apply in the UK, and how they apply can depend on who you are contacting and how you got their details. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance, and you should take advice if you are unsure.

The recruitment sector also has its own legislation governing how agencies and employment businesses operate, and advertising rules apply to your claims. This guide cannot cover those in detail, so check current requirements with your trade body or an adviser where needed.

Measure the employer side separately

Candidate activity can swamp your reporting. A jump in website traffic and applications may mean little for winning new clients. Set up separate tracking for employer actions, so that you can answer questions such as:

  • How many employer enquiries did the site generate?
  • Which pages and channels led to them?
  • How many became a live brief, and how many led to a placement?
  • Which sectors produced the most useful conversations?
  • How much fee income came from new, returning and referred clients?
StageWhat to look at
VisibilitySearches and page visits for employer-focused pages
EnquiryEmployer form submissions, calls and LinkedIn conversations
QualificationEnquiries that fit your sectors and terms
Commercial resultLive briefs, placements, fee income and repeat work

FAQs

How can a recruitment agency get more clients?

Begin with a clear focus, then give employers evidence through sector pages, case studies and useful content. Combine that with considered outreach and regular contact with past clients. No single channel suits every agency, so measure which ones produce real briefs.

Should employers and candidates have separate website sections?

In most cases, it helps. Their questions and next steps are different, so separate routes make it easier for each person to find what they need without wading through content meant for someone else.

Is content marketing worthwhile for a small agency?

It can be, particularly if you concentrate on a defined sector and share knowledge that comes from real conversations. A small number of well-considered pieces may serve you better than frequent generic posts.

Should we publish our fees?

That is a commercial decision. Some agencies prefer to explain how fees work without giving exact figures, since terms can vary by role and arrangement. Whichever you choose, make sure the process is easy to understand.

How long does employer marketing take to work?

It varies with your sector, reputation, competition and consistency. Direct outreach may start conversations sooner, whereas search visibility and reputation usually build gradually. Be wary of anyone promising a fixed result.

Next steps

Review your site as a busy hiring manager would. Can you tell within moments who the agency helps, and how to get in touch without registering as a candidate?
