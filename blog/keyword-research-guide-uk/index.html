Keyword Research Guide for UK Businesses: A Practical Step-by-Step Method

Keyword research means finding the searches your UK customers use, grouping them by intent and mapping each group to one page. Here is our step-by-step method.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start from your customers and your offer, not from a tool. Seed terms come from sales calls, enquiries and the words buyers actually use.
  • Group keywords by search intent and map each group to a single page, so you never have two pages competing for the same query.
  • Prioritise by commercial value and realistic difficulty, not volume alone. Low-volume UK long-tail searches often convert best.

Keyword research is the process of finding the words your customers type into search engines, understanding what they want when they type them, and deciding which page on your site should answer each one. For UK businesses, good keyword research starts with your customers and your offer, uses UK search data and UK spelling, groups terms by intent, and ends with a simple map of one primary topic per page.

The goal is not a spreadsheet of thousands of terms. It is a short, prioritised list of pages to create or improve, each with a clear target and the questions it must answer. This keyword research guide for UK businesses walks through the method we use, the free tools that are good enough to start, and the mistakes that waste the most time.

Why keyword research still matters in the age of AI search

Searchers increasingly get answers from AI Overviews and chat tools, and those systems break a question into several related sub-questions before answering. That makes keyword research more useful, not less. You still need to know the topics people care about and the language they use. The difference is that you now research the follow-up questions around each keyword too, so your page covers the whole topic well enough to be cited. Our guide to generative engine optimisation covers that side in more depth.

Keyword research guide UK: the step-by-step method

Step 1: Collect seed terms from real customers

Before opening any tool, write down how customers describe their problem. Look at enquiry forms, sales call notes, customer emails, reviews and the questions your team answers every week. You will often find that customers use different words from the ones on your site. A firm may call itself a "commercial property consultancy" while buyers search for "office space advice" or "lease renewal help".

Add your services or product categories, your locations if you serve specific areas, and the problems you solve. Aim for 20 to 50 seed terms.

Step 2: Expand the list with UK data

Use tools to grow each seed into related searches. Good free starting points include:

  • Google Search Console: the queries you already appear for, including many you never targeted. This is the most valuable source for an established site.
  • Google Keyword Planner: volume ranges and related terms, set to the United Kingdom.
  • Google search itself: autocomplete suggestions, the "People also ask" box and related searches at the bottom of the results page.
  • Google Trends: seasonality and regional interest within the UK.

Paid keyword tools add difficulty scores and competitor data, which help at scale, but they are not required for a small site. Whatever you use, set the location to the UK and check UK spellings: "optimisation" not "optimization", "colour" not "color", "trainers" not "sneakers", "estate agent" not "realtor".

Step 3: Work out the search intent

Intent is the reason behind a search. It decides what type of page can rank, and it matters more than volume. Search the keyword yourself and look at what already ranks. If the top results are all guides, a sales page will struggle. If they are all product categories, a blog post will struggle.

Step 4: Group keywords into topics

Many keywords mean the same thing. "SEO cost UK", "how much does SEO cost" and "SEO prices UK" should all be answered by one page. If the same pages rank for two keywords, they belong in the same group. Grouping prevents keyword cannibalisation, where several of your pages compete for one search and none ranks well.

Each group gets one primary keyword, the clearest and most searched phrasing, plus a set of secondary terms and questions that the page should also cover.

Step 5: Prioritise

Score each group on three things:

  1. Business value. How likely is someone searching this to become a customer, and what is that customer worth? A search with 30 monthly UK searches from buyers of a high-value service can beat a search with 3,000 from students.
  2. Realistic difficulty. Who ranks now? If the first page is all national brands and government sites, a newer site will need a long time and strong links to compete. Long-tail variations are often more realistic.
  3. Existing strength. If you already rank in positions 5 to 30 for a term, improving that page is usually faster than creating a new one.

Step 6: Map groups to pages

Build a simple keyword map: one row per page, showing the URL, the primary keyword, secondary keywords, intent and whether the page exists or needs creating. This becomes the brief for writing and the basis for SEO reporting later, because you can track each page against its target group.

Keyword research for local and ecommerce sites

Local businesses should research service plus location combinations, such as "accountant Bristol" or "dentist near me", and check whether the results show a map pack. Map pack results depend heavily on your Google Business Profile, so local keyword research and profile optimisation go together. Our local SEO guide explains how.

Online shops should focus keyword research on category and subcategory pages first, because these usually target the most valuable commercial searches. Product attributes such as size, colour, material and brand often reveal new categories worth creating. Our ecommerce SEO guide and our ecommerce sector page cover this in more detail. Paid search data helps here too: search terms reports from Google Ads show which queries actually convert, as explained in our PPC audit checklist.

Researching the questions around each keyword

Once each page has its primary keyword, list the questions a searcher is likely to ask next. For a page targeting "boiler repair cost UK", those might include how long a repair takes, whether it is worth replacing an old boiler instead, what affects the price and whether a landlord or tenant pays. Pull these from the "People also ask" box, from Search Console queries that include words like how, why, what and should, and from the questions customers ask your staff.

These questions shape the headings and FAQs on the page. Answering them clearly and directly helps the page rank for many long-tail variations at once, and it gives AI search tools short, quotable passages to draw on. It also stops you creating a separate thin page for every small question, which keeps your site tidy and your strongest pages stronger. A page that answers the main query in its first two sentences, then works through the follow-up questions in a logical order, tends to serve both readers and search engines well.

Common keyword research mistakes

  • Chasing volume. Big head terms are tempting but often too broad and too competitive to be worth it.
  • Ignoring intent. Trying to rank a service page for an informational query rarely works.
  • One page per keyword. Creating separate thin pages for close variations splits your strength.
  • Using US data or spelling. Global volumes and American terms can point you at the wrong words.
  • Researching once. Language and demand change. Revisit your map every few months using Search Console data.

FAQs

What should a keyword research guide for the UK include?

A useful keyword research guide for the UK should cover collecting seed terms from customers, expanding them with UK search data, checking intent, grouping related terms, prioritising by value and difficulty, and mapping each group to one page.

Can I do keyword research for free?

Yes. Google Search Console, Google Keyword Planner, Google Trends and the search results page itself are enough for most small sites. Paid tools help with scale and competitor analysis.

How many keywords should one page target?

One primary keyword plus its close variations and related questions. If two terms need different types of page, they belong on separate pages.

Are low-volume keywords worth targeting?

Often, yes. Specific long-tail searches tend to have clearer intent and less competition, and they frequently convert better than broad terms.

How often should I redo keyword research?

Review your keyword map every three to six months, using Search Console to find new queries and pages sitting just off the first page.

How long does it take to rank for new keywords?

For most UK businesses, SEO results typically take three to six months, depending on competition and the strength of your site. If you would like help building a keyword map, get in touch.
