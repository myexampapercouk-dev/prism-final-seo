Mobile Website Design: Building Sites That Work on Phones

How to design a website that works properly on phones: responsive layouts, thumb-friendly controls, mobile speed, forms, checkout and testing.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Design for the phone first, then expand for larger screens, rather than squeezing a desktop design down.
  • Speed, readable text and easy-to-tap controls matter more on mobile than decorative detail.
  • Test on real devices and real connections, not only by resizing a desktop browser.

Mobile website design means building a site that is easy to read, navigate and buy from on a phone, not just one that technically shrinks to fit. For many businesses a large share of visitors arrive on mobile, and Google primarily uses the mobile version of a site when indexing it. A site that frustrates phone users is leaving enquiries and sales on the table.

Responsive, mobile-first design

Most modern sites use responsive design: one set of pages whose layout adapts to the screen. "Mobile-first" takes this further by designing the small-screen version first and adding complexity as space allows. The advantage is discipline. When you only have a narrow column, you are forced to decide what really matters, and that clarity usually improves the desktop version too.

Separate mobile sites on their own subdomain are now uncommon and harder to maintain. If you still have one, it is worth considering a move to a responsive build.

Designing for thumbs

  • Tap targets: buttons and links need enough size and spacing that people do not hit the wrong one.
  • Reach: key actions near the bottom or middle of the screen are easier to reach one-handed than tiny icons in the top corner.
  • No hover: anything that relies on hovering, such as menus or tooltips, needs a tap-friendly alternative.
  • Sticky actions: a persistent call, book or basket button can help, as long as it does not cover content.

Readable content

Use a body text size that can be read without zooming, generous line spacing and short paragraphs. Break long pages up with clear headings. Tables are a common trouble spot: wide tables either need to scroll sideways inside their own container or be restructured into stacked cards on small screens. Check that images crop sensibly and that text over images stays legible.

Speed on mobile

Phones are often on slower or patchy connections, so page weight matters. The usual culprits are oversized images, heavy scripts, web fonts and third-party tools such as chat widgets and trackers. Practical steps include:

  • Serving appropriately sized, compressed images in modern formats.
  • Lazy-loading images further down the page.
  • Removing apps, plugins and scripts you no longer use.
  • Limiting the number of font families and weights.

Our Core Web Vitals guide explains the metrics Google uses to measure loading, responsiveness and visual stability.

Forms and checkout

Typing on a phone is slow, so every field costs more. Use the right input types so the correct keyboard appears, support autofill, keep forms short and show errors clearly next to the field. For online shops, offer express payment options where they suit your customers, allow guest checkout, and keep delivery costs visible early so nobody is surprised at the last step.

Things that annoy mobile users

ProblemBetter approach
Full-screen pop-ups on arrivalDelay them, make them small and easy to close, or drop them
Layout jumping as the page loadsReserve space for images, ads and embeds
Phone numbers that cannot be tappedMake them click-to-call links
Tiny close buttonsMake dismiss controls large and obvious
Auto-playing video with soundKeep video muted and user-controlled

Context: how people use phones

Mobile visitors are often doing something else at the same time: commuting, waiting, or comparing options between conversations. They may be in bright sunlight, using one hand, or on a weak signal. That context argues for pages that get to the point quickly, remember progress where possible and recover gracefully if a connection drops. It also explains why click-to-call, maps links and simple booking options are often used far more on phones than on desktop. Design for interruption, and make it easy for someone to pick up where they left off.

Accessibility overlaps

Many mobile improvements are also accessibility improvements: good contrast for outdoor glare, text that can be enlarged without breaking the layout, and controls that work with screen readers. Our website accessibility guide covers this in more detail.

Testing properly

Resizing a desktop browser is a useful first check, but it does not show how a page feels in the hand. Test on a few real phones, including an older or cheaper model, on mobile data as well as Wi-Fi. Complete your key journeys end to end: find a service, fill in the contact form, add to basket and check out. Your analytics can show which devices your visitors use and whether mobile visitors convert noticeably worse than desktop ones.

Prioritising content on a small screen

On a phone, content is read as a single column, so the order of things on the page becomes the order of importance. A desktop layout with a sidebar of key information can end up pushing that information to the very bottom on mobile. When planning a page, decide what a mobile visitor needs first and make sure the layout delivers it in that order. For a service business that is often what you do, where you work and how to contact you. For a shop it is usually the product, price, options and add-to-basket button.

Collapsible sections, such as accordions for FAQs or product specifications, can keep long pages manageable. Use them for secondary detail rather than essential information, and make sure the headings clearly describe what is inside.

Navigation on mobile

The collapsed menu behind an icon is now familiar to most people, but it hides your options, so the labels inside need to be clear and the list short. Consider:

  • Keeping the most important action, such as call, book or basket, visible outside the menu.
  • Limiting nested levels, because multi-level menus are fiddly on touch screens.
  • Making search prominent on shops with large ranges.
  • Showing a clear way back, such as breadcrumbs or a visible back link, on deep pages.

Images and media

Images that work at desktop width can lose their point on a phone if the important part is cropped away. Check focal points on key images, and consider using a different crop for small screens where the platform supports it. Product images should be easy to swipe and zoom. Video should be optional to play and should not consume large amounts of data automatically.

Mobile considerations for ecommerce

Shoppers on phones often browse in short bursts and return later, sometimes on another device. Features that help include a basket that persists between visits, easy account creation after purchase rather than before, and wish lists for people who are not ready to buy. Keep delivery and returns information close to the add-to-basket button, because mobile shoppers are less likely to go hunting for it. Make product options such as size and colour easy to select with large controls rather than small dropdowns.

Common mobile design mistakes

  • Designing and approving everything on a large monitor, then checking mobile only at launch.
  • Text set in light grey or thin fonts that become hard to read on a small, bright screen.
  • Horizontal scrolling caused by a single element that is wider than the screen.
  • Cookie banners and chat widgets that together cover much of the screen.
  • Form fields that zoom the page unexpectedly when tapped.

FAQs

What is the difference between responsive and mobile-friendly?

Responsive describes how a site is built, adapting one layout to different screens. Mobile-friendly describes the result: whether it is genuinely easy to use on a phone. A responsive site can still be awkward on mobile if it is not designed with phones in mind.

Do I need a mobile app as well as a website?

Most small businesses do not. A well-designed mobile website reaches people without a download. Apps tend to make sense when customers use your service frequently and need features a website cannot easily offer.

Does mobile design affect SEO?

Yes. Google mainly uses the mobile version of your pages for indexing, and page experience on mobile can influence how users behave once they arrive.

Should my mobile site have less content than desktop?

Generally the content should be the same, presented differently. Hiding important content on mobile can harm both users and search visibility.

How do I check how my site performs on mobile?

Use Google's PageSpeed Insights and Search Console for technical signals, then test key journeys yourself on real phones.

What screen sizes should I design for?

Rather than targeting specific devices, design layouts that adapt smoothly across widths, from small phones to large tablets. Check your analytics to see the most common screen sizes among your own visitors, and test those carefully.

Are pop-ups allowed on mobile?

Required notices such as cookie banners are fine when kept compact. Intrusive pop-ups that cover the main content can frustrate visitors and may affect how search engines view the page experience, so use them sparingly.
