Dance School Website Design UK: Timetables, Trial Classes and Parent Trust

Dance school website design guide for UK studios: clear timetables, trial class booking, safeguarding information, show pages and what to ask a designer.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most dance school visitors want three things fast: which class suits their child or level, when it runs, and how to book a trial.
  • A timetable that is easy to update and readable on a phone matters more than any visual flourish.
  • Parents look for reassurance on safeguarding, teacher qualifications and policies, so make those easy to find without overclaiming.

A dance school website has a simple job: help a parent or adult learner find the right class, see when it runs and book a first session without needing to phone. Good dance school website design puts the timetable, class levels and trial booking at the centre, then backs them up with clear information about teachers, policies and performances.

This guide covers what a UK dance school or studio website needs, how to structure it, which features are worth the effort and what to ask before hiring a designer. It focuses on the website itself. Wider promotion, such as local search and social media, is covered in our local SEO guide.

Who visits a dance school website

Before deciding on pages, it helps to picture the people arriving. A typical dance school site serves several groups at once:

  • Parents of young children looking for a first class, often comparing a few local schools on a phone in the evening.
  • Parents of existing pupils checking the timetable, term dates, uniform lists, exam information or show tickets.
  • Teenagers and adults looking for a style, such as ballet, contemporary, street, tap or ballroom, at a level that suits them.
  • Schools, venues and event organisers enquiring about workshops or performances, if you offer them.

Each group needs a different route through the site. New families need reassurance and an easy first step. Existing families need quick access to practical information. The design should serve both without burying either.

Make the timetable the strongest page on the site

For many dance schools the timetable is the most visited page. It is also the page most likely to go out of date, because classes move, fill up and change each term.

Readable on a phone

Wide grid timetables often break on small screens. A list grouped by day, or a filter by style and age, is usually easier to read than a large table that needs sideways scrolling. Our mobile website design guide covers this in more detail.

Easy for you to update

If changing a class time means emailing a developer, the timetable will drift. Ask for a timetable that you or your staff can edit in the content management system, ideally as structured entries rather than an uploaded image or PDF. Images of timetables are hard to read, cannot be searched and are not accessible to people using screen readers.

Clear status for each class

If a class is full or has a waiting list, say so on the timetable. It saves parents enquiring about places that do not exist and saves you answering the same message several times.

Trial classes and booking

The first class is usually the biggest step for a new family. The website should make it simple to take.

  • One clear call to action. A "Book a trial class" button on the home page, class pages and timetable gives people the same next step wherever they land.
  • Explain what happens. What to wear, when to arrive, whether parents can watch and how long the class lasts. Small details lower the barrier.
  • Choose a booking approach that fits. Some studios use an enquiry form and confirm by email. Others use an online booking or class management system. Both can work; what matters is that the process is clear and that someone responds.

If you already use a class management or booking platform, the website should link to it or embed it cleanly rather than duplicate it. Our online booking systems guide compares the main approaches.

Safeguarding, policies and parent trust

Parents leaving children with a teacher want reassurance. A dance school website can provide it by making relevant information easy to find, without making claims you cannot back up.

  • Safeguarding policy. Publish it or summarise it with a link, and name a contact for concerns if your policy designates one.
  • Teacher qualifications. Only list qualifications, memberships and checks each teacher actually holds. If you mention background checks, describe them accurately rather than in general terms.
  • Photography and filming. Explain how photos of pupils are taken and used, and how consent is gathered. Be cautious about showing children on the website at all without clear, recorded consent from parents.
  • Privacy. Enquiry and booking forms collect personal data, often about children. Your privacy notice should explain what you collect and why. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for small organisations, and it is worth taking advice if you are unsure what applies to you.

Requirements vary depending on how your school is set up, the venues you use and any organisations you are affiliated with, so check the position for your own studio rather than relying on a website template. Our trust signals guide covers how to present this kind of information well.

Class pages that answer the real questions

Thin class pages, with a style name and a stock photo, rarely help anyone decide. A useful class page covers:

  • Who the class is for, including age range and level.
  • What a typical session involves.
  • Whether the class works towards exams or performances.
  • When and where it runs, pulled from the timetable if possible.
  • How to book a trial.

Separate pages for each style and age group also give search engines something specific to rank. Someone searching for adult ballet classes in their town is looking for a different page from someone searching for toddler dance classes. Our website copywriting guide explains how to write pages like these clearly.

Shows, exams and the existing family experience

Existing families visit often, and their experience shapes word of mouth. A dedicated area for current pupils can save time on both sides.

  • Show information. Dates, rehearsal schedules, costume notes and ticket links in one place.
  • Exam information. If you enter pupils for graded exams, explain the process and timing without promising outcomes.
  • Term dates and closures. A simple, current list prevents a surprising number of messages.
  • Uniform and kit. What each class needs and where to get it.

If you sell show tickets, uniform or merchandise, a small shop or a link to a ticketing tool can sit alongside this. Keep it simple unless sales volume justifies more.

Photos and video

Dance is visual, and good images help. They also add weight to a page. Compress images properly and avoid autoplaying large video on the home page, especially for parents browsing on mobile data. Our image optimisation guide and website video guide cover the practical side.

Use photography you have permission to use. Rehearsal shots, studio spaces and adult classes can show the atmosphere without relying on images of children.

Local search and the website

Most dance school enquiries come from people searching close to home. The website supports this when it clearly states the venues you teach at, the areas you serve and the styles you offer, and when that information matches your Google Business Profile. If you teach at several venues, a page for each can help, provided each one has genuinely useful content rather than repeated text with a town name swapped. Platform choice

Many small dance schools start on Squarespace or Wix, and that can be perfectly adequate for a simple site. WordPress gives more flexibility for structured timetables, multiple venues and integrations. The right choice depends on who will update the site and how complex your timetable is. Our website builder vs custom website guide sets out the trade-offs.

What to ask a web designer

  • How will we update the timetable each term, and can you show us?
  • How will the site work with our current booking or class management system?
  • How will the timetable display on a phone?
  • How will you handle photos of pupils and consent?
  • What accessibility standard will the site be built to?
  • Who owns the domain, hosting and content once the site is live?

FAQs

Should a dance school put fees on its website?

It often helps, because parents compare schools and may not enquire if they cannot see an indication of cost. If fees vary by class or term, explain how they are worked out and link to your terms. Our pricing page design guide covers different approaches.

Is it safe to show children on a dance school website?

Only with clear, recorded consent from parents, and with care over names and identifying details. Many schools choose to use images of studios, adult classes or performances from a distance instead. Take advice if you are unsure about your obligations.

Do we need online booking?

Not always. A clear enquiry form with a quick response can work well for a small school. Online booking becomes more useful as class numbers grow and admin time increases.

How many pages should a dance school website have?

Enough to cover each style or age group properly, plus the timetable, teachers, policies, parent information and contact pages. Quality and clarity matter more than page count.

Can a new website help us appear in local search?

A well-structured site with clear class and location pages gives search engines more to work with, but rankings depend on many factors and cannot be guaranteed. Our SEO for small business guide explains what tends to matter.
