Photographer Website Design: From Portfolio to Booked Shoots

Website design for UK photographers: curated portfolios, service and package pages, fast image loading, client galleries, image protection and enquiries.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A photography website should be organised around the services clients book, such as weddings or headshots, not just one long gallery.
  • Curate tightly, compress images properly and test on a phone, because slow, oversized galleries lose visitors.
  • Get consent before publishing people, be clear about pricing and what is included, and make the enquiry step easy.

For a photographer, the website is the shop window. Visitors decide within seconds whether your style suits them, then look for what you offer, roughly what it costs and how to book. A beautiful gallery alone is not enough: if potential clients cannot work out whether you cover their date, location or type of shoot, they move on to the next search result.

This guide is about designing the website. For social media, directories, referrals and advertising, see our guide to photographer marketing in the UK.

Organise the site around what clients book

Many photographers work across several areas, such as weddings, families, newborns, corporate headshots, events or products. Each attracts a different client with different questions. Rather than one mixed portfolio, give each service its own page with its own gallery, explanation and enquiry route. That helps visitors see relevant work immediately and gives search engines a clear page for each type of shoot.

If one area brings in most of your income or is where you want to grow, make it the most prominent. A site that tries to give equal weight to everything can feel unfocused.

A structure that suits many photographers

  • Home: a strong selection of your best work and clear routes into each service.
  • Service pages: weddings, portraits, commercial or whatever you offer, each with a curated gallery.
  • Pricing or packages: either full prices or a clear starting point and what affects cost.
  • About: who you are, your approach, and what a shoot with you is like.
  • Client stories or real shoots: full sets from individual sessions, with consent.
  • FAQs: travel, turnaround, how images are delivered, what happens if it rains.
  • Contact or enquire.

Because the brand is "you", it is tempting to lead with an about page. In practice, visitors usually want to see the work first, then learn about the person behind it.

Curating the portfolio

A tightly curated portfolio is usually more persuasive than a huge one. Show the work that represents the shoots you want to be booked for, in a consistent style. Remove older images that no longer reflect your standard. For weddings and events, showing a full story from a single day can help couples picture their own, alongside a highlights gallery.

Order matters. Put your strongest, most representative images first on each page, and think about the flow, mixing wide shots, details and close portraits so the gallery is not repetitive.

Image quality versus page speed

Photographers face a real tension: images need to look excellent, but full-resolution files make pages painfully slow on a phone. The answer is not to lower your standards but to prepare images for the web. Export at dimensions suited to how they are displayed, use sensible compression, use modern formats where your platform supports them, and let galleries load images as the visitor scrolls. Test each gallery on a mobile connection, not just your studio broadband. Our guide to website image optimisation goes into more detail, and Core Web Vitals explains how speed is measured.

Avoid heavy effects such as autoplaying slideshows with music, or galleries that hijack scrolling. They tend to frustrate visitors and can make the site harder to use with assistive technology.

Consent and the people in your photos

Photographs of identifiable people are personal data, and using them to promote your business needs care. Have a clear written agreement, often in your booking contract, about whether you can use images on your website and social media, and respect clients who say no. Take particular care with children, newborns and sensitive settings, and do not publish images that reveal where someone lives or their children's school. Your privacy notice should explain how you handle client images and data under UK GDPR. The Information Commissioner's Office has guidance if you are unsure.

For commercial work, check whether your contract with the client allows you to show the images in your portfolio, and whether any embargo applies.

Pricing and packages

Clients often want a sense of cost before they enquire. You can publish full packages, "from" prices, or an explanation of what affects price, such as hours of coverage, number of edited images, albums, travel or usage rights for commercial work. Whatever you choose, be clear and accurate about what is included, and keep the page updated. Hidden extras that only appear later damage trust and may conflict with consumer protection rules on clear pricing.

Image protection: realistic expectations

Many photographers worry about image theft. Disabling right-click or adding heavy watermarks offers limited protection and can make the site feel less polished. More practical steps are publishing web-sized rather than full-resolution files, adding copyright information to image metadata, including a short copyright notice in the footer and acting on misuse when you find it. You own copyright in your photographs in most circumstances, but contracts can change that, so check your agreements.

Client galleries and print sales

Many photographers deliver images through a dedicated online gallery service, which often includes downloads and print ordering. You can link to it from your site or, with some platforms, integrate it more closely. If you sell prints, wall art or presets directly, a simple shop built with Shopify, WooCommerce or your website builder's ecommerce features may suit. Our guide to Shopify for art print sellers covers selling prints online in more depth.

The enquiry form

The enquiry form is where browsing becomes booking. Ask for the essentials: name, email, type of shoot, date if relevant, location and a short message. For weddings, asking for the venue and guest numbers can help you reply with a tailored quote. Keep it short, say when you will respond, and send an automatic confirmation so people know the message arrived. If you offer online booking for simpler sessions, such as headshots or minis, see our guide to website online booking systems.

Local and niche search

Most photographers serve a particular area. Mention the regions you cover on your service pages and in your footer, and keep your name, location and contact details consistent with your Google Business Profile. Avoid creating dozens of near-identical pages for every town nearby; pages for genuinely distinct areas or venues you have photographed at, with real images and useful content, are more helpful. Our local SEO guide covers the basics.

Choosing a platform

Squarespace and Wix are popular with photographers for their image-led templates and ease of use. WordPress offers more flexibility for blogs and custom layouts. Shopify suits photographers whose main business is selling prints or products. Choose based on how you will run the business and how comfortable you are updating it yourself.

Writing about your work

Photographers often let images speak for themselves, but a little text goes a long way. Short introductions on each service page explaining your approach, what a session involves and who it suits help visitors decide whether you are right for them, and give search engines something to understand. Blog posts or real-shoot write-ups about a particular venue, location or type of session can be useful too, as long as they are written for potential clients rather than stuffed with keywords. Add descriptive alt text to key images so they make sense to people using screen readers.

Testimonials and reviews

Kind words from past clients are persuasive, but only if they are genuine. Use real feedback with the client's permission, do not edit it in a way that changes the meaning, and do not invent or combine quotes. Linking to your Google reviews shows feedback visitors can check for themselves.

Common mistakes on photography websites

  • One huge mixed gallery with no clear services.
  • Full-resolution images that make pages slow on phones.
  • No indication of price or what is included.
  • Publishing clients' photos without clear permission.
  • Contact details hidden behind a long, complicated form.
  • Old work that no longer reflects your current style.

FAQs

Should I show prices on my photography website?

Showing at least a starting price or what affects cost helps clients decide. Be clear about what is included.

How many images should my portfolio have?

Enough to show your style and range for each service, carefully curated. Fewer strong images usually work better than many average ones.

Do I need permission to put clients' photos on my website?

Get clear permission, ideally in your contract, and take extra care with children and sensitive shoots.

How do I stop my images loading slowly?

Export at web dimensions, compress sensibly, use lazy loading and test on a phone.

Which platform is best for photographers?

Squarespace, Wix and WordPress are all common. Shopify suits those focused on selling prints.
