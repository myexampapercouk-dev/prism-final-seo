How to Prepare Your Website Content Before the Build Starts

How to prepare website content before a build: list your pages, gather words and images, assign owners, check permissions and keep a redesign on track.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 6 November 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • Website content delays are easier to avoid when one person owns the content and each page has a named supplier and a due date.
  • Start with a page list and a short brief for each page, so that you gather only what the site actually needs.
  • Check you have permission to use every image, logo and quote, and give your builder final wording rather than rough notes.

To prepare website content before a build, list every page your site needs, decide what each page must do, then gather the words, images and supporting details in one organised place with a named owner for each item. Doing this early means your designer can work with real material rather than guesses, and your project is less likely to stall while everyone waits for a page of text or a photo that nobody has been asked to find.

This guide is for UK small business owners who are about to commission a new website or redesign an existing one. It gives you a practical order of work, with a simple tracker you can set up in a spreadsheet. Our guide to choosing a web designer helps if you have not yet appointed anyone, and our website discovery workshop guide explains the planning that often happens first.

Why content should come early

Design and content affect each other. A page layout that looks tidy with placeholder text can fall apart when the real service description is three times as long, or when a team photo is landscape and the design expects a portrait. Starting with real content, or at least a realistic draft, gives you several benefits:

  • Better design decisions. Your designer can shape pages around what you actually have to say.
  • Fewer surprises. You find out early if you lack case studies, photos or product details.
  • Clearer costs. Knowing the number of pages and what needs writing makes quoting more reliable.
  • A smoother launch. Late content can hold a project up, so preparation protects your launch plans. Our guide to how long it takes to build a website covers the other factors.

You do not need every word finished before you speak to anyone. A web designer can help you plan the structure, and many offer copywriting or photography too. What matters is that you agree early who is producing what, and when.

Step one: choose one person to own the content

Content gathering tends to stall when it belongs to everyone. Choose one person to chase, collect, and sign things off. In a small business, this is often the owner or an office manager, and it does not mean they write everything. They simply keep the process moving and have the authority to make a decision.

Agree who else has to approve content, and keep that group small. If a partner, director or regulated professional must review certain pages, build their time into the plan. Sensitive claims in sectors like finance, health or law may need extra review, so allow for that at the start.

Step two: list every page

Start with a simple site map: a list of the pages you need, grouped in the way you expect visitors to look for them. If you already have a website, a review of what exists is the place to start. Our guide to the website content audit shows how to decide what to keep, improve, merge or remove.

A typical small business site may include pages like these, though yours should reflect how you actually sell:

  • Home page
  • A page for each main service or product range
  • About page
  • Contact page
  • Case studies, projects or testimonials
  • Frequently asked questions
  • Pricing, if you publish it
  • Blog or news, if someone will keep it updated
  • Legal pages, such as privacy, cookies and terms

Be honest about how much you can maintain. Ten strong pages are better than thirty thin ones that nobody has time to write. Our guide to website navigation structure can help you organise the list once you have it.

Step three: write a short brief for each page

Before anyone starts writing, answer four questions for each page:

  1. Who is this page for?
  2. What are they trying to find out or get done?
  3. What do you want them to do next?
  4. What information or evidence will help them do it?

A few sentences are enough. This stops pages from becoming a list of everything you could possibly say, and it gives your writer a clear target. If you are redesigning a site with search traffic, also note which existing pages already bring in visitors, so that you do not lose them by accident. Our guide to redesigning without losing rankings explains why that matters.

Step four: gather the facts

Much of the writing is easier once the raw facts are in one place. Pull these together even if someone else will write the final copy:

  • Business details. Your trading name, registered company details if applicable, address, phone number, email address, opening hours and service areas.
  • Services and products. What you offer, who it is for, what is included, how it works and any exclusions.
  • Evidence. Accreditations, memberships, awards, case studies and genuine customer feedback.
  • Answers. The questions customers repeatedly ask by phone or email. Your team will know these. Our guide to the website FAQ page shows how to use them.
  • Process. What happens after someone gets in touch, as this reassures people who have not bought from you before.
  • Legal notices. Any wording your regulator, insurer or professional body requires you to display.

Check that facts are current. An old website is often full of out-of-date team details, retired services and accreditation logos that have lapsed, and copying them across simply moves the problem to a new design.

Step five: write or commission the copy

There are three routes: write it yourself, hire a copywriter, or do a mixture, with you drafting and a writer refining. Each one can work. Your decision will depend on time, budget and how confident you feel. Our website copywriting guide explains what good copy involves, and our tone of voice guide can help you sound consistent.

If you write it yourself, a few habits help:

  • Open with what the visitor needs to know, not the history of the company.
  • Use the words your customers use, rather than internal jargon.
  • Use short paragraphs and clear headings, because visitors tend to scan.
  • Be specific where you can, and only say what you can stand behind.
  • End each page with a clear next step.

Be careful with AI-generated drafts and with text borrowed from competitors or suppliers. If you use a writing tool, a person who knows the business should check every claim. Do not copy another website's wording, as this creates both copyright and credibility problems. If you stock branded products, ask what the manufacturer's terms allow.

Step six: collect images and other media

Good images can do a great deal to make a small business look trustworthy, and they often take longer to arrange than people expect. List what each page needs, then work out what you already have and what needs creating.

  • Logo files. Ask for the original vector version from whoever designed it, not just a small image from your old site.
  • Photos of your team and premises. Real pictures tend to be more convincing than generic stock, particularly on an about page.
  • Work examples. Projects or products, in the highest resolution you have.
  • Video. Only if it serves a purpose. Our website video guide helps you decide.

Send originals rather than files that have been through a messaging app or pulled from social media, since these are often compressed. Your developer will prepare final versions for the web, as our website image optimisation guide explains.

Check you are allowed to use them

Do not take an image from a search engine and assume you can use it. For anything you did not create yourself, confirm the licence or written permission. A photographer normally keeps copyright unless your agreement says otherwise, so check the terms of older shoots. If identifiable people appear in photos, particularly staff, customers or children, get permission and keep a record. Data protection and consent requirements vary, so seek advice if you are unsure.

Apply the same care to testimonials. Use genuine customer words, get permission, and do not edit them in a way that changes the meaning. Our guide to the website testimonials page has more detail.

Step seven: organise it in a tracker

A shared spreadsheet and folder are usually all you need. Give each page a row with columns such as these:

ColumnWhat goes in it
PageThe page name, matching your site map
OwnerWho is responsible for supplying it
Copy statusNot started, drafting, in review or approved
ImagesWhat is needed, and whether it is ready
ApproverWho gives the final yes
Due dateWhen your builder needs it
NotesPermissions, redirects or anything to check

Keep one folder for each page and use clear file names, such as team-photo-sarah.jpg rather than IMG-final-new.jpg. Keep your latest version obvious. Ask your builder how they would like to receive content, as they may provide their own template or portal. If they do, use theirs instead of creating a competing system.

Step eight: proofread and sign off

Changing text is much easier before it is on the site than after it has been laid out across dozens of pages. Before handing content over, make sure that:

  • Someone other than the writer has proofread it.
  • Prices, phone numbers, addresses and links are correct.
  • Claims can be supported, and regulated wording has been checked where necessary.
  • The agreed approver has signed each page off.
  • You have marked anything that is deliberately temporary.

Late changes will happen, so agree with your builder how they will be handled and whether a certain number of amendments is included in the price. Your staging and testing phase is the right time to review everything in place, and you should still expect to spot things there that you missed on a document.

What to do when content is running late

Even a well-run plan can slip, and a busy owner often has customers to serve first. If you are falling behind, try these options before pushing back the launch:

  • Launch a smaller site. Publish the pages that matter for sales, then add the rest in planned stages.
  • Prioritise. Finish the service pages and contact details first. A company history can wait.
  • Interview rather than write. Talk through a service with someone taking notes, as this can be quicker than facing a blank page.
  • Bring in help. A writer or photographer can unblock a specific page or section.

Avoid launching with placeholder text, stock claims or sections that say coming soon, as they can make a new website look unfinished. It is better to leave a page out until it is ready. FAQs

Does all my content need to be finished before design starts?

Not necessarily. A clear page list, briefs and realistic drafts are often enough to begin. Ask your designer what they need and at what stage, and agree this in your project plan.

Can a web designer write my content?

Some can or will work with a copywriter, while others expect you to supply it. Check this when you get a quote, so it is clear who is responsible and what is included.

Can I reuse the content from my old website?

Yes, if it is still accurate and working well. Review it page by page, because old content may need updating, and be careful about removing pages that bring in search traffic without planning a redirect.

Can I use stock photos?

Yes, if the licence covers your use. They can work well for backgrounds or concepts, but real photos of your people and work are often more convincing where trust matters.

What format should I send my content in?

Editable documents for text and original high-resolution files for images are a sensible default. Your builder may have a preferred template, so ask first.

What about my privacy policy and terms?

These still need attention before launch. Our guide to website terms and privacy policies explains what to consider, and a legal adviser can confirm what your business needs.
