Tone of Voice Guide UK: How to Define and Write Your Brand's Voice

A tone of voice guide sets how your business sounds in writing. Learn what to include, how to create one and how UK businesses keep it consistent.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A tone of voice guide is a short, practical document that tells anyone writing for your business how it should sound, with examples.
  • Base your voice on your customers and positioning, describe it with a few clear traits and show what each looks like in practice.
  • Your voice stays the same everywhere, but your tone adapts to the situation, such as a sales page, a complaint reply or a social post.

A tone of voice guide is a short document that explains how your business sounds in writing, with clear traits, do and don't examples and guidance for different situations. For UK businesses, a good tone of voice guide makes websites, emails, social posts and ads sound like one confident brand, whoever writes them.

Most businesses have a voice whether they planned it or not. The problem is that it changes depending on who is writing. The website sounds formal, social posts sound chatty and sales emails sound like someone else entirely. This guide explains what a tone of voice guide should include, how to create one and how to make sure people actually use it.

Voice vs tone: what is the difference?

The two words are often used together, but they mean slightly different things.

  • Voice is your brand's personality in writing. It stays consistent. If your voice is plain-speaking and warm, it should be plain-speaking and warm everywhere.
  • Tone is how that voice adapts to the moment. You would not use the same tone in a celebratory social post as in a reply to a customer complaint, but both should still sound like you.

A good guide defines the voice and then shows how the tone shifts across situations.

Why a tone of voice guide UK businesses can use matters

A clear voice does several practical jobs:

  • Recognition: people start to recognise your writing, just as they recognise your logo.
  • Trust: consistency signals a well-run business.
  • Speed: writers spend less time guessing and editors spend less time rewriting.
  • Better briefs: agencies and freelancers can match your style from the start.
  • Differentiation: in crowded markets, how you say things can set you apart as much as what you say.

UK audiences also tend to respond well to plain English and are often wary of overblown claims. A voice that is clear and honest usually works harder than one full of superlatives.

What to include in a tone of voice guide

Keep the guide short enough that people will read it. Most effective guides fit on a few pages and cover the following.

How to create your tone of voice guide

1. Start with positioning

Your voice should reflect who you serve and how you want to be seen. If you are unclear on positioning, the voice will be too. Our brand strategy service covers positioning, messaging and voice together, and our guide to brand strategy agencies explains how those pieces fit.

2. Gather what you already write

Collect examples from your website, emails, proposals, social posts and ads. Mark the pieces that feel most like you and the ones that feel wrong. Patterns usually appear quickly.

3. Listen to customers

Read reviews, enquiry emails and notes from sales calls. Note the words customers use to describe their problems and your business. Using their language makes writing feel familiar and helps with search, because people search in their own words.

4. Choose three or four voice traits

Pick a small number of traits, such as clear, practical, warm and confident. More than four becomes hard to remember. Avoid traits every business claims, such as professional, unless you can say exactly what it means for you.

5. Define each trait with this, not that

For each trait, explain what it means and what it does not. For example, "confident, not arrogant" or "friendly, not over-familiar". This is the part of the guide writers use most.

6. Write before and after examples

Take real sentences from your materials and rewrite them in the new voice. Examples teach far better than descriptions.

7. Show tone by situation

Add short guidance for the main situations your business writes in: website pages, sales emails, customer support, complaint responses, social posts and ads.

8. Add style rules

Settle the small things: UK spelling, how you write prices and dates, whether you use the Oxford comma, how you refer to your business and capitalisation in headings.

Example: one trait in practice

Here is how a single trait might be described in a guide.

Clear, not clever. We say what we mean in plain English. We use short sentences and everyday words. We explain technical terms the first time we use them. We do not use jargon to sound impressive, and we do not hide the answer behind a clever line.

A before and after might look like this:

  • Before: "We leverage best-in-class solutions to drive synergistic growth outcomes."
  • After: "We help you get more enquiries from your website."

Tone of voice across channels

Your voice should be recognisable everywhere, but each channel has its own demands.

  • Website: clear, answer-first and easy to scan. Our guide to landing page design covers structure.
  • Blog and guides: helpful and thorough. See our guide to content marketing for planning.
  • Ads: short and direct, within advertising rules. In the UK, ads must follow the CAP Code, so avoid claims you cannot support.
  • Email: personal and relevant. Our guide to ecommerce email marketing has more detail.
  • Social: more relaxed, but still recognisably you.
  • Customer support: calm, helpful and human, especially when something has gone wrong.

Regulated sectors

If you work in finance, healthcare or law, your voice still matters, but it sits alongside compliance rules. A guide can set the style, but approval of customer-facing content should rest with your compliance team. Build those checks into your process rather than treating them as an afterthought.

Making the guide stick

Many tone of voice guides are written once and forgotten. To make yours useful:

  • Share it with everyone who writes for the business, including agencies and freelancers.
  • Keep it short and easy to find.
  • Include it in every content brief.
  • Review a sample of content every few months and update the guide when you spot gaps.
  • Collect good examples as they appear and add them.

Common tone of voice mistakes

  • Choosing traits nobody can disagree with. Words such as professional, innovative or passionate rarely help writers, because every business claims them.
  • No examples. A list of adjectives without before and after rewrites leaves too much to interpretation.
  • Forcing personality. Jokes and slang in every sentence can feel forced and irritate customers, especially when something has gone wrong.
  • Ignoring the reader. The guide should reflect how your customers talk and what they need, not just how the leadership team likes to sound.
  • Making it too long. A guide nobody reads does not change anything.
  • Never updating it. As the business grows or changes direction, the voice may need to evolve too.

Using your voice with AI writing tools

Many UK businesses now use AI tools to help draft emails, posts and web copy. Without guidance, these tools tend to produce generic text that sounds like everyone else. A tone of voice guide gives you something concrete to work with: you can include your voice traits, this-not-that definitions and before and after examples in your prompts, then edit the output against the guide.

Whatever tools you use, a person should still review the final text. Check that it sounds like you, that every claim is accurate and that it follows advertising rules where relevant.

FAQs

What is a tone of voice guide UK businesses should have?

A short, practical document describing how your business sounds, with voice traits, this-not-that definitions, examples, tone by situation and basic style rules.

How long should a tone of voice guide be?

Short enough that people will read it. A few pages with clear examples is usually more useful than a long document nobody opens.

What is the difference between tone of voice and brand voice?

Brand voice is your consistent personality in writing. Tone is how that voice adapts to different situations, such as sales or support.

How many voice traits should a brand have?

Three or four is usually enough. More becomes hard to remember and apply.

Does tone of voice affect SEO?

Indirectly. Clear writing in your customers' own words helps readers and matches how people search, which supports engagement and relevance.

Who should write the tone of voice guide?

Someone who understands your positioning and customers, often with input from leadership and front-line staff. An agency can facilitate and write it.
