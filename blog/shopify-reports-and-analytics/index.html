Shopify Reports and Analytics: What to Check and What It Means

A guide to Shopify reports and analytics for store owners: the overview dashboard, key metrics, useful reports, tracking differences and a review routine.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 6 November 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • Shopify analytics is the place to start for sales, orders and products because the figures come from your store's own records.
  • A metric on its own says little. Compare it with a sensible earlier period and look at what sits behind any change.
  • A short regular routine with a few agreed questions is more useful than checking every report whenever you feel worried.

Shopify reports and analytics show how your store is selling, where visitors come from and what they do, all from the Analytics area of your admin. The overview dashboard gives you the headline figures, while individual reports let you drill into sales, customers, products, marketing and inventory. The useful skill is not finding the reports. It is knowing which figures answer your question, and what they can and cannot tell you.

This guide is for UK store owners and small ecommerce teams who look at their numbers but are not sure what to do with them. It explains the main metrics in plain English, the reports that help with everyday decisions and why Shopify's figures may not match your other tools. Report access can vary with your Shopify plan, so you may not see everything mentioned here.

Where to find your analytics

In your Shopify admin, select Analytics. You will generally find three areas:

  • The overview dashboard. Cards showing headline measures such as total sales, sessions, orders and conversion rate.
  • Reports. A library of more detailed tables, grouped by subject, that you can filter, adjust and often export.
  • Live View. A real-time view of current visitors and activity, handy during a launch or a promotion.

Before you rely on any of it, check two settings. Your store's time zone determines when each day starts and ends in your reports, and your currency affects how sales are shown. Then set a date range and a comparison period. Almost every number is more helpful once it has something to be compared with.

The metrics that matter, in plain English

Several terms in Shopify sound alike but measure different things. Here are the ones you will meet regularly.

MetricWhat it tells youQuestion it helps answer
Gross salesThe value of products sold before discounts and returnsWhat was the demand at list price?
Net salesSales after discounts and returnsWhat was actually kept from products sold?
Total salesNet sales plus items such as shipping and taxHow much went through the store overall?
SessionsVisits to the store, with one person able to make severalHow much traffic came in?
Conversion rateThe share of sessions that resulted in an orderAre visits turning into purchases?
Average order valueThe typical amount spent per orderAre customers buying more or less each time?
Returning customer rateThe share of customers who have ordered more than onceAre people coming back?

Shopify's exact definitions are shown in its help documentation and can be updated, so check them if you are reconciling figures closely. One point deserves particular attention: total sales usually includes tax and shipping. If you quote it as revenue, you may overstate what the business earned. Net sales is often the better figure for trading discussions, and your accountant may want something different again, so ask them which figure to use.

Read metrics in pairs

A change in one number rarely explains itself. Looking at related measures together helps you find out what happened.

  • Sales are down, sessions are down. You may have a traffic question. Look at which channel fell before changing the store.
  • Sales are down, sessions are steady. Look at conversion rate and average order value. Then check for a stockout, a broken page or a change in the visitors you attract.
  • Conversion is down, sessions are up. This can be normal if a new campaign brings colder traffic. Check conversion by channel before assuming the site is at fault.
  • Sales are up, net sales are flat. Heavier discounts or more returns may be absorbing the growth.
  • Average order value is up, orders are down. A higher free delivery threshold or a price change could be behind this, so consider whether it is a good trade-off.

Compare like with like as well. A week containing a promotion will flatter or distort a comparison with an ordinary one, and a seasonal business may learn more from the same period last year than from last month, once it has that history. Keep a simple note of promotions, price changes, stock problems and site changes, as this makes later comparisons much easier to interpret.

Reports worth knowing

You do not need every report. These are useful places to start, grouped by the question you are trying to answer.

What is selling?

Sales reports can be broken down by product, variant, vendor or time period. Look beyond the top sellers: slow products may need better pages or less stock, and a high-selling product with heavy returns may be worth a closer look. If a best-selling variant keeps running out, see our guide to Shopify inventory management.

Where do visitors come from?

Acquisition and marketing reports show sessions by referrer and source, and sales attributed to marketing activity where the data is available. They help you see whether search, social, email or paid ads are bringing in visitors who actually buy. Compare channels by orders and net sales as well as sessions, since a large traffic source can still be a weak one for sales. Our GA4 setup guide covers more detailed analysis of traffic sources outside Shopify.

How are people behaving?

Behaviour reports can include things like conversion over time, sessions by landing page and top online store searches. Landing page data shows which pages create first impressions, while site searches with no results reveal products people expected but could not find. Our guide to Shopify search and discovery settings explains how to act on that.

Who is buying?

Customer reports can separate first-time and returning buyers and show customers over time. These are useful when deciding whether to spend effort on winning new customers or encouraging repeat orders. Our guide to Shopify customer segments shows how to turn that insight into groups you can reach.

What about stock?

Inventory reports can show stock levels, sell-through and how long stock may last. They help you plan reorders and spot money tied up in slow lines, and they are only as accurate as your stock counts.

What do I give my accountant?

Finance reports summarise sales, payments, tax and related items. Agree with your bookkeeper which report and date range they want. They may ask for payouts as well, because the money that reaches your bank differs from sales once fees and refunds are taken into account.

Customising and exporting reports

Most reports let you change the date range, add a comparison, apply filters and choose which columns or breakdowns appear. Depending on your plan, you can save an edited version so that you do not have to rebuild it each time. Name saved reports clearly so that other staff can tell what they show, and restrict access where appropriate. Our guide to Shopify staff accounts and permissions explains how to control who can see financial data.

Exports are useful when you need to combine Shopify data with costs or stock figures. Take care when exporting customer-level data, because it can contain personal information and should be stored and shared accordingly.

Why Shopify and other tools do not match

If you compare Shopify to Google Analytics or an ad platform, the numbers will rarely be identical. This does not automatically mean something is broken. Differences arise because:

  • Sessions are defined differently. Each tool has its own way of counting a visit and filtering out bots.
  • Consent affects tracking. Visitors who decline analytics cookies may not appear in some tools. Our cookie banner guide covers that side.
  • Attribution differs. An ad platform may claim credit for a sale that Shopify assigns to another channel, as each uses its own rules and time windows.
  • Time zones and dates differ. A sale near midnight can fall on different days if accounts use different settings.
  • Refunds and edits are handled differently. Shopify reflects changes to an order, while other tools may only record the original purchase.

As a working rule, use Shopify as your reference for orders and sales, and use other tools to explore traffic and behaviour. Watch the direction of each tool's trend as well as its absolute figure. If the gap between tools suddenly changes, then investigate, because that may point to a tracking problem. Our guide to website analytics for small business explains how the pieces fit together.

A simple review routine

Consistency beats intensity. Daily fluctuations can tempt you into changing things that do not need changing, particularly on a store with modest order numbers. A rhythm like the one below can be adapted to your size and trading pattern.

Each week

  • Check net sales, orders, sessions and conversion rate against the previous period.
  • Review top-selling products and whether they are in stock.
  • Note anything unusual and find out why before reacting.

Each month

  • Review sales and orders by channel, and compare them with what you spent on each.
  • Check landing pages that attract visits but few sales.
  • Look through site searches and returns for recurring themes.
  • Review the returning customer rate and your discount use.

Each quarter

  • Look at the range: which products to reorder, promote or retire.
  • Check that analytics and tracking are still working after any site changes.
  • Choose one or two improvements to test, based on what you have learned.

End each review with a decision, even if the decision is to change nothing. If a report never leads to an action, stop checking it. When conversion is the concern, our Shopify conversion rate optimisation guide sets out where to look next.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Reacting to small numbers. With few daily orders, one good or bad day can swing a percentage a long way.
  • Testing with your own traffic. Test orders and staff visits can skew figures on a new store, so bear that in mind when reading early results.
  • Chasing conversion rate alone. A deep discount can raise it while lowering profit.
  • Ignoring margin. Shopify's sales figures do not show your full profit unless costs are recorded and every expense is considered.
  • Treating attribution as fact. Marketing reports are a useful estimate of influence, not proof of what caused a sale.

FAQs

Are Shopify analytics accurate?

Sales and order figures come directly from your store's records, so they are a strong reference point. Traffic and attribution data are measured by tracking and rely on estimates, so treat them as a guide.

Do I need Google Analytics as well?

Many small stores can cover the basics in Shopify. A separate analytics tool can add detail about journeys and content, and it is worth setting up if you will actually use it.

Why can I not see certain reports?

Availability depends on your Shopify plan and on your staff permissions. Check your plan's current features and ask the account owner to review your access if needed.

What is a good conversion rate?

There is no single right figure, because it varies with products, price, traffic source and device. Your own trend over time, broken down by channel, is usually more useful than a benchmark from elsewhere.

Can I see profit in Shopify?

Shopify can show profit-related reports on some plans if you record the cost per item. That still excludes many business expenses, so use your accounts for true profit.

How often should I check my numbers?

A weekly look and a deeper monthly review suit many small stores, though busy trading periods may call for closer attention. Choose a routine you will keep up.
