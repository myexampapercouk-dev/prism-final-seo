Website Discovery Workshops: Getting a Project Right Before Design Starts

What a website discovery workshop is, what it can cover, how to prepare, and why agreeing goals and scope early helps a website project run smoothly.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A discovery workshop is a structured conversation that agrees goals, audience, content and scope before design or build begins.
  • Its value is in surfacing assumptions and disagreements early, when changing direction is cheap.
  • You get more from it by preparing data, examples and decisions in advance and bringing the people who will sign off.

A website discovery workshop is a focused session, or short series of sessions, held at the start of a website project to agree what the site needs to achieve, who it is for, what it must contain and what is in and out of scope. It turns a loose idea of "we need a new website" into a shared understanding that designers, developers and the business can all work from.

The format varies a great deal between agencies and projects. This guide explains what discovery can cover and how to get the most from it, whoever you work with.

Why discovery is worth the time

Most website projects that go wrong do so because of misunderstandings rather than technical problems: a stakeholder who was not consulted, a feature everyone assumed was included, content nobody owned, or a goal that was never written down. Discovery brings those issues to the surface before money is spent on designs that have to be redone.

It also makes quotes more reliable. Without a clear scope, any estimate is a guess. With one, both sides know what is being built.

What a discovery workshop can cover

TopicQuestions worth answering
Business goalsWhat should the website achieve? How will we know it is working?
AudienceWho are the main visitors, what do they need, and what stops them acting?
Current siteWhat works, what does not, and what does the data say?
ContentWhat pages are needed, what exists already, and who will write or update the rest?
Features and integrationsForms, booking, ecommerce, CRM, email, payments: what is essential and what can wait?
Brand and design directionWhich sites do we admire and why? Are brand guidelines in place?
SEO and migrationWhich pages bring traffic now, and how will redirects be handled?
ConstraintsBudget range, deadlines, platform preferences, approval process

Not every project needs every topic. A simple brochure site may need a single conversation; a complex ecommerce rebuild may justify much more.

Common outputs

Depending on the project, discovery might produce a written brief, a proposed sitemap, a prioritised list of features, notes on content responsibilities, and a list of open questions. What matters is that decisions are written down and agreed, so everyone can refer back to them. Ask whoever runs your discovery what you will receive at the end.

How to prepare

  • Bring the decision-makers. If someone will need to approve the final site, involve them now.
  • Gather data. Analytics access, top pages, enquiry or sales figures and common customer questions are all useful.
  • Collect examples. Note sites you like and dislike, and be specific about why.
  • Be honest about constraints. A budget range and real deadline lead to better recommendations than an open brief.
  • Think about content early. Content is often the slowest part of a website project. Our website copywriting guide can help.

Questions to think about before the session

Walking into discovery with some answers already drafted makes the conversation more productive. You do not need polished responses; rough notes are fine.

About the business

  • What do you sell, and which products or services matter most to the business right now?
  • What is changing in the next year or two, such as new services, new markets or a rebrand?
  • What has prompted the website project now?

About customers

  • Who are your best customers, and how do they usually find you?
  • What questions do they ask before buying or getting in touch?
  • What objections or worries come up most often?

About the website

  • What single action do you most want visitors to take?
  • Which parts of your current site do customers or staff complain about?
  • Who will update the site after launch, and how often?

Making decisions stick

A workshop is only useful if its conclusions are respected later. A few habits help. Agree who has the final say on each area, such as content, design and budget, before the project starts. Write decisions down and share them promptly so anyone who missed the session can raise concerns early. When new ideas arise mid-project, which they always do, compare them against the agreed goals and decide consciously whether to add them now, swap them for something else, or save them for a later phase.

This is not about being rigid. Projects should adapt as you learn. The point is that changes are deliberate and their effect on time and budget is understood, rather than drifting in unnoticed.

Prioritising features

Discovery often produces a long wish list. Sorting it is one of the most valuable parts of the process. A simple approach is to divide items into three groups: what the site must have at launch, what would be good to have if time and budget allow, and what can wait for a later phase. Launching with a focused, well-built site and adding features based on real usage is often more effective than trying to include everything at once.

Be wary of features included because a competitor has them. Ask whether your customers actually need them and whether you have the time and content to support them.

Discovery for redesigns

When replacing an existing site, discovery should look carefully at what the current site already does well, particularly in search. Pages that bring in traffic or enquiries need to be kept, improved or redirected properly. Our guide to redesigning without losing rankings explains why, and our website redesign cost guide covers budgeting.

Discovery for ecommerce

Online stores add questions about platform choice, catalogue structure, product data, payments, shipping, stock and integrations with other systems. Getting these right early avoids expensive changes later. Our ecommerce website design guide and Shopify vs WooCommerce comparison are useful background reading.

Discovery and content planning

Content deserves particular attention because it shapes the design rather than simply filling it. A page layout built around placeholder text often breaks when real copy arrives: headings turn out longer, sections are missing, or there are not enough images. Agreeing early which pages are needed, what each must say and who will produce it lets designers work with realistic material.

A simple content inventory helps. List every page on the current site, mark whether it should be kept, rewritten, merged or removed, and note any new pages required. Add an owner and a rough deadline for each. This one document often reveals the true size of a project more clearly than any feature list.

Signs you need more discovery

  • Different people in the business describe the project differently.
  • You cannot say what success looks like.
  • Quotes from different suppliers vary wildly, suggesting each has interpreted the brief differently.
  • Nobody is sure who will provide content or approve designs.

FAQs

What is a website discovery workshop?

A structured session at the start of a website project to agree goals, audience, content, features and scope before design and development begin.

Is a discovery workshop worth paying for?

For anything beyond a very simple site, clarifying scope early usually reduces costly changes later. Whether a separate paid phase makes sense depends on the size and complexity of the project.

How long does website discovery take?

It varies with the project. A small site may need one conversation; a large or complex build may need several sessions plus research.

Who should attend a discovery workshop?

The people who understand the business goals and customers, and anyone who will approve the final site.

Is a discovery workshop held in person or online?

Either can work. Remote sessions using video calls and shared documents are common and make it easier to involve people in different locations. What matters most is that the right people take part and have time to think, rather than where the session happens.

What happens if we change our minds after discovery?

That is normal. Discovery is not meant to freeze every decision. The written brief simply makes it clear what is changing, so the effect on timescales and budget can be discussed openly before work continues.

Do I need discovery for a small brochure website?

A small site still benefits from clear goals, a list of pages and agreement on who provides content, but this may take a single conversation rather than a formal workshop. Scale the effort to the size and risk of the project.

Can I do discovery myself?

You can do a lot of the groundwork yourself by writing down goals, audiences, required pages and features, and gathering data. That makes any conversation with a web designer far more productive.
