Google Ad Grants and Paid Search for UK Charities: A Practical Guide

How Google Ad Grants and paid search can work for UK charities: eligibility to check, account rules, campaign planning and when paid Google Ads may also help.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 7 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Google Ad Grants offers eligible charities free search advertising, but eligibility and account rules can change, so always check Google's current terms.
  • A grant account needs active management, because it must meet Google's programme requirements and works best with clear goals and useful landing pages.
  • A standard paid Google Ads account can sit alongside a grant where a charity needs more flexibility, but that spend needs a clear purpose.

Google Ad Grants is a Google programme that gives eligible charities in-kind search advertising on Google, so they can show text adverts without paying for the clicks within the programme's allowance. UK charities can use it to reach people looking for support, volunteering opportunities, information or ways to donate. Eligibility and rules are set by Google and can change, so you should always check the current Google terms for charities in the UK before you make plans around the programme.

This guide is for trustees, chief executives and fundraising or communications staff at UK charities. It explains what to check, how grant accounts tend to work and when ordinary paid search might also be worthwhile. For the wider picture of how search fits with other activity, see our guide to charity marketing in the UK.

What Google Ad Grants is

Ad Grants is offered through Google for Nonprofits. An accepted organisation receives a monthly allowance of in-kind advertising to use on Google search. It is a separate arrangement from a normal paid Google Ads account, and comes with its own rules. Google publishes the current allowance and conditions, so check them directly, since any figure you read in an older article may be out of date.

Your adverts appear in Google search results in much the same way as other text adverts. They do not guarantee a particular position, and paying advertisers may be shown ahead of you, so the grant is best viewed as a useful extra channel rather than a guaranteed source of traffic or donations.

Is your charity likely to be eligible?

Google decides eligibility, and the wording of its terms is what counts. As a general guide, organisations usually need to:

  • Hold valid charity status in the country where they apply, such as registration with the relevant UK regulator, or another status accepted by Google for that country.
  • Complete sign-up and verification through Google for Nonprofits.
  • Accept the programme's terms, including those on non-discrimination, donation use and appropriate advertising.
  • Have a suitable website with substantive content about their work, meeting Google's policy requirements.

Some kinds of organisations have historically been excluded, such as government bodies and certain hospitals and healthcare organisations, or schools and academic institutions, which may have separate routes. Charities with unusual structures, such as those exempt from registration, or those with links to public bodies, should check carefully rather than assuming either way. Rules differ by country and change over time, so the current Google for Nonprofits eligibility page is the place to confirm your own position. This is general guidance and not a statement of whether your organisation qualifies.

Getting ready to apply

Before you start, it helps to gather the basics, as this can make the process smoother:

  • Your registered charity details, exactly as they appear on the relevant register.
  • An organisational email address and details of who will manage the account.
  • A website that clearly explains your work, with accurate contact and charity information.
  • A named person to own the account, so that access does not disappear if a volunteer or staff member moves on.

Your website matters more than you might think. If it is slow, out of date or hard to use on a phone, advertising may simply send people to a poor experience. Our guide to charity website design covers what a good site should do, and it can be worth fixing the worst problems first.

How grant accounts work in practice

Grant accounts are not a matter of setting up and walking away. Google has placed programme requirements on these accounts, and these have included expectations about account structure, keyword quality, click-through rate and conversion tracking. Falling short of the requirements can put an account at risk of suspension. Because the details can change, check the current compliance guidance and treat the following as areas to look at, not as a definitive list.

Meaningful keywords

The programme has discouraged very broad or vague single-word keywords and those with low quality. Keywords should relate closely to what your charity does and what people are actually looking for.

Mission-focused adverts

Adverts should reflect your charitable work and lead to relevant pages. Commercial activity, such as promoting products or some kinds of fee-based service, may be restricted. If you have a trading arm or a charity shop site, check how the terms apply before including it.

Conversion tracking

You need to know whether the traffic does anything useful. A conversion might be a donation, a volunteer enquiry, a helpline page view, a guide download or an event booking. Decide what counts as a meaningful action for your charity, then measure it accurately and in line with your cookie consent and privacy obligations.

Regular attention

Someone should check the account on a regular basis, review search terms, remove keywords that perform poorly and update adverts as campaigns and appeals change. Accounts left alone can drift out of compliance without anyone noticing.

Planning campaigns around your charity's goals

A grant is most useful when it is built around clear goals. Think about the different reasons people might search for something connected to your work, then plan separate campaigns for each.

Be sensitive about wording. People searching for help may be in a difficult situation, so adverts and pages should be clear and calm, and should make it easy to find support quickly. If you run services for vulnerable people, consider whether emergency contact details should be prominent, and have the content checked by someone who knows the service well. Accurate, up-to-date information matters particularly for health, legal or financial topics.

Fundraising messages should also be honest about how money is used. The fundraising regulators and the advertising rules can both apply, so check the relevant codes. Our guide to landing page design offers general advice on making sure pages match what the advert promises.

Where Ad Grants can fall short

The grant is valuable, but it has limits you should plan around:

  • Search only. The programme is built around text adverts on Google search, so it does not cover every format that a paid account can use.
  • Limited demand. You can only reach people who are actually searching for relevant things. A small local charity may struggle to spend its full allowance, and that is not necessarily a problem.
  • Lower visibility on competitive terms. Paid advertisers can often outbid grant accounts, which may make it hard to appear on the most contested donation-related searches.
  • Time needed. The clicks may be free within the allowance, but management is not. It takes staff time, volunteer time or agency fees.

When paid Google Ads may also make sense

Some charities run a standard paid account alongside their grant. This can offer access to more formats and more flexibility, and may help you appear for searches that a grant account struggles to reach. Examples might include a major appeal, an emergency response, a regional event or searches for your own charity's name where others are bidding on it.

Paid spend should be held to a clear standard, because it uses money that donors expect to be used carefully. Before you commit any budget:

  • Decide what you want to achieve and how you will measure it.
  • Work out what an acceptable cost would be, taking into account the value of a donor or a supported beneficiary as well as a single gift.
  • Ask whether trustees should be involved in the decision, and record the reasoning.
  • Check how the two accounts will work together, including the separation Google requires between grant and paid accounts.

Our guides to Google Ads for small organisations and Google Ads costs can help you to think through a paid budget, while our Google Ads agency guide covers what to look for in outside help.

Who should manage the account?

Options include trained staff, a skilled volunteer or an agency. Each can work. Whichever you choose, make sure that the charity retains ownership of the account, that more than one person has access, and that the work is documented. If you use an outside supplier, ask how they keep up with programme rules and what their fees cover, since the management cost is real even when the clicks are funded by the grant.

Data protection also applies. If you are collecting enquiries or donations through your pages, your privacy notice and consent arrangements should reflect this. Take appropriate advice if you are handling sensitive information, such as details relating to health.

FAQs

Is Google Ad Grants really free?

The advertising is provided in kind within the programme's allowance, so you do not pay for clicks under the grant. You may still incur costs for staff time, website work or outside management. Check Google's current terms for full details.

Can any UK charity get Google Ad Grants?

Not automatically. Google sets the eligibility criteria and may exclude certain organisation types. Check the current Google for Nonprofits requirements for the UK and apply through its official process.

What happens if our account breaks the rules?

Google may suspend an account that does not meet its programme requirements, and you may need to fix the issues and request reinstatement. Regular checks against the current guidance help reduce this risk.

Can we use Ad Grants to promote our charity shop?

Possibly not in all cases, as commercial activity can be limited under the programme. Read the current policies carefully and consider how they apply to your specific activity before you create any campaigns.

Should we spend all of the allowance?

Only if it brings useful results. Spending the allowance is not a goal in itself. A smaller amount of relevant traffic that leads to real support or donations is more useful than a larger amount of poorly matched clicks.
