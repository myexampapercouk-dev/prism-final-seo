Event Planner Website Design UK: Portfolios, Enquiries and Client Confidence

Event planner website design guide for UK planners and event management firms: service pages, event portfolios, enquiry forms and what to ask a designer.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • An event planner website has to show taste and organisation at once: strong visuals backed by a clear process and practical detail.
  • Separate pages for each event type, such as corporate, private and charity events, help both visitors and search engines.
  • A well-designed enquiry form that asks for date, location, guest numbers and budget range saves time on every lead.

An event planner website needs to show two things at once: that you have good taste and that you are highly organised. Good event planner website design combines a strong visual portfolio with clear service pages, a straightforward explanation of how you work and an enquiry form that gathers the details you need to respond properly.

This guide is for UK event planners and event management businesses covering corporate events, private parties, conferences, launches and charity events. If weddings are your main focus, our wedding business marketing guide covers that market, and venues should see our wedding venue website design guide. Here we focus on the planner's own website.

What clients want to know

People hiring an event planner are usually trusting someone with an important day, a budget and their reputation. Before they enquire, they tend to want answers to a handful of questions:

  • Do you plan the kind of event I am organising?
  • Have you done something similar before, and what did it look like?
  • How do you work, and what exactly do you take care of?
  • Do you cover my area or venue?
  • Roughly what level of budget do you work with?
  • How do I start?

A strong event planner site answers these quickly, with the portfolio doing much of the persuading and the service pages doing the explaining.

Event type pages

A corporate client planning a conference has different concerns from someone planning a milestone birthday. Grouping everything on one services page forces both to read material that does not apply to them.

Separate event type pages let you speak to each audience directly. A corporate events page might cover delegate management, venue sourcing, supplier coordination and reporting. A private events page might cover themes, styling, entertainment and guest experience. Each can link to relevant portfolio examples.

These pages also help search visibility, because people search for specific event types, often with a location. Our website copywriting guide explains how to write pages that are specific rather than generic.

A portfolio that tells a story

Image galleries alone show what an event looked like but not what you did. A short write-up for each featured event makes the portfolio far more persuasive.

  • The brief. The occasion, the client type and what they wanted to achieve.
  • Your role. Full planning, styling only, on-the-day coordination, or something else. Be precise.
  • Key challenges. A tight timeline, a difficult venue, a large guest list.
  • The result. Described honestly, with images.

Permissions for images and names

Event photos are usually taken by a photographer, who typically owns the copyright. Check that you have permission to use images on your website, and credit photographers if that is agreed. Clients, especially corporate ones, may not want their name or event shown publicly, so ask before publishing. Guests appearing in photos also have privacy interests, so take care with close-up images of identifiable people.

Testimonials should be real, from real clients, with their permission. Our testimonials page guide covers how to present them well.

Explaining how you work

Many people have never hired an event planner before. A clear page explaining how working with you typically goes reduces uncertainty and makes enquiries more likely.

Describe your actual process honestly. It might cover an initial conversation, a proposal, planning and supplier booking, and the event day itself. Avoid promising services you do not always provide, and be clear about what is included and what is arranged separately. If you charge fees in different ways, explain the general approach and link to more detail rather than listing figures that may change.

The enquiry form matters more than usual

Event enquiries vary hugely in size and fit. A good form helps you respond quickly and qualify leads without endless back and forth.

Useful fields include:

  • Type of event.
  • Approximate date or date range.
  • Location or venue, if known.
  • Approximate guest numbers.
  • Budget range, offered as bands rather than an open box.
  • Which services they are interested in.
  • How they found you.

Keep required fields to a minimum so people are not put off, and explain in your privacy notice how you use the details. Our contact forms guide goes into more depth on balancing qualification and conversion.

Showing availability and coverage

Planners often cover a region rather than a single town. Make the areas you cover clear on the home page and contact page. If you regularly work at particular venues or in particular cities, location-focused content can help people searching locally, but avoid creating near-identical pages with only the place name changed. Our local SEO guide covers how to approach this.

If your diary fills up for certain dates or seasons, a short note on the site can manage expectations and encourage early enquiries.

Design: let the work lead

An event planner's website is itself a demonstration of taste. Some design principles help:

  • Restraint. Clean layouts let event imagery stand out. Busy designs compete with the work.
  • Consistent visual identity. Colours and typography that reflect your style. See our colour and typography guide.
  • Fast loading. Large image galleries are the most common cause of slow event planner sites. Compress images and use modern formats. Our image optimisation guide explains how.
  • Mobile first. Many people browse planners on their phones, often while comparing several options.

Supporting corporate buyers

Corporate clients may need things a private client does not: information for procurement, evidence of insurance, policies on data protection and sustainability, and a clear point of contact. If you work with businesses, consider a page that brings this together. Only list insurance, accreditations or memberships you actually hold, and keep them current.

Helpful content beyond the portfolio

Practical articles can support both trust and search visibility, provided they are genuinely useful and written from your own knowledge. Examples might include how far ahead to start planning a company conference, what to consider when choosing a venue for a large party, or questions to ask before booking entertainment. Content like this helps people early in their planning, before they are ready to enquire, and gives them a reason to remember you.

Keep advice general where rules apply, such as licensing, capacity or health and safety at venues, and point readers to the venue or the relevant local authority for confirmation. An FAQ page can also answer recurring practical questions, such as how far you travel, whether you work with a client's chosen suppliers and how changes to plans are handled. Our FAQ page guide covers how to structure one so it stays easy to scan.

Platform choice

Squarespace and Wix are popular with event planners because they handle visual layouts well, and they can be a good fit for a solo planner. WordPress offers more flexibility as portfolios, event type pages and content grow. Choose a platform you can update easily, because a portfolio that stops being updated loses its value. Our Squarespace vs WordPress guide compares the two.

What to ask a web designer

  • How will the portfolio be structured, and can I add events myself?
  • How will you keep image-heavy pages fast?
  • Can the enquiry form be tailored to the information I need?
  • How will event type pages link to relevant portfolio examples?
  • Who owns the site and domain when it is finished?

FAQs

Should an event planner show prices on their website?

Many planners explain how fees work rather than listing exact prices, because every event differs. A starting point or general approach can still help filter enquiries.

Can I use photographers' images of events I planned?

Only with permission. Photographers usually own the copyright, so agree usage and any credit before publishing.

Do I need separate pages for each event type?

If you plan meaningfully different events, separate pages usually help visitors find relevant information and give search engines clearer signals.

How many portfolio examples should I show?

Enough to show range and quality. A smaller number of well-explained events is usually more convincing than a long, unexplained gallery.

Is an event planner website different from a wedding planner website?

There is overlap, but corporate and private event clients have different concerns from couples, so the structure, tone and enquiry questions often differ.
