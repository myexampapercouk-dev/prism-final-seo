Website Video: When It Earns Its Place and How to Keep Pages Fast

When video genuinely helps a business website or online store, where to place it, and how to add it without slowing pages down or hurting usability.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Video helps when it shows something words and photos cannot, such as a product in use or a process explained.
  • Background and autoplay videos are the most likely to slow pages down and distract visitors, so use them sparingly.
  • Lazy loading, compressed files, poster images and captions keep video fast, accessible and useful.

Video can make a website feel more alive. It can show a product moving, explain a service in a couple of minutes or introduce the people behind a business. It can also make pages slow, distract visitors from the thing you want them to do and use up a large share of a mobile visitor's data allowance. The difference usually comes down to whether the video has a clear job and how it has been added to the page.

This guide covers when video is worth including on a business website or online store, where it tends to work, and the practical steps that keep it from dragging down page speed and usability.

Start with the job the video needs to do

Before filming anything, it is worth writing down in one sentence what the video should achieve. "Show how the folding mechanism works", "Explain what happens at a first consultation" and "Demonstrate the fit of the jacket on a moving person" are all clear jobs. "Make the homepage look more modern" is not, and videos added for that reason are often the ones that cause problems.

Video tends to earn its place when it shows something that text and still images struggle with:

  • Movement and use. How a product opens, folds, pours, fits or assembles.
  • Scale and texture. How large something really is next to familiar objects, or how a fabric drapes.
  • Processes. What happens step by step during a service, an installation or an onboarding.
  • People and places. A short look at a venue, workshop or treatment room can help visitors feel prepared.
  • Complex ideas. A short explainer can sometimes do the work of several paragraphs for a technical or unfamiliar service.

If the video only repeats what a good photo and a sentence already say, it is adding weight without adding much value.

Where video tends to work on a website

Product pages

For online stores, a short clip in the product gallery can answer questions shoppers would otherwise ask before buying. Keep it close to the photos so it feels like part of the gallery rather than a separate feature. Our guide to ecommerce product photography covers the still images that should sit alongside it, and a video should complement rather than replace those.

Service pages

A brief explainer near the top of a service page can help visitors understand an offer quickly. It works best when the page still makes complete sense without the video, because many visitors will not press play.

About pages

A short introduction to the business can add warmth to an about page. Keep it authentic and brief; a polished corporate film is not necessary for it to be useful.

Help and how-to content

Set-up guides, care instructions and troubleshooting walkthroughs are natural fits for video, and can reduce repetitive customer questions. Pair each video with written steps so people can skim or follow along at their own pace.

Homepages

Homepage video is where the most caution is needed. A large background video behind the main headline is a popular design choice, but it adds a heavy file to the most visited page on the site, can make text harder to read and rarely tells visitors much. A clear headline, a strong image and an obvious next step usually do more. Our guide to homepage design covers what that first screen needs to achieve.

How video affects page speed

Video files are large compared with text and images, and how they load matters a great deal. The main risks are:

  • Loading a video before it is needed. If the browser downloads a video file as soon as the page opens, it competes with everything else the visitor needs to see first.
  • Heavy embedded players. Players from video platforms often load scripts, styles and tracking code even before anyone presses play.
  • Autoplaying background video. These download and play straight away, often on mobile connections, and can become the largest element on the screen.
  • Layout shift. If space is not reserved for the video, the page can jump as it loads, which frustrates visitors and affects layout stability measures.

Google's Core Web Vitals look at loading, responsiveness and visual stability. A video that loads clumsily can affect all three. That does not mean video is bad for performance, only that it needs to be added with care.

Practical ways to keep video fast

Use a poster image and load on click

One of the most effective approaches is to show a lightweight still image with a play button, and only load the actual player when the visitor clicks. The page stays light for everyone, and the video is still one click away for those who want it. This is sometimes called a facade or click-to-load approach.

Lazy load videos further down the page

Videos that sit below the first screen do not need to load until the visitor scrolls near them. Modern browsers and most platforms support lazy loading for embedded content, and your developer can make sure it is set up correctly.

Compress and size the file properly

If you host video files yourself, export them at a sensible resolution for how they will be displayed. A clip shown in a small gallery box does not need to be a large, high-bitrate file. Modern formats and compression settings can reduce file size considerably while keeping quality acceptable.

Reserve space in the layout

Set the video's dimensions or aspect ratio so the browser knows how much room to leave before it loads. This prevents content jumping around.

Think carefully about autoplay

If a video must autoplay, for example a short silent loop showing a product in motion, keep it brief, muted and small in file size, and consider not loading it at all on slower connections or for visitors who have asked their device to reduce motion.

Hosting: self-hosted or a video platform?

There are broadly two ways to put video on a website.

ApproachStrengthsThings to watch
Embedding from a video platform such as YouTubeHandles streaming, different screen sizes and bandwidth automatically; videos can also be found on the platform itselfEmbedded players can be heavy; related videos and branding may appear; cookies may be set, which affects your consent setup
Self-hosting through your website platformMore control over the look of the player and what appears around it; Shopify supports product videos in the media galleryFile size and compression are your responsibility; large files can strain hosting on some setups

For short product clips, using your ecommerce platform's built-in media support is often straightforward. For longer explainers, a video platform embed with a click-to-load facade can be a sensible balance. If an embedded player sets cookies, think about how that fits with your cookie banner and consent approach; privacy-enhanced embed options exist on some platforms. The rules on cookies and consent can be nuanced, so check the guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office or take advice if unsure.

Accessibility and usability

A video that some visitors cannot use is a missed opportunity, and accessibility is also part of providing a fair service. Good practice includes:

  • Captions for any video with speech, so it works for people who are deaf or hard of hearing and for anyone watching with the sound off.
  • A text alternative such as a transcript or written summary for important information.
  • Player controls that work with a keyboard and are clearly labelled.
  • No unexpected sound. Videos should not start playing audio on their own.
  • Pause controls for moving content, and respect for reduced-motion settings where practical.
  • No flashing content that could trigger seizures.

Our guide to website accessibility covers these principles in more depth.

Video and search

Search engines cannot watch a video the way a person does, so the surrounding page matters. Give each video a clear title, a short description and, where helpful, a transcript on the page. Structured data for video can help search engines understand what a video contains; our schema markup guide explains how structured data works in general. Avoid putting important information only in a video, since visitors and search engines alike benefit from having it in text too.

Making video that does not need a big budget

Useful website video does not have to be expensive. Many effective product and how-to clips are short, simply lit and filmed on a modern phone with a steady mount. What matters more is clarity: a clean background, good light, a clear subject and a length that respects the viewer's time. Plan the shots in advance, keep each clip focused on a single point and trim anything that does not help.

If you do commission professional filming, ask for versions cut for different places on the site, such as a short loop for a product gallery and a longer version for a help page, so the same shoot serves several purposes.

Measuring whether video helps

Once a video is live, check whether it is doing its job. Useful signals include how many visitors press play, how far they watch and whether pages with video perform differently from similar pages without it. GA4 can record video engagement for some embedded players, and our GA4 setup guide covers configuring events. Keep an eye on page speed reports too, so you notice if a new video has slowed things down.

If a video is rarely played and adds weight, it may be better as a link to a separate page, or removed altogether.

Common mistakes

  • Large autoplaying background videos on the homepage with little purpose.
  • Loading full video players on page load rather than on click.
  • Important information that only exists inside a video.
  • No captions or text alternative.
  • Videos that play sound automatically.
  • Uncompressed files uploaded straight from a camera.

FAQs

Does video slow down a website?

It can, particularly if players or files load as soon as the page opens. Click-to-load players, lazy loading and compressed files reduce the impact considerably.

Should I use a background video on my homepage?

Usually it is worth questioning. A background video adds weight and can make text harder to read. A strong image and clear headline often do the job better.

Is it better to host video on my site or embed it?

It depends on the video. Short product clips often suit your platform's built-in media support, while longer videos may suit an embedded platform player loaded on click.

Do website videos need captions?

Captions are strongly recommended for any video with speech, both for accessibility and for visitors watching without sound.

Can video help with SEO?

Video can make a page more useful, which helps indirectly. Clear titles, descriptions, transcripts and structured data help search engines understand video content.
