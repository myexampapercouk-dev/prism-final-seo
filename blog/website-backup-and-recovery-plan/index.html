Website Backup and Recovery: How to Plan for the Worst

How to plan website backups and recovery: what to back up, how often, where to store copies, and how to test restores on WordPress, Shopify and more.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A backup only counts if you can restore it, so test restores rather than assuming they work.
  • Back up files and database separately from your host, keep more than one copy and store at least one away from the live server.
  • Hosted platforms such as Shopify, Wix and Squarespace look after the platform, but your own content and data may still need exporting.

Websites break. An update goes wrong, a plugin conflicts, a hosting account is suspended, someone deletes the wrong page or the site is hacked. When that happens, the only thing that matters is how quickly you can get a working version back. That depends almost entirely on decisions made long before anything went wrong.

This guide explains how to put together a simple website backup and recovery plan: what to back up, how often, where to keep copies and how to check that a restore actually works. It applies to small business sites and online shops on most platforms. For the wider picture of keeping a site healthy, see our website maintenance guide.

Backup versus recovery

A backup is a copy of your site. Recovery is the process of turning that copy back into a working website. A backup that has never been restored is an untested assumption. When the day comes, the files may turn out to be incomplete, the database missing, the copy months old or nobody sure how to use it.

So think of the plan in two halves: making reliable copies, and being able to use them under pressure.

Two questions to answer first

Before choosing tools or settings, answer two questions for your own business:

  • How much data can you afford to lose? If your site changes once a month, a weekly backup is plenty. If you take orders or bookings every hour, losing a day of data could mean lost orders and confused customers. This tells you how often to back up.
  • How long can you afford to be offline? A brochure site being down for a day is a nuisance. A shop being down on a busy weekend is lost revenue. This tells you how much effort to put into making restores fast.

Write your answers down. They shape everything else in the plan.

What needs backing up

What you back up depends on how your site is built.

Self-hosted sites (WordPress, WooCommerce and similar)

A WordPress site has two parts, and you need both:

  • Files: WordPress itself, your theme, plugins and the uploads folder holding images and documents.
  • Database: pages, posts, settings, users and, on a WooCommerce shop, orders and customer records.

A copy of the files without the database, or the other way round, will not restore a working site. Also keep a note of anything set up outside WordPress, such as server configuration, redirects at host level and scheduled tasks.

Hosted platforms (Shopify, Wix, Squarespace)

On hosted platforms, the provider runs the servers and the software. You do not need to back up the platform itself. That does not mean all of your work is protected from every kind of mistake. If a member of staff deletes products, overwrites a theme or a third-party app changes data in bulk, the platform may not offer a simple way to roll back that specific change.

Practical steps on hosted platforms include:

  • Exporting products, customers and orders regularly using the platform's built-in export features.
  • Duplicating your theme before making changes. On Shopify, an unpublished copy gives you a fallback you can republish.
  • Keeping your own copies of images, product copy and page content.
  • Checking what each platform's own help documentation says about restoring deleted content, as this differs between platforms and changes over time.

Things that are easy to forget

  • Domain and DNS settings. Record where your domain is registered and what DNS records exist, including email records. Rebuilding these from memory is slow.
  • Email. If your mailboxes are on the same hosting account as your website, they may not be included in a website backup.
  • Logins and ownership. Know who holds the accounts for hosting, domain and platform. Our ownership and handover guide explains why these should be in the business's name.
  • Licences. Premium themes and plugins may need licence keys to be reactivated after a restore.

How often to back up

Match frequency to how often the site changes and how much loss you can tolerate.

Type of siteChangesSensible starting point
Brochure site, rarely updatedMonthly or lessWeekly, plus before any update
Site with regular blog postsWeeklyDaily database, weekly files
Shop or booking siteMany times a dayFrequent database backups, daily files, plus before any change

Whatever the schedule, always take a fresh backup immediately before updating WordPress, plugins or themes, changing hosting, or making significant design changes. That single habit avoids a great deal of pain.

Where to keep backups

A backup stored only on the same server as the website is fragile. If the server fails, the account is suspended or the site is compromised, the backup may go with it. A widely used rule of thumb is the 3-2-1 approach:

  • 3 copies of your data (the live site plus two backups).
  • 2 different storage locations or types.
  • 1 copy kept away from your hosting provider altogether.

Hosting providers often include backups, which are useful, but they are not a complete plan on their own. Check how long the host keeps them, whether you can download them, and whether restoring costs extra. Keeping a separate copy under your own control means you are not dependent on one company for recovery.

Also think about how long to keep backups. If a site is hacked quietly, the problem may not be noticed for weeks. Keeping older copies as well as recent ones gives you a better chance of finding a clean version.

Security and personal data in backups

Backups of a shop or a site with user accounts contain personal data such as names, addresses and order history. Under UK data protection law, that data generally needs the same care as the live site. Store backups securely, restrict who can access them, and do not keep them longer than you need. The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) publishes guidance on security and retention; check it, or take advice, if you are unsure how it applies to you.

Test your restores

This is the step that is easiest to skip. A restore test means taking a backup and turning it into a working copy of the site somewhere safe, such as a staging site, and checking it works. Look for:

  • Pages load and look correct.
  • Images and downloads are present.
  • Forms send, and on a shop, test orders complete.
  • Logins work.
  • Recent content you expected to see is there.

Doing this a few times a year, and after changing hosting or backup tools, means you will know the process before you need it in an emergency.

Write a short recovery runbook

When a site goes down, people panic and forget steps. A one-page runbook helps. It should cover:

  1. Who to contact first, and how (the person or company who looks after the site, the host).
  2. Where backups are stored and how to access them.
  3. The steps to restore, written plainly.
  4. How to put up a holding page if the restore will take a while.
  5. What to check once the site is back.
  6. Who needs telling, including customers if orders or data are affected.

Keep the runbook somewhere that does not depend on the website or its hosting being online.

When the cause is a hack

If a site has been compromised, restoring the latest backup may simply restore the problem. The backup itself may contain the malicious code, and the weakness that let it in, such as an outdated plugin or a weak password, will still be there. The usual approach is to identify how the attacker got in, restore from a backup taken before the compromise, update everything, change passwords and then monitor closely. If personal data may have been accessed, you may have reporting obligations; the ICO's guidance on personal data breaches explains when. Our WordPress security guide covers prevention.

A simple checklist

  • Decide how much data loss and downtime you can tolerate.
  • Back up files and database (self-hosted) or export content and data (hosted platforms).
  • Set a schedule, plus a manual backup before any change.
  • Keep at least one copy away from your host.
  • Protect backups that contain personal data.
  • Test a restore a few times a year.
  • Write and store a recovery runbook.

FAQs

Does my hosting company back up my website?

Many hosts include backups, but the frequency, retention period and restore process vary. Check the details, and keep at least one copy under your own control.

Does Shopify back up my store?

Shopify runs and maintains the platform. Whether you can easily undo your own mistakes, such as deleted products or overwritten themes, is a different question, so export data regularly and duplicate themes before editing.

How often should I back up a WordPress site?

It depends on how often it changes. A rarely updated site might be backed up weekly; a busy shop needs much more frequent database backups. Always back up before updates.

Where should I store website backups?

In more than one place, with at least one copy kept away from your hosting provider, so a problem with the host does not take your backups with it.

How do I know my backups work?

Restore one to a staging site and check pages, images, forms, logins and recent content. Until you have done that, you cannot be sure.
