Florist Website Design: Selling Flowers Online Locally

Website design for UK florists: online ordering, delivery areas and dates, gift messages, wedding and event enquiries, seasonal stock and mobile checkout.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A florist website is mostly a delivery shop: make postcode coverage, delivery dates and cut-off times clear before checkout.
  • Design product pages for natural variation, with honest wording about seasonal substitutions and what the customer will receive.
  • Separate quick gift orders from wedding and event enquiries, which need a consultation-style route rather than a basket.

Most people who visit a florist's website want to send flowers to someone, often for a specific date, sometimes at the last minute, usually on a phone. A florist website therefore works more like a local delivery shop than a brochure. If customers cannot quickly tell whether you deliver to their recipient's postcode on the day they need, they will order elsewhere. At the same time, weddings, funerals and events bring a different kind of customer who needs a conversation, not a basket.

This guide covers the website itself. For local search, social media, seasonal campaigns and wider promotion, see our guide to florist marketing in the UK.

Two kinds of customer, two journeys

Florist sites usually serve two very different groups:

  • Gift buyers, ordering bouquets for birthdays, anniversaries, sympathy or thank-yous, who want a quick, reliable checkout.
  • Event clients, planning weddings, funerals or corporate flowers, who want to see your style, discuss options and get a quote.

Design separate routes for each. A clear "Shop flowers" route leads to products and checkout; a "Weddings and events" route leads to portfolio pages and an enquiry form. Mixing them, for example asking a couple to add a wedding package to a basket, rarely works well.

A structure that suits many florists

  • Shop: bouquets, arrangements, plants and gifts, grouped by occasion and by type.
  • Sympathy and funeral flowers: handled sensitively, with a route to discuss tributes.
  • Weddings and events: portfolio, approach and enquiry form.
  • Delivery information: areas, charges, cut-off times and how deliveries work.
  • About and visit us: the shop, opening hours and address.
  • Contact.

Delivery areas, dates and cut-offs

Delivery is the heart of a florist's ecommerce site. Make it clear before the customer gets far into checkout:

  • Which areas you deliver to, ideally with a postcode checker on product pages or early in checkout.
  • Delivery charges, which may vary by distance.
  • Your same-day cut-off time, and what happens to orders placed after it.
  • Days you do not deliver, and how busy periods such as Valentine's Day and Mother's Day are handled.
  • What happens if the recipient is out.

Use a date picker that blocks dates you cannot deliver, rather than accepting an order and calling the customer to explain. Most ecommerce platforms, including Shopify and WooCommerce, can support delivery dates and local delivery zones, either built in or through apps and plugins. Test the full flow on a phone for an order placed just before and just after your cut-off.

Product pages for a product that varies

Flowers are natural and seasonal, so the bouquet a customer receives may not look exactly like the photo. Say this honestly on product pages and in your terms: explain that you may substitute stems of similar style and value when particular flowers are unavailable. Under UK consumer law, customers are generally entitled to receive goods as described, so the description should be accurate and the substitution policy clear. Check your terms with a suitable adviser.

Good product pages include clear photos of your own arrangements, a short description of the style and main flowers, size options with photos if possible, care information, and whether a vase is included. Avoid stock images that do not show your actual work.

Perishable goods made to order may be treated differently from other goods when it comes to cancellation rights, but the rules are specific. Check the current Consumer Contracts Regulations guidance rather than assuming no refunds apply.

Gift messages and recipient details

Checkout needs to handle two people: the buyer and the recipient. Make it obvious where to enter the recipient's name, address and phone number, and where to write the card message, with a character limit shown. Offer an option to keep the sender anonymous if you provide that. Ask only for the information you need for delivery, and handle recipients' data carefully in line with UK GDPR, as they have not dealt with you directly.

Seasonal ranges and peak days

Valentine's Day, Mother's Day and Christmas can bring a large share of orders in a short time. Plan the site around them: seasonal collections that can be switched on and off, banners with clear cut-off dates, and limited product ranges at peak times if that helps you fulfil reliably. Make sure your hosting and checkout can cope, and consider capping orders per delivery slot so you do not take more than you can deliver. Remove seasonal banners promptly afterwards so the site does not look neglected.

Sympathy and funeral flowers

Funeral flowers need a calm, respectful section. Keep the design understated, explain how delivery to funeral directors works, ask for the funeral details you need, and offer a phone number for people who would rather talk. Bespoke tributes are often better handled through an enquiry form or call than a standard product page.

Weddings and events pages

Couples choose a wedding florist mainly on style. Show real weddings you have done, with permission from the couples and credit to photographers where their images are used. Explain your approach, the kinds of items you provide, how far in advance to book and how consultations work. If you have a minimum spend, saying so can save time for everyone. A short enquiry form asking for the date, venue and rough style is usually enough to start the conversation.

Mobile checkout and speed

Many flower orders are placed on a phone, often in a hurry. Keep checkout short, offer common payment methods including digital wallets where your platform supports them, and make sure the delivery date and message steps are easy on a small screen. Compress product photos so pages load quickly. Our guides to website image optimisation and Shopify conversion rate optimisation cover these areas in more detail.

Choosing a platform

Shopify and WooCommerce both suit florists who want a proper online shop with delivery dates and local zones. Squarespace and Wix can work for smaller ranges. Consider how easily you can update products as stock changes, how delivery dates and cut-offs are handled, and whether the platform works with the tools you already use. Our comparison of Shopify and WooCommerce may help.

Local trust signals

Customers ordering for local delivery want to know you are a real local florist. Show your shop address, opening hours and a photo of the shop, keep details consistent with your Google Business Profile, and link to genuine reviews. Our guides to local SEO and website trust signals explain more.

Subscriptions and corporate flowers

Some florists offer recurring deliveries, such as weekly flowers for a home, or regular arrangements for offices, hotels and restaurants. These suit a different presentation from one-off gifts. A subscription product needs clear wording on frequency, price per delivery, how to pause or cancel and what happens around bank holidays. Recurring payments must be set up so customers understand exactly what they are agreeing to, and cancelling should be straightforward.

Corporate clients usually want a conversation and a tailored quote rather than a checkout. A short page explaining the kinds of business flowers you provide, with photos of real installations used with permission, and an enquiry form, is often enough.

Order confirmations and customer updates

Because flowers are usually a gift for someone else, buyers can be anxious about whether the order arrived. Clear confirmation emails that repeat the delivery date, address and card message help, as does a short message once the order is delivered, if your systems support it. Make your contact details easy to find in every email so customers can reach you quickly if something needs changing. Keep marketing emails separate and only send them where the customer has given appropriate consent.

Common mistakes on florist websites

  • Delivery areas and cut-offs only revealed at the end of checkout.
  • Stock photos that do not reflect what the customer receives.
  • No clear substitution policy.
  • Wedding enquiries forced through a shop basket.
  • Out-of-date seasonal banners.
  • Checkout that is difficult on a phone.

FAQs

Can customers choose a delivery date on my website?

Yes. Shopify, WooCommerce and other platforms can support delivery date selection, either built in or through apps or plugins.

How do I handle flower substitutions?

State your substitution policy clearly on product pages and in your terms, and substitute with flowers of similar style and value.

Should wedding flowers be sold through the shop?

Usually not. An enquiry form and consultation suit weddings and events better than a basket.

How do I limit orders on busy days?

Many platforms allow you to cap orders per delivery date or slot, or block dates when you are full.

Do cancellation rights apply to flowers?

The rules for perishable and personalised goods are specific. Check current Consumer Contracts Regulations guidance and take advice.
