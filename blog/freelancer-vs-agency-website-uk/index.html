Freelancer vs Agency for Your Website: How to Decide

Should you hire a freelance web designer or an agency? A UK guide to the trade-offs in skills, capacity, cost, risk and support, with questions to ask both.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Neither a freelancer nor an agency is automatically better; the right choice depends on your project's scope, skills required and support needs.
  • Freelancers can offer a direct relationship, while agencies can bring a broader mix of skills and more capacity, but individual providers vary widely.
  • Whoever you choose, check relevant work, ownership arrangements, continuity plans and post-launch support.

When you need a new website or online shop, one of the first decisions is who to hire. Broadly, the choice is between a freelance web designer or developer and an agency or studio. Both build excellent websites, and both can disappoint. The labels themselves tell you less than people think, because freelancers and agencies vary enormously in skill, focus and working style. This guide sets out the genuine differences, the trade-offs that matter and the questions to ask so you can decide based on your project rather than assumptions.

If you are choosing support for search rather than a website build, our separate article on SEO agency vs freelancer covers that decision.

What we mean by freelancer and agency

A freelancer is an individual working independently. They might be a designer, a developer or both, and some collaborate with other freelancers for copy, photography or specialist development.

An agency or studio is a business with several people, or a coordinated team, offering a range of services. Some are large, others small. Some focus solely on web design; others combine websites with SEO, paid media, content and branding. Some work from offices; others, like Prism Works Studio, work remotely with clients across the UK.

The boundary is blurry. A small studio may feel very like a freelancer, and an experienced freelancer with trusted collaborators may cover as much ground as an agency.

When a freelancer can be a good fit

  • Your project is well defined. A clear brief with a modest scope suits one person working closely with you.
  • You want a direct relationship. You speak to the person designing or building the site.
  • You need a specific skill. For example, a developer who specialises in a particular platform or a designer with a style you love.
  • You have other pieces covered. If you have copy, branding and marketing handled, you may only need design and build.
  • Budget is tight. Lower overheads can make a freelancer more affordable, although this is not guaranteed.

When an agency can be a good fit

  • Your project needs several disciplines. Strategy, branding, copywriting, design, development and SEO coordinated together.
  • The scope is larger or more complex. Online shops with integrations, large content sites or redesigns with migrations.
  • You want continuity. A team may be able to cover holidays or absence more easily.
  • You want marketing support after launch. If you plan to invest in SEO, paid ads or content, it can help to have the same team understand the site.
  • You prefer a structured process. Defined stages, sign-offs and documentation can suit businesses with several stakeholders.

The risks on each side

Freelancer risks

The biggest risk is dependency on one person. If they become unavailable, change career or simply get too busy, your project or support can stall. Mitigate this by keeping all accounts in your own name, asking about backup arrangements and making sure the site is built on a widely used platform that other developers could pick up. Our guide to website maintenance explains why ongoing support matters.

Agency risks

With an agency, you may deal mainly with an account manager rather than the people building your site, which can slow communication. Smaller clients may get less attention than larger ones. Some agencies use rigid processes that add cost without adding value for simpler projects. Mitigate this by asking who will work on your project, how you will communicate with them and how the process will be adapted to your scope.

Risks that apply to both

  • Domains and accounts registered in the provider's name rather than yours
  • Vague proposals that leave out content, testing or launch steps
  • No written agreement on ownership of design and code
  • Websites built in ways that are hard for anyone else to maintain

Our guide to marketing agency contracts covers terms worth checking, and it is sensible to take independent legal advice on any contract you are unsure about.

Cost: comparing like with like

It is common to assume freelancers are cheaper, and their overheads are often lower. But price comparisons only make sense if you compare the same scope. An agency proposal may include copywriting, SEO setup, testing and post-launch support that a freelancer's quote leaves out, or vice versa. List what each proposal includes against your brief before comparing totals. Our guides to website design costs and ecommerce website costs explain the factors that drive price. Questions to ask either option

  • Who exactly will work on our project, and what will each person do?
  • Can we see live examples of similar sites you have built?
  • What is included in your proposal, and what would cost extra?
  • Which platform do you recommend and why?
  • Will the domain, hosting and platform accounts be in our name?
  • What rights will we have in the design and code once paid for?
  • What happens if you are unavailable during the project or afterwards?
  • What support is included after launch?
  • Will we be able to update the site ourselves?

The answers tell you far more than whether the provider calls itself a freelancer or an agency. Our article on how to choose a digital marketing agency offers further questions if you also need marketing support.

A hybrid approach

You do not have to pick one model for everything. Some businesses use an agency for the initial strategy and build, then a freelancer for small updates. Others use a freelancer for design and a separate specialist for development or SEO. Hybrid arrangements can work well, but they need clear responsibilities, shared access to accounts and someone who owns the overall picture. Otherwise, issues can fall between providers.

How communication differs in practice

Day-to-day communication is one of the areas where the experience can feel most different. With a freelancer, you typically message or call the person doing the work, so questions can be answered quickly and nuance is less likely to be lost. The flip side is that when they are focused on another client, replies may wait. With an agency, a project lead may coordinate several people, which can keep things organised but adds a layer between you and the designer or developer. Ask each provider how they prefer to communicate, how quickly they usually respond, how feedback is collected and how decisions are recorded. Clear answers here often predict how smoothly the project will run.

Thinking beyond launch

A website is not finished on launch day. Software and plugins need updating, content needs refreshing and you may want to add pages, products or features as the business grows. Before choosing, think about what you will need in the year after launch. If you expect to invest in search, advertising or content, consider whether you want that work done by the same provider, by someone else, or in-house. If you plan to manage the site yourself, check that the provider will train you and that the platform suits non-technical editing. These considerations can matter as much as the build itself.

Making the decision

Start with your brief. List the skills your project needs, how complex it is, what support you want after launch and how much involvement you can give. Then speak to a few providers of different types, give each the same brief and compare their answers to the questions above. Choose the one whose skills, process and communication best match your project, and make sure ownership and support are agreed in writing.

FAQs

Is a freelancer always cheaper than an agency?

Not always. Freelancers often have lower overheads, but what matters is what each quote includes. Compare scope before comparing price.

Are agencies better for ecommerce websites?

Not necessarily. Some freelancers specialise in Shopify or WooCommerce. Larger or more integrated shops may benefit from a team with a wider skill set, but check each provider's relevant experience.

What happens if my freelancer disappears?

If your domain, hosting and platform accounts are in your name and the site uses a common platform, another developer can usually take over. This is why ownership matters.

Can I switch from a freelancer to an agency later?

Yes, provided you control your accounts and have the access and files you need. A clear handover makes the switch smoother.

Do agencies use freelancers?

Some do, for specialist skills or extra capacity. It is reasonable to ask who will work on your project.
