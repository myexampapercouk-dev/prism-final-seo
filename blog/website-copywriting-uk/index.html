Website Copywriting for UK Businesses: How to Write Pages People Act On

How to write website copy that is clear, credible and easy to act on: home pages, service pages, product copy, calls to action and writing for search.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Good website copy starts with the visitor's problem, not the business's history.
  • Each page should have one main job and one obvious next step.
  • Specific, honest wording builds more trust than superlatives you cannot back up.

Design gets most of the attention in a website project, but words do most of the persuading. A visitor decides within moments whether a page is for them, and they decide that by reading the headline, scanning the subheadings and looking for an answer to their question. If the copy is vague, the best design in the world will not rescue it.

Prism Works Studio is a UK web design, ecommerce and digital marketing agency working remotely with businesses across the country. This guide is for owners and marketers who are writing their own website copy or briefing someone else to do it.

Write for one reader at a time

Before writing a word, describe the person most likely to land on the page. What have they just searched for? What are they worried about? What would make them pick up the phone or add something to the basket? Copy written for "everyone" tends to say very little to anyone.

A useful test is to replace your company name with a competitor's. If the page still reads as true, it is too generic. Look for the details that are genuinely yours: the area you cover, the way you work, the type of customer you suit best.

Give every page one job

PageIts main job
Home pageConfirm quickly what you do and who for, then route people to the right place
Service pageExplain one service clearly enough that a suitable buyer gets in touch
Product pageAnswer the questions that stop someone buying
About pageShow who is behind the business and why it can be trusted
Contact pageMake getting in touch easy and set expectations about what happens next

When a page tries to do everything, it usually does nothing well. Split topics into their own pages and link between them.

Headlines that say something

"Welcome to our website" and "Excellence in everything we do" waste the most valuable space on the page. A good headline tells a visitor what you offer and, ideally, who it is for. Compare "Quality solutions for your business" with "Bookkeeping for sole traders and small limited companies". The second is less grand and far more useful.

Subheadings matter almost as much, because many people scan a page by reading only those. Read your subheadings in isolation. If they tell the story of the page, you are on the right track.

Features, benefits and proof

Features describe what something is. Benefits describe what it means for the customer. Both have a place. "Same-day dispatch on orders before 2pm" is a feature; "Order this morning, have it by the weekend" turns it into something the reader can picture. Make sure any benefit you describe is genuinely true for your business.

Proof is what makes a claim believable. That might be real reviews, case studies you have permission to share, qualifications, or simply a clear explanation of how you work. Never invent testimonials, figures or results. Readers are good at spotting hollow claims, and in regulated sectors it can create real problems.

Calls to action

Every page should make the next step obvious. Clear buttons such as "Book a consultation", "Request a quote" or "Add to basket" usually work better than vague ones like "Submit" or "Learn more". Tell people what happens after they click: who will reply, roughly when, and whether there is any obligation. Uncertainty at that moment puts people off.

Writing product copy for online shops

Manufacturer descriptions copied across many retailers give a shopper no reason to buy from you and give search engines no reason to rank your page. Write your own, covering:

  • What the product is and who it suits
  • Sizes, materials, dimensions and compatibility, in a scannable format
  • Care, delivery and returns information close to the buy button
  • Answers to the questions customers actually ask you

Copy and search

Search-friendly copy is mostly clear copy. Use the words your customers use, give each page a distinct topic, write a descriptive title and meta description, and answer questions directly. Stuffing in the same phrase over and over reads badly and does not help. Our guide to SEO for small businesses explains how pages and search fit together.

Tone of voice

Decide how you want to sound and stay consistent. A solicitor and a skate shop should not sound alike. Whatever the tone, plain English almost always wins: short sentences, everyday words, active verbs. Spell out anything a newcomer might not know, and cut jargon unless your audience genuinely uses it.

UK businesses should also write for a UK reader. That means UK spelling, prices in pounds and references that make sense here.

Editing your draft

  • Cut the first paragraph and see if the page reads better without it. It often does.
  • Remove words such as "very", "leading", "world-class" and "innovative" unless you can prove them.
  • Read it aloud. Anything you stumble over needs rewriting.
  • Check it on a phone, where long paragraphs look like walls of text.
  • Ask someone outside the business what they think you do after reading it.

Answering objections before they are asked

Every buyer has doubts. Will it fit? How long will it take? What if I am not happy? Is this business too big or too small for me? Good copy anticipates those questions and answers them on the page, rather than leaving the visitor to email and ask, or more likely to leave. Look through your inbox and call notes for the questions customers ask most often, and make sure each one has a clear answer somewhere on the relevant page. An FAQ section can help, but the most important answers deserve a place in the main copy.

About pages that earn trust

The about page is often one of the most visited pages on a small business site, and one of the weakest. Rather than a history lesson or a list of values, tell visitors who runs the business, what experience they bring, how you work and what kind of customer you suit. Real photos help. If you are a small team, say so with confidence; many customers actively prefer dealing with the owner.

Microcopy matters

Small pieces of text, such as button labels, form hints, error messages and confirmation screens, shape how a site feels to use. An error message that says "Invalid input" is less helpful than one that says "Please enter a UK postcode, for example SW1A 1AA". A form that explains why you need a phone number is more likely to get one. These details are easy to overlook and quick to improve.

Plan copy before design

Many website projects design pages first and then try to squeeze words into the boxes. It usually works better the other way round: decide what each page needs to say, then design around it. FAQs

How long should a service page be?

Long enough to answer the questions a serious buyer has, and no longer. For many services that means several sections covering who it is for, what is involved and how to start.

Should I write my own website copy?

You know your business best, so your input is essential either way. Some owners write a draft and have it edited; others brief a writer. What matters is that the final copy is clear and accurate.

Can I use AI tools to write website copy?

They can help with drafts, but check every fact and claim, add genuine detail about your business and edit for tone. Generic, unchecked text rarely persuades anyone.

How often should website copy be updated?

Whenever your services, prices, team or processes change, and review key pages periodically to make sure they still reflect how you work.

Does copy affect search rankings?

Yes. Search engines need text to understand what a page is about, and helpful, specific pages tend to perform better than thin or duplicated ones.
