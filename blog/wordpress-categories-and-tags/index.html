WordPress Categories and Tags: Organising Your Blog for Readers and Search

The difference between WordPress categories and tags, how to plan a tidy structure, and how to avoid thin archive pages that confuse visitors and search.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Categories are broad sections of your blog; tags describe more specific details within posts.
  • Plan a small set of categories in advance and use tags sparingly and consistently.
  • Every category and tag creates an archive page, so avoid creating many with only one or two posts.

As a blog grows, organisation starts to matter. Visitors want to find more posts on the topic they care about, and search engines need to understand how your content fits together. WordPress gives you two built-in tools for this: categories and tags. Used well, they make a blog easier to browse. Used carelessly, they create clutter and dozens of near-empty pages.

This guide explains the difference between categories and tags, how to plan a sensible structure, what each choice means for SEO and how to tidy up a blog that has become disorganised.

What are categories?

Categories are the main sections of your blog. Think of them like the chapters of a book or the departments of a shop. Every post in WordPress must have at least one category, which is why posts without one are placed in the default category, often called "Uncategorised" until you rename it.

Categories can be hierarchical, so you can create subcategories beneath a parent. A business blog might have a "Marketing" category with subcategories for email and social media, for example. Use this sparingly: deep hierarchies can make navigation harder rather than easier.

What are tags?

Tags are optional labels that describe specific details of a post. If categories are chapters, tags are closer to the index at the back of the book. A post in a "Guides" category might be tagged with the particular product, platform or theme it covers.

Tags are not hierarchical, and a post can have several of them. Their value comes from connecting posts that share a detail across different categories.

Planning your categories

The best category structures are decided before a blog grows, though it is never too late to improve one. Start by listing the main topics you plan to write about consistently. These should reflect the questions your customers ask and the services or products you offer.

  • Keep the list short. A handful of clear categories is easier for visitors to understand than a long list.
  • Make each one distinct. If two categories overlap heavily, combine them.
  • Use plain names. Choose the words your readers would use, not internal jargon.
  • Give each post one main category. Assigning many categories to every post blurs the structure.
  • Rename the default category. "Uncategorised" looks unfinished; change it to something meaningful.

Using tags well

Tags are where blogs most easily become messy. Because they are quick to add, it is tempting to type new ones on every post. Over time this creates long lists of tags used once, slight variations of the same word and tags that duplicate categories.

A few habits keep tags useful:

  1. Keep an agreed list. Write down the tags you use and share them with everyone who publishes.
  2. Reuse before creating. Check whether a suitable tag already exists before adding a new one.
  3. Be consistent with wording. Decide on singular or plural and stick to it.
  4. Do not repeat categories. A tag with the same name as a category adds nothing.
  5. Only tag what you will cover again. A tag is useful when several posts will share it.

Some blogs do not need tags at all. If your categories are clear and your posts link to each other well, skipping tags is a perfectly reasonable choice.

Categories, tags and SEO

Each category and tag in WordPress creates an archive page listing the posts assigned to it. These pages can be useful entry points: a well-organised category page can help visitors and search engines understand your expertise on a subject.

Problems arise when there are many archive pages with very little on them. A tag used on a single post creates a page that mostly repeats a snippet of that post. Lots of these pages can add little value and make it harder for search engines to focus on your best content.

Practical steps include:

  • Keep categories and tags to those with a meaningful number of posts.
  • Add a short introduction to important category pages so they offer more than a list of links.
  • Consider whether thin tag archives should be kept out of search results, using your SEO settings. Take advice if you are unsure, as this affects how your site is indexed.
  • Link from posts to relevant category pages and related posts to strengthen the structure.

Displaying categories and tags on your site

How categories and tags appear depends on your theme. Many themes show the category above or below the post title and list tags at the end of a post. Block themes let you control this in the site editor, where you can add or remove the blocks that display post categories and tags in your templates.

Think about what helps the reader. A visible category link gives visitors a simple way to browse more posts on the same subject. A long row of tags at the bottom of every post, on the other hand, can look cluttered and rarely gets used. If your tags are not adding value for readers, there is no obligation to display them.

Category pages themselves deserve attention. Many themes show only a title and a list of posts. Adding a short description explaining what the category covers, and linking to the most useful posts within it, turns a basic archive into a helpful landing page. WordPress lets you write a description for each category, and many themes display it at the top of the archive.

Category and tag URLs

By default, WordPress includes a base word in category and tag addresses, such as "category" or "tag". You can change these bases in the permalink settings; see our guide to WordPress permalink settings. Making changes on an established site affects existing URLs, so plan redirects before changing anything, otherwise old links may lead to error pages. Our guide to redesigning without losing rankings covers redirect planning.

Tidying up a messy blog

If your blog already has too many categories or tags, a structured clean-up helps.

  1. Export a list. Note every category and tag with the number of posts in each.
  2. Decide the target structure. Choose the categories you want to keep and the tags worth keeping.
  3. Merge and reassign. Move posts from unwanted categories or tags into the ones you are keeping.
  4. Redirect removed archives. Point removed category or tag pages that had links or visits to the most relevant remaining page.
  5. Update menus and internal links. Check that navigation does not point to removed archives.
  6. Document the rules. Write down how categories and tags should be used from now on.

Take a backup before bulk changes, and work through them steadily rather than all at once on a large blog.

Custom taxonomies

Categories and tags are both taxonomies, which is the WordPress term for ways of grouping content. Sites with more complex content, such as a directory, a portfolio or a resource library, sometimes use custom taxonomies alongside custom post types. Our guide to WordPress custom post types covers when that extra structure is worth it. For a typical business blog, categories and tags are usually enough.

FAQs

Should I use categories or tags?

Use categories for every post to define its main section. Tags are optional and best used for specific details shared by several posts.

How many categories should a blog have?

There is no fixed number. Aim for a small set of distinct topics that you will write about regularly.

Can a post have more than one category?

Yes, but giving each post one main category usually keeps the structure clearer.

Are tag pages bad for SEO?

Not in themselves, but many thin tag pages with only one or two posts can add little value. Keep tags purposeful and consider your indexing settings.

What happens to posts if I delete a category?

Posts left with no category are moved to the default category. Reassign posts to the right category before deleting, and redirect the old archive if needed.
