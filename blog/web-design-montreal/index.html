Web Design for Montreal Businesses: Planning a Bilingual French and English Website

Web design for Montreal businesses: plan a bilingual French and English site, structure languages for SEO, check Quebec rules and keep both versions current.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • For most Montreal businesses the website question is not whether to publish in French but how to give French and English visitors an equally good experience.
  • Separate URLs for each language, a clear language switcher and correct hreflang signals help search engines show the right version to the right searcher.
  • Quebec's French-language and privacy rules affect websites, so check current official guidance and plan translation, consent and maintenance before the build starts.

Web design for a Montreal business almost always means planning a bilingual site, with French and English versions that are equally clear, equally fast and equally easy to find in search. That can be done remotely: Prism Works Studio is a UK-based studio that plans and builds websites for Canadian clients online, with no office or staff in Montreal or elsewhere in Quebec, and with your translator or French-speaking team supplying and checking the French content.

This guide explains how to structure a bilingual site, what Quebec rules you should check, how a remote project runs and what drives cost and timelines.

Why web design in Montreal is different

Montreal is a bilingual city in a province where French is the official language. Many customers will prefer French, many will prefer English, and plenty switch between the two. A website that treats one language as an afterthought sends a clear signal about how much you value those customers.

There is also a legal dimension. Quebec's Charter of the French Language, as amended in recent years, generally expects businesses operating in Quebec to make commercial information available in French, and French versions should not be less available than other languages. The detail can be complex and depends on your business, so please check current guidance from the Office québécois de la langue française and take your own legal advice. Our role is to build a site that makes compliance practical, not to give legal opinions.

Structuring a bilingual website

The structure you choose affects search visibility, maintenance and cost. The three common options are:

StructureExample patternStrengthsDrawbacks
Subfoldersyoursite.ca/fr/ and yoursite.ca/en/One domain, shared authority, simplest to manageNeeds a platform that handles languages cleanly
Subdomainsfr.yoursite.ca and en.yoursite.caClear separation, flexible hostingSearch engines may treat them more separately, so more SEO effort
Separate domainsA French domain and an English domainEach brand name can suit its languageTwo sites to maintain, renew and build authority for

For most Montreal businesses, subfolders on a single domain are the practical choice. Whichever you pick, each language version should have its own URL. Swapping text on the same URL depending on a visitor's browser setting makes it hard for search engines to index both versions.

Search engines also need to know that the French and English pages are equivalents. That is done with hreflang annotations, which point each page at its counterpart. Our guide to international SEO explains hreflang and the wider choices involved in serving several language markets.

Designing for two languages

Good bilingual design starts with layout, not translation. French text often runs longer than English, so buttons, menus and headings need room to grow without breaking on a phone. Some practical points:

  • Language switcher. Place it in the header on every page, label it with the language name rather than a flag, and link to the equivalent page, not the homepage.
  • Navigation. Menu labels should be natural in each language rather than literal translations. Our guide to website navigation structure covers how to keep menus short and clear.
  • Forms and messages. Labels, error messages and confirmation emails must exist in both languages. Our article on form error messages shows how to write them clearly.
  • Images with text. Avoid text baked into images where possible, so each language can be edited easily.
  • Default language. Decide what a first-time visitor sees. Many Quebec sites default to French and offer English clearly, but check the current rules that apply to you.

Translation, copywriting and upkeep

Translated copy that reads like a translation rarely converts well. The best bilingual sites are written, or carefully adapted, by people fluent in each language and familiar with your industry. Quebec French also has its own vocabulary and conventions, so a translator familiar with Quebec usage is worth seeking out.

We plan structure, page templates and content workflow, and we can write English copy as part of the project. Our guide to website copywriting explains how we approach it. For the French content, your translator or French-speaking team supplies and checks the text, and we build it in.

The harder part comes after launch. If English pages change and French pages do not, the site drifts out of step. Agree before launch who updates each language, how quickly, and whether a change can go live in one language before the other.

Privacy, cookies and accessibility in Quebec

Quebec has its own private sector privacy law, significantly updated in recent years, alongside federal privacy law. Among other things, it affects how consent for tracking and cookies is handled and what your privacy information must say. Your cookie banner should work in both languages and record visitors' choices properly. Our guides to cookie banner design and website terms and privacy policies cover the general principles, but Quebec-specific requirements should be checked against current official guidance from the Commission d'accès à l'information and with your own advisers.

If you collect email sign-ups, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) sets rules on consent and unsubscribing, so forms should capture consent clearly in each language. Accessibility is also worth building in from the start: readable text, good contrast, keyboard-friendly navigation and correctly declared page languages help everyone, including people using screen readers that need to know which language to read in.

Choosing a platform for a bilingual site

Not every platform handles two languages equally well. WordPress can run bilingual sites through well-established multilingual plugins, and some businesses prefer separate language sites within a WordPress multisite network, which our WordPress multisite guide explains. Shopify supports multiple languages for online stores, with translated product content, menus and checkout. The right choice depends on whether you mainly sell products, take bookings or publish information.

Whichever platform you use, test the whole journey in both languages before launch. That means browsing in French, adding to a basket or submitting a form, receiving the confirmation email and following any links in it. It is common for a site to look fully bilingual on the surface while an automated email, an invoice, a cookie notice or a third-party booking widget still appears only in English. Listing every customer touchpoint at the start of the project makes these gaps much easier to catch, and it gives your translator a complete list of the text they need to supply.

Cost and timelines for a Montreal website

A second language adds work in templates, testing and content entry, so it is scoped as part of the quote. A small business website typically takes three to five weeks to build and an ecommerce site five to twelve weeks. For bilingual projects, the timeline depends heavily on when translated content is ready, so we recommend starting translation as soon as the English copy is approved.

FAQs

Does my Montreal business website need to be in French?

Quebec's French-language rules generally expect businesses operating in Quebec to make commercial information available in French. The detail depends on your business, so check current guidance from the Office québécois de la langue française and take legal advice if unsure.

What does web design in Montreal cost with Prism Works Studio?

Our published prices are in pounds sterling: £1,000 to £2,000 for a small business website and £1,500 to £3,500 for ecommerce. A second language is scoped into the quote, and Canadian dollar quotes are available on request.

Do you translate the French content?

No. We plan the structure and build the site, and your translator or French-speaking team supplies and checks the French text. We can write the English copy.

Should French and English pages have separate URLs?

Yes. Separate URLs, usually subfolders such as /fr/ and /en/, let search engines index both versions. Hreflang annotations then tell them which pages are equivalents.

Which platform is best for a bilingual website?

WordPress with a multilingual plugin suits most service and content sites, while Shopify handles multiple languages well for online stores. The best choice depends on what the site needs to do.

Can a UK studio build a website for a Montreal business?

Yes. The project runs over video calls, shared drafts and a private staging site. The UK is typically five hours ahead of Montreal, so calls are held in your morning.
