Sticky Header Design: Keeping Navigation Close Without Getting in the Way

When a sticky header helps a business website or online store, how to design one that stays slim and accessible, and the mistakes that annoy visitors.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A sticky header keeps key navigation and actions within reach as visitors scroll, which suits longer pages and online stores.
  • The best sticky headers are slim, uncluttered and leave plenty of the screen for content, especially on mobile.
  • Test sticky behaviour with keyboard users, zoomed text and anchor links, as these are where problems usually appear.

The header is the strip at the top of a website that usually holds the logo, the main navigation and perhaps a search box, basket or contact button. On a traditional site it scrolls away as soon as the visitor moves down the page. A sticky header stays fixed at the top of the screen, or reappears when needed, so those links and actions are always close at hand.

Sticky headers are now a familiar pattern on business websites and online stores, but they are not automatically a good idea. A badly designed one eats screen space, hides content and frustrates people. This guide looks at when a sticky header makes sense, how to design one well and how to avoid the common pitfalls.

What is a sticky header?

A sticky header, sometimes called a fixed header, is a site header that remains visible as the visitor scrolls. There are a few variations:

  • Always visible: the header stays fixed at the top for the whole scroll.
  • Shrinking: the header starts at full size and becomes slimmer once the visitor scrolls, often with a smaller logo.
  • Scroll-up reveal: the header hides when the visitor scrolls down and slides back into view when they scroll up, a pattern sometimes called a smart or partially persistent header.

Each approach balances access to navigation against the space given to content. The right choice depends on your site and what visitors need to do.

When a sticky header helps

Long pages

On long service pages, guides and category pages, visitors may scroll a long way from the top. A sticky header means they do not have to scroll all the way back to move elsewhere or get in touch.

Online stores

For ecommerce, a persistent basket icon and search field can be genuinely useful. Shoppers can check their basket or search for another product from anywhere on a page, which supports browsing across several products.

Sites with a clear primary action

If your main goal is a booking, a call or an enquiry, a sticky header can keep that button visible. Visitors who decide to act halfway down a page can do so straight away.

When to think twice

Short brochure pages with little scrolling gain less from a sticky header. Content-heavy reading experiences, such as long articles, can feel cramped if a large header permanently takes the top of the screen. On small phone screens in landscape orientation, a fixed header can take up a significant share of the visible area.

None of these rule out a sticky header, but they suggest choosing a slimmer or scroll-up reveal version, or leaving it static on certain templates.

Design principles for a good sticky header

Keep it slim

The sticky version of your header should be noticeably compact. Reduce padding, shrink the logo and remove anything that is not essential while scrolling. Visitors came for the content, so the header should support it rather than compete with it.

Prioritise what goes in it

Decide which elements earn a permanent place. For most sites that means the logo linking home, a menu, and one primary action. For stores it may be search and basket. Secondary links such as social icons or a full utility bar can usually drop out of the sticky state.

Make it visually distinct

A sticky header sits on top of changing content, so it needs a solid background and a subtle divider or shadow to separate it. Transparent headers that look striking over a hero image can become hard to read once text and images scroll beneath them.

Smooth, not jumpy

When a header changes size or position, the transition should be quick and smooth. Avoid layouts that jump as the header switches state, as content shifting under the visitor's finger or cursor is disorientating and can count against visual stability measures.

Mind the mobile version

On mobile, the header often contains just the logo, a menu button and perhaps a basket. Keep tap targets comfortably large and consider the scroll-up reveal pattern so the full screen is available while reading.

Accessibility considerations

Sticky headers can create accessibility problems if they are not tested properly.

  • Focus hidden behind the header: when keyboard users tab through a page, the focused link can end up underneath a fixed header and become invisible. Adding scroll padding at the top of the page helps keep focused elements in view.
  • Zoom and large text: visitors who zoom in or increase text size can find a fixed header taking up much of the screen. Test at high zoom levels and consider letting the header scroll away when space is limited.
  • Skip links: a skip to content link remains important so keyboard and screen reader users can bypass repeated navigation.
  • Motion: keep animations subtle and respect the reduced motion preference that some visitors set on their devices.

Our guide to website accessibility covers these principles more broadly.

Anchor links and in-page navigation

A frequent issue with sticky headers is anchor links that jump to a heading only for the heading to land underneath the fixed header. This affects tables of contents, FAQ links and back to top features. The fix is usually a small amount of scroll offset in the site styles so that anchored sections stop just below the header. It is worth testing every page type that uses in-page links.

Performance and technical points

Modern browsers can make headers sticky with styling alone, which is generally lighter than relying on scripts that constantly watch scroll position. Where scripts are needed, for example to reveal the header on scroll up, they should be efficient so scrolling stays smooth on lower powered phones.

Also check how the header interacts with other fixed elements. Cookie banners, chat buttons, announcement bars and sticky add to basket bars can pile up and leave very little visible content. Review the whole screen together rather than each element in isolation.

Sticky headers on Shopify and WordPress

Many Shopify themes include a sticky header option in the theme editor, often with a choice between always visible and reveal on scroll up. On WordPress, block themes and many classic themes offer similar settings, or the behaviour can be added in the theme styles. Before customising, check what your theme already supports, as using built-in settings is usually easier to maintain through updates.

Announcement bars and sticky headers

Many stores show a slim announcement bar above the header for delivery messages or offers. Decide whether it should stick with the header or scroll away. Keeping both fixed doubles the space taken at the top of the screen, so letting the announcement bar scroll away is often the more comfortable option.

Testing your sticky header

Before launch, run through a simple checklist:

  1. Scroll through long pages on desktop, tablet and phone in both orientations.
  2. Tab through each page with a keyboard and check focus stays visible.
  3. Zoom the browser and increase text size to see how much space the header uses.
  4. Click every in-page anchor link and check headings are not hidden.
  5. Open menus and search from the sticky state to make sure they work there too.
  6. Check the header alongside cookie banners and other fixed elements.

If you have analytics or session recordings, watch how visitors use the header after launch and adjust if it seems to get in the way.

FAQs

Are sticky headers good for SEO?

A sticky header does not directly affect rankings. It can support a better visitor experience, but a header that causes layout shifts or hides content could work against you, so focus on usability.

Should my sticky header include a call to action?

If your site has one clear primary action, such as booking or enquiring, a compact button in the sticky header can be useful. Avoid adding several competing buttons.

What is better: always visible or reveal on scroll up?

It depends on the site. Reveal on scroll up gives more room for content and suits reading pages and mobile. Always visible suits stores where search and basket are used often. Testing with real visitors is the best guide.

Why do my anchor links hide headings under the header?

Fixed headers overlap the top of the screen, so the browser scrolls the heading under them. Adding a scroll offset in your site styles usually solves this.

Do sticky headers work with mega menus?

They can, but large dropdowns need to fit within the screen and scroll if necessary. Test them carefully on smaller laptop screens. Our guide to website navigation structure covers menu planning.

Can I turn off the sticky header on some pages?

Often yes, depending on your theme. Landing pages and checkout flows sometimes benefit from a simpler header, so it is worth checking what your theme allows.
