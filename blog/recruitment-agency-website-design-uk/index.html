Recruitment Agency Website Design: Job Boards, Candidates and Clients

Website design for UK recruitment agencies: job search, candidate applications, CV uploads, client vacancy forms, sector pages and data-protection-aware design.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A recruitment agency website serves two audiences, candidates and hiring clients, so give each a clear, separate route from the home page.
  • Job listings should be easy to search and filter on a phone, with structured data so roles can appear in Google's job search features.
  • CVs contain a lot of personal data, so application forms and storage need to be handled in line with UK GDPR and a clear privacy notice.

A recruitment agency's website has two jobs at once. It needs to attract candidates, help them find relevant roles and make applying easy. It also needs to persuade hiring managers that the agency understands their sector and can fill their vacancies. Those audiences want very different things, and a site that blurs them often serves neither well.

This article covers the website itself. For promotion, including content, social media, advertising and business development, see our guide to recruitment agency marketing in the UK.

Two audiences, two journeys

From the home page, visitors should be able to identify themselves quickly as a candidate or an employer. A simple split, such as "Looking for a job" and "Looking to hire", works for many agencies. Each route then leads to content designed for that audience.

Candidates generally want:

  • A job search that works quickly on a phone.
  • Clear job descriptions with location, salary or rate, contract type and hours.
  • A simple way to apply or register.
  • Reassurance about what happens to their CV and data.

Clients generally want:

  • Evidence that you understand their sector and the roles they hire for.
  • A clear explanation of how you work, including permanent, temporary or contract recruitment.
  • A quick way to register a vacancy or arrange a call.

Structuring a recruitment website

  • Jobs, with search and filters.
  • Candidates, covering registration, advice and how you work with jobseekers.
  • Employers or Clients, explaining your services and how to brief you.
  • Sectors or Specialisms, with a page for each area you recruit in.
  • About, including your team and approach.
  • Insights or Advice, if you will keep it updated.
  • Contact, including office or regional contacts if relevant.

Job search and listings

The job board is often the most-visited part of a recruitment site. It should be fast and easy to use on a phone. Useful filters include keyword, location, sector, job type (permanent, temporary, contract), salary range and remote or hybrid options. Show results clearly, with the key details visible without opening each listing.

Each job page should include:

  • Job title written as candidates would search for it, rather than internal jargon.
  • Location, including whether the role is remote, hybrid or on site.
  • Salary or rate, where the client has agreed it can be shown.
  • Contract type and hours.
  • Responsibilities and requirements, written clearly.
  • The consultant handling the role, if appropriate, and a clear apply button.

Remove filled or expired roles promptly, or mark them clearly as closed. Leaving old jobs live frustrates candidates and can make the site look neglected. Advertising jobs that do not exist to collect CVs is misleading and can breach regulations covering employment agencies, so only list genuine vacancies you are working on.

Google job search and structured data

Google can display job listings in a dedicated job search experience. To be eligible, job pages generally need structured data following Google's JobPosting guidelines, including details such as title, description, location, employment type and date posted. Google sets and updates the requirements, so follow its current documentation. Our guide to schema markup explains structured data more generally.

Connecting to your recruitment software

Most agencies use applicant tracking or recruitment CRM software. Many of these systems can feed jobs to your website automatically and pass applications back into your database. This avoids duplicate data entry and keeps listings current. When planning a site, check what integration options your software offers, such as plugins for WordPress, job feeds or an API, and how applications and CVs are transferred and stored.

If your system offers its own hosted job board, consider whether embedding it on your site or building a custom integration is better for user experience and search visibility. Hosted pages on a separate domain may not support your own site's search performance in the same way.

Application and registration forms

Long application forms put candidates off, particularly on phones. Keep the initial application simple: name, contact details, CV upload and perhaps a short note. You can gather more detail later. Allow common CV formats, and test the upload on mobile devices, including from cloud storage.

Make clear what happens after applying: whether the candidate will be contacted, how quickly, and what to do if they do not hear back. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design in more detail.

Data protection and CVs

CVs contain substantial personal data, and applications may include sensitive information. Recruitment agencies must handle this in line with UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act. On the website, this means:

  • A clear privacy notice explaining what data you collect, why, how long you keep it and who you share it with, including clients.
  • Secure transfer and storage of CVs, rather than unencrypted email attachments where avoidable.
  • Asking for consent to marketing separately from the application itself.
  • Not collecting information you do not need.

The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance relevant to recruitment. Take professional advice on your specific obligations.

Employer pages that build confidence

Employer-facing pages should explain how you work: the types of recruitment you offer, the process from briefing to placement, and the sectors and roles you cover. Be specific about your specialisms rather than claiming to recruit for everything.

A vacancy registration form or call booking option gives hiring managers a quick next step. Ask for enough to qualify the enquiry, such as role, location and timescale, without making the form a chore. If you choose to publish fees or terms, link to them clearly; otherwise explain how pricing is agreed.

Sector and specialism pages

Pages for each sector you recruit in serve both audiences. Candidates see relevant roles and advice; clients see that you understand their market. Each page might include an overview of the roles you recruit for, live jobs in that sector, relevant consultants and useful content. Write about each sector from genuine knowledge, avoiding unsupported claims about market conditions or placement figures.

Team pages

Recruitment is a relationship business, and both candidates and clients like to know who they will deal with. Consultant profiles with real photos, specialisms and contact details can encourage direct contact. Keep the team page current as people join and leave.

Trust and compliance signals

Agencies in the UK operate under employment agency regulations, and the Employment Agency Standards Inspectorate enforces rules that cover areas such as how agencies advertise and treat workers. Only display memberships or accreditations you actually hold, and follow each body's rules on using logos. Genuine reviews from candidates and clients, shared with permission, can help. Our guide to website trust signals covers the principles.

Speed and mobile performance

Many candidates search for jobs on phones, often in spare moments. A slow job board or clunky application process loses applicants. Optimise images, limit heavy scripts and test job search and applications on real devices. See our guide to mobile website design.

Candidate advice content

Advice pages can help candidates and give your site useful content for search. Topics might include writing a CV for a particular sector, preparing for interviews, understanding temporary and contract work, or what to expect when registering with an agency. Write from the genuine knowledge of your consultants, keep pages focused on one question each, and link relevant advice to live jobs where it makes sense. Date anything time-sensitive, such as guidance that depends on employment rules, and review it periodically.

Avoid publishing salary surveys or market statistics unless they come from your own verifiable data or a properly cited official source. Unsupported figures can undermine trust with the very clients you are trying to impress.

Temporary and contract workers

If you supply temporary or contract workers, they may need ongoing access to information after placement, such as timesheet submission, pay dates and who to contact with questions. A dedicated section, or a link to a worker portal provided by your software, keeps these practical details separate from the job search. Make sure any information about pay, holiday or worker rights is accurate and checked against current rules, since these areas are regulated.

Common mistakes on recruitment websites

  • A single journey that mixes candidate and client content.
  • Expired or filled roles left live on the job board.
  • Application forms that are long or broken on mobile.
  • Vague privacy information about what happens to CVs.
  • Claims to cover every sector without specific evidence.
  • Team pages listing consultants who have left.

FAQs

Should candidates and clients have separate sections?

Yes. Their needs are different, so clear separate routes from the home page usually work best.

Can my jobs appear in Google's job search?

They may be eligible if job pages include structured data that follows Google's current JobPosting guidelines.

Can my recruitment software feed jobs to my website?

Many systems offer job feeds, plugins or APIs. Check what your software supports.

How should CVs be handled?

Securely, in line with UK GDPR, with a clear privacy notice explaining how data is used and shared.

Should I show salaries on job listings?

Where the client agrees, showing salary or rate generally helps candidates decide whether to apply.
