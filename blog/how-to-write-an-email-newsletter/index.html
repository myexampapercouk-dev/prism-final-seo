How to Write an Email Newsletter People Actually Open and Read

To write an email newsletter, pick one main message, write a clear subject line, keep the body short and end with one action. A practical UK guide.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A good small business newsletter has one main message, a specific subject line, short scannable sections and a single clear call to action.
  • Consistency matters more than length: a short, useful email sent on a regular schedule builds more trust than an occasional long one.
  • Send only to people who have agreed to hear from you, make unsubscribing easy, test on mobile, and track clicks and enquiries rather than opens alone.

To write an email newsletter for a small business, choose one main message for each issue, write a specific subject line that tells readers what they will get, keep the body short and easy to scan, and finish with a single clear action. Send it on a regular schedule to people who have agreed to hear from you, and measure clicks and enquiries rather than just opens.

Many UK business owners start a newsletter with good intentions, send three issues, then stop because writing it feels like a chore and nobody seems to respond. Usually the problem is not the idea but the format: too much content, no clear purpose and a subject line that gives no reason to open. This guide sets out a simple, repeatable way to write newsletters that people read.

Decide what your newsletter is for

Before writing anything, be clear about the job the newsletter does for your business. Most small business newsletters fall into one of a few types:

Newsletter typeMain purposeTypical content
Helpful tipsBuild trust and stay front of mindSeasonal advice, how-tos, answers to common questions
Updates and newsKeep customers informedNew services, opening hours, events, team availability
Offers and launchesDrive sales or bookingsNew products, limited offers, early access
Curated roundupPosition you as a useful sourceYour recent blog posts, short commentary on industry changes

Most businesses mix these, but each issue should lean towards one. A heating engineer might send seasonal boiler tips in autumn and a servicing reminder in spring. An independent shop might alternate new arrivals with styling or care advice. If you have not yet built a list to send to, our guide on how to build an email list covers sign-up forms and consent.

How to write an email newsletter, step by step

  1. Pick one main message. Write it as a single sentence before you start: "Book your boiler service before the cold weather" or "Our new autumn range is in". Everything else supports that message.
  2. Write the subject line. Do this early, because it forces clarity. More on subject lines below.
  3. Open with the point. The first two lines should tell the reader why this email matters to them. Skip long greetings and apologies for not writing sooner.
  4. Add the main section. A short article, tip, offer or announcement, usually between 100 and 300 words.
  5. Add one or two short extras if useful. A recent blog post, a customer question answered, or a reminder about opening hours.
  6. End with one clear action. Book, buy, reply, read more. One main button is better than five competing links.
  7. Check the footer. Your business name, address details as required, and an easy unsubscribe link.
  8. Test, then send. Send a test to yourself, read it on your phone, click every link, then schedule it.

Writing subject lines and preview text

The subject line decides whether the rest of the email is read. Good subject lines for small business newsletters tend to be specific, plain and honest.

  • Be specific. "How to stop your pipes freezing this winter" is better than "November newsletter".
  • Keep it reasonably short. Many people read email on phones, where long subject lines are cut off.
  • Match the content. A misleading subject line may lift opens once but damages trust and can increase unsubscribes and spam complaints.
  • Use preview text. Most email platforms let you set the short line shown after the subject in the inbox. Use it to add detail rather than letting it default to "View this email in your browser".
  • Go easy on capitals, symbols and emojis. A little personality is fine, but a subject line full of exclamation marks looks like spam.

Writing the body so it gets read

People skim emails, so structure matters as much as wording.

  • Short paragraphs. Two or three sentences each, with plenty of space.
  • Clear subheadings. If you include more than one section, give each a short heading.
  • Write to one person. Use "you" and keep the tone close to how you would speak to a customer in person. Our tone of voice guide helps define how your business should sound.
  • Lead with benefits. Explain what the reader gets, not just what you did.
  • Do not paste whole blog posts. Write a short summary and link to the full article on your website, which brings readers to your site and keeps the email light.

The same principles that make website copy work apply here. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing clearly for busy readers in more depth.

Images, layout and mobile

Most email platforms offer templates, and a simple single-column layout is usually best for small businesses. It reads well on phones and is easy to keep consistent.

  • Use your logo and brand colours so readers recognise you instantly.
  • Add images only when they help, such as a product photo or a picture of finished work, and add alt text in case images are blocked.
  • Do not put important text inside images; some inboxes hide images by default.
  • Make buttons large enough to tap and label them with the action, such as "Book a service", not "Click here". Our guide to call to action buttons covers button wording.
  • Check how the email looks in dark mode on your own phone, as colours and logos can change.

How often to send

There is no single right frequency. A monthly newsletter is a realistic starting point for most small businesses, and some shops send more often around launches or busy seasons. What matters most is consistency. Readers who know roughly when to expect you are more likely to open, and a predictable schedule makes the writing easier to plan.

A simple content plan helps. Jot down twelve themes for the year, tied to your seasons, launches and common customer questions, and you will rarely face a blank page. Our content strategy guide explains how to plan topics that serve both email and your website.

UK rules to keep in mind

Marketing emails in the UK are covered by PECR and UK GDPR. In general terms, you need people's consent to send marketing emails, unless a specific exception applies, such as the soft opt-in for existing customers who bought or negotiated to buy similar products and were given a chance to opt out. Every email should identify your business and include an easy way to unsubscribe. This is general information rather than legal advice, so check the ICO's current guidance for your situation.

Deliverability matters too. Sending from your own domain, with the authentication records your email platform asks for, helps your newsletter land in inboxes rather than spam folders. Our guide to business domain and email setup explains the basics, and your email platform's help pages will list the records it needs.

Measuring whether it works

Open rates are a rough guide at best, because privacy features in some email apps can make opens appear higher than they really are. Focus instead on:

  • Clicks: which links people actually used.
  • Replies: a strong sign of engagement for service businesses.
  • Enquiries, bookings and sales: tracked in your analytics by tagging newsletter links. Our UTM tracking links guide shows how.
  • Unsubscribes and complaints: a sudden rise suggests the content, frequency or audience is off.

Review results every few issues and keep what works. For online shops, automated emails such as welcome and abandoned basket flows often do more than newsletters, and our ecommerce email marketing guide covers those.

FAQs

How to write an email newsletter for a small business?

Choose one main message, write a specific subject line and preview text, open with why it matters to the reader, keep the body short and scannable, and finish with one clear call to action. Test it on mobile before sending.

How long should a newsletter be?

Short is usually better. A main section of a few hundred words, plus one or two brief extras, is plenty for most small businesses. Link to your website for anything longer.

What should I put in my first newsletter?

Welcome readers, explain briefly what they can expect and how often, and include one genuinely useful piece of content or offer. Keep it simple; you can refine the format as you learn what readers respond to.

Can I email my existing customers without asking first?

Possibly, under the soft opt-in, if they bought or negotiated to buy from you, the marketing is about similar products or services, and they were given a simple chance to opt out at the time and in every message. Check the ICO's current guidance to confirm it applies.

What is a good subject line for a newsletter?

One that tells readers specifically what they will get, in plain language, such as a tip, a date or a new product. Avoid vague titles such as the month's name and avoid misleading claims.

Which email platform should I use?

Choose one that suits your list size, connects with your website or shop, offers easy templates and handles unsubscribes automatically. Compare current features and prices on each provider's website, as plans change often.
