Shopify Pre-Orders: A Practical Guide to Selling Before Stock Arrives

How to set up pre-orders on a Shopify store, from product page wording and payment options to stock handling, customer emails and UK consumer law points.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • Pre-orders let customers commit to a product before it is in stock, but only work well when expectations about dispatch are crystal clear.
  • Decide early whether to take full payment, a deposit or no payment upfront, because that choice shapes the setup on Shopify.
  • Keep pre-order products, stock levels and customer emails tidy so that orders can be fulfilled smoothly once stock lands.

A pre-order lets a customer buy a product that is not yet ready to ship. It might be a new release that is still being manufactured, a seasonal line that arrives later, or a bestseller that has temporarily sold out and is due back in. Instead of showing a disappointing "sold out" message, the store invites the customer to secure their item now and receive it later.

Shopify can support pre-orders in several ways, and the right approach depends on how you want to take payment and how your stock is managed. This guide walks through the decisions involved, how to present pre-orders clearly, and the practical housekeeping that keeps them from turning into a customer service headache.

When pre-orders make sense

Pre-orders suit situations where demand is likely to exist before the stock does. Common scenarios include:

  • New product launches. Opening orders before launch day lets keen customers commit early and gives you a sense of interest.
  • Restocks. When a popular item sells out and more is on the way, a pre-order keeps the sale rather than sending the customer elsewhere.
  • Made-to-order or limited runs. Some products are only produced once orders are in, and a pre-order model reflects that honestly.
  • Seasonal ranges. Items for a specific season or occasion can be offered in advance so customers can plan ahead.

Pre-orders are less suitable when you cannot give a reasonable idea of when stock will arrive. Taking orders with no realistic dispatch window tends to create frustrated customers and cancellation requests.

The key decision: how and when to take payment

Before touching any settings, decide how payment will work. This affects which tools you use and how orders flow through Shopify.

Full payment upfront

The customer pays the whole amount at checkout, just like a normal order. This is the simplest option for customers to understand and for you to manage. The trade-off is that you are holding money for goods you have not yet sent, so clear communication and a reliable plan for refunds matter.

Deposit now, balance later

Some stores take a partial payment at checkout and collect the remainder when the item is ready. Shopify supports this kind of arrangement through its purchase options features, often used alongside an app from the Shopify App Store. It can lower the barrier to committing, but it adds a second payment step that needs to be explained and that may fail if a card has expired.

Pay later when the item is ready

Another approach is to save the customer's payment details and charge them when the product ships. This can feel low-risk to customers, though again there is a chance the charge does not go through later. It also means your revenue lands only when you fulfil.

There is no single right answer. The best choice depends on your product, your cash flow and how confident you are about arrival dates.

Ways to set up pre-orders on Shopify

Continue selling when out of stock

The most basic method uses a built-in inventory setting. On a product variant, you can allow customers to keep purchasing when stock reaches zero. Combined with clear wording on the product page, this turns an out-of-stock item into something customers can still order.

This works for simple cases, but it has limits. By default, the product page and checkout will look like a normal purchase unless your theme or content says otherwise, so customers could miss that the item is not ready to ship. It also applies to every out-of-stock moment, not just planned pre-orders, so it needs careful use.

Purchase options and apps

For deposits, delayed charges or more control over how pre-order products are labelled, Shopify's purchase options features and apps built on them are the usual route. These can change the button text, add notes to the order, and tag pre-order items so they are easy to spot when fulfilling. Before adding any app, it is sensible to check whether it is actively maintained and how it affects your theme and checkout. Our Shopify apps audit guide covers how to weigh up apps generally.

Theme changes

Whichever method you use, the product page usually needs some adjustment so that pre-order items are obvious. That might mean a different button label, a short notice near the price, or a badge on collection pages. These changes can often be made in the theme editor, with more involved changes handled through theme customisation.

Presenting pre-orders clearly

The single biggest factor in a smooth pre-order is clarity. A customer should never be surprised that their item is not arriving next week.

Change the button wording

Replace "Add to cart" with wording such as "Pre-order now" for pre-order items. This simple change does a lot of work, because it reminds customers at the moment of decision that this is not a standard purchase.

State the expected dispatch window

Near the price and button, explain when you expect to send the item. Phrase it honestly, for example as an expected month rather than a precise date if you are not sure. If the estimate changes, update the page promptly.

Explain payment

If you are taking a deposit or charging later, say so plainly on the product page and again in the cart. Customers should understand exactly what they are paying now and what happens next.

Handle mixed carts

Think about what happens when a customer adds a pre-order item and an in-stock item to the same cart. Will you hold the whole order until the pre-order arrives, or ship the in-stock item first? Either approach can work, but customers should be told which applies. Splitting shipments may affect your shipping rates, so factor that into your decision.

Repeat key details in confirmation emails

Shopify's notification templates can be edited so that order confirmations for pre-order items repeat the expected dispatch window and payment arrangement. This gives customers a written reminder they can refer back to.

Managing stock and fulfilment

Pre-orders sit awkwardly with stock counts, because you are selling items that do not yet exist in your warehouse. A few habits help.

  • Set a cap where appropriate. If you only expect a certain quantity to arrive, consider limiting how many pre-orders you accept so that you do not oversell. Our guide to Shopify inventory management explains how stock levels and tracking work.
  • Tag or label pre-order orders. Using order tags makes it easy to filter pre-orders in the admin and avoid sending them by mistake before stock arrives.
  • Switch off pre-orders when stock lands. Once the product is physically in stock, return it to a normal listing so that new customers see standard availability and dispatch.
  • Fulfil in order. When the stock arrives, working through pre-orders in the order they were placed is generally fairest and easiest to explain.

How you describe availability across the site matters too. Our article on displaying stock availability covers wording for in-stock, low-stock and back-in-stock messages that pair well with pre-orders.

Keeping customers informed

Silence is what usually turns a pre-order customer into a complaint. Plan your communication in advance.

  • Send a clear confirmation explaining the pre-order terms.
  • If the expected date moves, tell customers promptly, explain briefly why and offer a cancellation if they no longer want to wait.
  • Let customers know when their item has shipped, as you would for any order.
  • If you are collecting a balance payment, give advance notice before the charge is taken.

Short, honest updates go a long way. Customers tend to be more patient when they feel kept in the loop.

UK consumer law considerations

Pre-orders are still distance sales, so the usual UK consumer protection rules for online selling are likely to apply. This is general information rather than legal advice, and you should take proper advice for your own circumstances, but a few points are worth being aware of.

  • Delivery timing. Consumer law generally expects goods to be delivered within any period agreed with the customer, or within a reasonable time if none was agreed. Stating an expected dispatch window and sticking to it, or updating customers when it changes, helps keep you on the right side of this.
  • Cancellation rights. Customers buying online usually have cancellation rights under distance selling rules. How these apply to pre-orders, including when the cancellation period starts, is worth checking with guidance from official sources such as the Competition and Markets Authority or a legal adviser.
  • Clear information. Pre-contract information about the product, the total price, payment arrangements and delivery should be given clearly before the customer commits.
  • Refunds. If you cannot fulfil an order, customers should be refunded promptly. Have a plan for this before you start taking pre-orders.

Your terms and conditions and refund policy should reflect how you handle pre-orders. Shopify includes policy templates, but these are starting points and may need tailoring for your business.

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • Vague dispatch information. "Coming soon" with no indication of timing invites anxious emails.
  • Leaving pre-orders switched on by accident. Using the "continue selling" setting broadly can mean customers order items you have no plan to restock.
  • Forgetting the checkout and emails. A clear product page means little if the confirmation email reads like a normal order with standard delivery promises.
  • Overcomplicated payment schemes. Deposits and delayed charges can work well, but only if customers understand them. Keep the explanation simple.
  • Too many apps. Layering several tools to manage pre-orders can slow your store and cause conflicts. Pick one approach and keep it tidy.

FAQs

Can Shopify do pre-orders without an app?

For simple pre-orders with full payment upfront, you can use the built-in option to continue selling a variant when it is out of stock, combined with clear product page wording. For deposits, delayed payments or automatic labelling, an app built on Shopify's purchase options is usually needed.

Should I take full payment for a pre-order?

It depends on your product, cash flow and confidence in the arrival date. Full payment is simplest to explain and manage. Deposits or delayed charges can feel lower risk to customers but add extra steps and the chance of a failed payment later.

How do I stop overselling a pre-order?

Set a limit based on the quantity you expect to receive, either through stock levels or the tool you use for pre-orders. Review it as your supplier confirms quantities, and switch the product back to a normal listing once stock arrives.

What happens if the stock is delayed?

Tell affected customers as soon as you know, give a revised estimate and offer them the option to cancel for a refund. Update the product page so that new customers see the new timing too.

Do pre-orders affect my store's SEO?

A pre-order product page can be indexed like any other product page, which can help people find a new product before launch. Keep the page content accurate and avoid deleting it once the product is in stock, so that any visibility it has built carries forward. Our Shopify SEO guide covers product page basics.
