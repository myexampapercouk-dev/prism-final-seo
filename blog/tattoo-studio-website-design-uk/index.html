Tattoo Studio Website Design: Portfolios, Artists and Bookings

Website design for UK tattoo studios: artist portfolios, consultation and booking requests, deposits, aftercare pages, age policies and local search.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A tattoo studio website is mainly a portfolio and an enquiry tool, so artist pages and a clear booking request process usually matter more than anything else.
  • Spelling out deposits, cancellations, age requirements and aftercare in plain language can cut down repetitive messages and set expectations before anyone sits in the chair.
  • Licensing and registration for tattooing is handled locally in the UK, so studios should check the rules with their own council rather than relying on general guidance.

People rarely choose a tattoo studio on price alone. They choose an artist whose style they like, a studio that feels clean and welcoming, and a process that seems clear and professional. Much of that judgement now happens online, often after someone has already seen an artist's work on social media and wants to know more before getting in touch. A studio website is where that curiosity turns into a booking request, or quietly drifts away.

This guide covers website design for UK tattoo studios, from single-artist private studios to busy shops with several resident and guest artists. It focuses on the website itself. For getting found in local search, see our guides to local SEO and Google Business Profile.

What a tattoo studio website needs to do

Social media profiles are good at showing fresh work, but they are less good at answering practical questions or collecting structured enquiries. A website fills that gap. For most studios, its main jobs are to:

  • Show each artist's style through a well-organised portfolio.
  • Explain how to book, from first enquiry to consultation and appointment.
  • Set out deposits, cancellation terms and age requirements clearly.
  • Reassure visitors about hygiene, licensing and professionalism.
  • Provide aftercare information people can return to after their session.
  • Make the address, opening hours and contact details easy to find.

Keeping those jobs in mind helps avoid a site that looks striking but leaves people unsure what to do next.

Artist pages and portfolios

Most people search for a style before a studio, such as fine line, blackwork, traditional, realism or lettering. That makes individual artist pages one of the most useful parts of the site. A good artist page might include:

  • A short introduction in the artist's own voice.
  • The styles they specialise in, and any they do not take on.
  • A curated gallery of healed and fresh work, clearly labelled.
  • Their availability or how often their books open.
  • A link straight to the enquiry form, with their name already selected.

Quality matters more than quantity. A tight selection of well-lit, well-cropped photos tends to say more than hundreds of images of varying quality. Showing healed work alongside fresh pieces can help, because it shows how tattoos settle over time. Images should be optimised so galleries load quickly on mobile, which is where most visitors will browse. Our guide to website image optimisation covers the practical side.

If your studio hosts guest artists, a simple guest spots page listing who is visiting and when can be useful, as long as someone keeps it up to date. Out of date guest listings can frustrate people who travel to book.

Before publishing any photo, make sure the client is comfortable with their tattoo being shown, particularly for pieces on more personal areas of the body. Some studios ask for this consent as part of their booking or release form.

The enquiry and booking process

Booking a tattoo is usually different from booking a haircut. Custom pieces need a design conversation first, sizes and placements affect timings, and some artists only open their books at certain times. The website should explain whatever process your studio actually uses. Common approaches include:

  • A structured enquiry form asking for the idea, placement, approximate size, preferred artist, reference images and availability.
  • Consultation bookings, in person or by video, before the main appointment.
  • Online booking for fixed items such as flash designs, small walk-in style pieces or piercings, if your studio offers them.

A good enquiry form saves time on both sides. It means artists receive the information they need in one go, rather than through a long back-and-forth in direct messages. Keep it as short as possible while still collecting the essentials, and allow image uploads for references. Our guide to website contact forms has more on getting forms right, and our guide to online booking systems covers the general options for appointments.

Tell people what happens after they submit. If artists typically reply within a certain timeframe, or if they only respond to enquiries that fit their style, saying so upfront can reduce follow-up messages.

Flash days and available designs

Many studios run flash days or keep a selection of pre-drawn designs available. A dedicated page can show available flash, mark designs as claimed and explain how flash bookings work. This can be a simple gallery updated by the studio, or a booking setup that lets people reserve a design and slot together. Whichever approach you take, make sure it is easy for whoever runs the studio to update without needing technical help.

Deposits, cancellations and policies

Deposits are common in tattooing, and misunderstandings about them can cause friction. A clear policies page, linked from the booking form, might cover:

  • Whether a deposit is required, how it is paid and whether it comes off the final price.
  • What happens if someone cancels, reschedules or does not turn up.
  • How design changes are handled before the appointment.
  • Payment methods accepted on the day.

Write these in plain language rather than legal-sounding terms. If you take deposits online, make sure your terms are shown before payment. Consumer law can affect what is fair and enforceable in cancellation terms, so it may be worth taking advice if you are unsure. For prices themselves, many studios explain how pricing works, such as a minimum charge or an hourly or day rate approach, rather than listing fixed figures for custom work.

Age, ID and health questions

In the UK, it is illegal to tattoo anyone under 18, even with parental consent. Stating this clearly, and explaining that ID may be requested, avoids awkward conversations at the door. A short section on who may need to wait or seek medical advice before being tattooed, for example during pregnancy, with certain skin conditions or on certain medications, can also help, as long as it is phrased carefully and encourages people to raise concerns with the artist or their GP rather than offering medical advice.

If your booking form or consent form collects health information, that is sensitive personal data under UK GDPR. Only ask for what you genuinely need, explain how it will be stored and used, and make sure your privacy notice covers it. The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance for small businesses.

Hygiene, licensing and trust

A first tattoo can be nerve-wracking, and visitors will look for reassurance that a studio is clean and professional. In much of the UK, tattooists and premises need to be registered or licensed with their local council, and the specific requirements vary depending on where you are. Rather than making broad claims, a studio might simply state that it is registered or licensed with its local authority, if that is the case, and describe its hygiene practices in plain terms, such as single-use needles and how equipment is cleaned.

Photos of the studio space, a short note about the team and genuine reviews from clients can all help. Our guide to website trust signals covers this in more detail. Only display reviews that are real, and avoid editing them in ways that change their meaning.

Aftercare pages

An aftercare page is one of the most useful things a studio website can offer. Clients often lose the printed sheet they are given and search for advice in the days that follow. A clear page explaining your studio's recommended aftercare, what is normal during healing, and when to seek medical advice gives them something reliable to return to. Keep the advice consistent with what artists tell clients in person, and suggest people speak to a pharmacist or GP if they are worried about signs of infection.

A short link to this page in confirmation emails or on a card handed out after the session can make it easy to find again.

Design and tone

Tattoo studios have more freedom with visual identity than many businesses. Dark backgrounds, bold typography and strong imagery can all work, but they should not get in the way of readability. Text needs enough contrast to read comfortably, buttons should be obvious, and the site needs to work well on a phone. Our guides to website colour and typography and mobile website design cover the principles, and accessibility guidance applies here as much as anywhere.

Let the studio's personality come through in the writing too. A friendly, direct tone can make the process feel less intimidating for first-timers, while still being clear about policies.

Selling merchandise and gift vouchers

Some studios sell prints, clothing or aftercare products, and gift vouchers can be popular. These can be added through a simple online shop, or through ecommerce features on platforms such as Shopify, WooCommerce or Squarespace. Our guide to gift cards and vouchers covers how vouchers can work on a website. Keep the shop secondary to the main purpose of the site, so it does not distract from artist pages and enquiries.

Choosing a platform

Most tattoo studio websites are portfolio and enquiry sites, so WordPress, Squarespace and Wix can all be suitable. The decision often depends on how many artists need their own galleries, whether you want online deposits or a shop, and who will update the site day to day. FAQs

Does a tattoo studio need a website if it is active on social media?

Social media is good for showing new work, but a website can organise portfolios by artist, collect structured enquiries and explain policies in one place, which social profiles do less well.

Should tattoo studios take deposits online?

Many studios do, as it can reduce no-shows. If you take deposits online, show your deposit and cancellation terms clearly before payment, and consider taking advice on whether they are fair.

What should a tattoo enquiry form ask for?

Typically the idea, placement, approximate size, preferred artist, reference images and availability. Keep it short enough that people complete it.

Should prices be listed on a tattoo studio website?

Fixed prices are hard for custom work, so many studios explain how pricing works, such as a minimum charge or how time is quoted, and invite people to enquire.

Do tattoo studios need a licence in the UK?

Requirements are set locally and vary across the UK. Check with your local council about registration or licensing for your premises and artists.
