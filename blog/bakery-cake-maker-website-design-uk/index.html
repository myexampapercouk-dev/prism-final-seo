Bakery and Cake Maker Website Design: Orders, Galleries and Allergens

Website design for UK bakeries and cake makers: custom cake enquiries, online ordering, galleries, allergen information, collection and delivery details.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Custom cake customers usually want to see examples, understand rough pricing and know how far ahead to order.
  • Allergen information needs particular care for bakery products, so check current Food Standards Agency guidance for how you sell.
  • A bakery selling everyday bread and a cake maker taking bespoke orders often need quite different websites, so build for how you actually sell.

Bakeries and cake makers sell something that is hard to resist in person, but a growing share of orders now start online. A customer planning a birthday, wedding or office celebration will often search, browse photos and compare makers before getting in touch. A high street bakery may want to take preorders or sell gift boxes for delivery. Either way, the website needs to make products look as good as they taste and make ordering simple.

This guide covers website design for UK bakeries, cake makers and home bakers who sell to the public, whether from a shop, a home kitchen or online. It focuses on the website itself. If you run a cafe that also bakes, see our guide to cafe website design. For shipping food products nationally, our guide to food and drink ecommerce covers the wider picture.

Work out how you sell first

Bakery businesses vary widely, and the website should reflect the real model. Common types include:

  • High street bakeries selling bread, pastries and cakes over the counter, perhaps with preorders.
  • Custom cake makers creating bespoke celebration and wedding cakes to order.
  • Online bakeries posting brownies, cookies or gift boxes across the UK.
  • Wholesale bakers supplying cafes, shops and restaurants.

Many businesses combine several of these. Decide which matters most and design the homepage around it, with clear routes to the others. A wedding cake maker and a sourdough bakery need very different first impressions. Our guide to homepage design covers how to prioritise.

Galleries that sell custom cakes

For custom cake makers, the gallery is often the most important part of the site. Customers want to see your style and skill before they enquire. Some principles:

  • Organise by occasion, such as weddings, birthdays, children's cakes and corporate, so visitors can find relevant examples quickly.
  • Use good, consistent photography with natural light and uncluttered backgrounds.
  • Show only your own work, and get permission before showing identifiable people or private events.
  • Add short captions, such as the number of tiers or flavours, to help customers describe what they want.
  • Keep the gallery current and remove older work that no longer reflects your style.

Compress images so the gallery loads quickly on phones, where most browsing tends to happen. See our guides to product photography and image optimisation.

Custom cake enquiry forms

A well-designed enquiry form saves time on both sides. It might ask for:

  • The occasion and the date needed.
  • Number of servings.
  • Preferred flavours and any dietary requirements.
  • Design ideas, with an option to upload inspiration images.
  • Whether the cake will be collected or delivered, and the location.
  • A rough budget, if you find it useful.

State your typical notice period, for example how far ahead wedding cakes should be booked, and when the customer can expect a reply. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design.

Pricing guidance

Customers often hesitate to enquire if they have no idea what a cake might cost. You do not have to publish fixed prices for bespoke work, but some guidance helps. Options include "from" prices for common sizes, a price guide by servings, or example cakes with indicative prices. Explain what affects cost, such as tiers, decoration complexity and delivery. Be clear about deposits and cancellation terms. Our guide to pricing page design covers presentation.

Online ordering for standard products

For everyday items, such as celebration cakes in set designs, cupcake boxes, brownies or bread preorders, online ordering can work well. Consider:

  • Clear product options, such as size, flavour and personalised message.
  • A date picker for collection or delivery, with lead times built in.
  • Limits on daily capacity so you do not take more orders than you can bake.
  • Clear cut-off times for next-day or weekend orders.

Our guides to product variants and gift cards and vouchers cover related features.

Allergen and ingredient information

Allergen information is especially important for bakeries, where common allergens such as gluten, eggs, milk and nuts are often present. UK food law requires allergen information to be available to customers, and the rules differ depending on whether food is prepacked, prepacked for direct sale or sold loose, and whether it is sold online. For distance selling, allergen information generally needs to be available before purchase and on delivery. Check current Food Standards Agency guidance and your local authority's advice for your situation.

On the website, show allergen information clearly on each product page and explain your approach to cross-contamination honestly. Avoid claims such as "nut-free" or "gluten-free" unless you can genuinely meet the legal requirements for those claims. If you take custom dietary orders, explain how you handle them and their limits.

Registration and hygiene information

Food businesses in the UK, including home bakers, generally need to register with their local authority before trading. Many customers check food hygiene ratings, and in some parts of the UK displaying them is a legal requirement in premises; online display rules vary by nation. You may choose to show your rating on the website. Check current requirements with your local authority rather than relying on general advice.

Collection, delivery and posting

Make it easy to understand how customers receive their order:

  • Collection address, times and any instructions.
  • Local delivery areas, costs and time slots.
  • For posted products, which items travel well, packaging used and delivery days.
  • What happens if a delivery is missed.

Cakes are fragile and perishable, so be realistic about what can be sent by post. Our guide to delivery and returns pages covers how to present this. Note that consumer cancellation rights for perishable and personalised goods differ from standard goods; check current guidance.

Wedding cake pages

Wedding cakes are usually a considered purchase made months ahead. A dedicated page can explain your process in your own words, such as how consultations or tastings work if you offer them, booking timelines, delivery and set-up, and how to check availability for a date. A gallery of wedding work with couples' permission helps. Make the next step clear, whether that is an enquiry form or booking a consultation.

Wholesale and corporate orders

If you supply cafes or offer corporate orders, a separate page explaining what you offer, minimum orders and how to set up an account keeps these enquiries away from your retail routes. Keep it brief and only describe what you can deliver consistently.

Local visibility

Most bakeries serve a local area. Make your location and opening hours clear, keep your Google Business Profile current with good photos, and see our guide to local SEO.

Seasonal ranges and special occasions

Many bakeries see demand rise around particular dates, such as Christmas, Easter, Mother's Day and Valentine's Day. The website can help you plan for these rather than being caught out. Consider creating seasonal collection pages a few weeks ahead, with clear order deadlines and collection dates, so customers know exactly when to order. Promote them on the homepage while they are live, then take them down promptly afterwards so the site does not look out of date.

If you sell out regularly, say so plainly and consider a simple way for customers to register interest for the next batch or the next occasion. A short email list can be useful here, provided people actively opt in and you explain how you will use their details, in line with UK GDPR. Our guide to seasonal sales preparation covers planning busy periods in more depth, and our guide to FAQ pages can help you answer recurring questions about lead times, storage and dietary options in one place.

Choosing a platform

Shopify and WooCommerce can handle online orders with date pickers and product options, often with apps or plugins. Squarespace or Wix can suit a smaller cake maker who mainly needs a gallery and enquiry form. Choose something you can update easily as your menu and seasonal ranges change. See our guides to Shopify vs WooCommerce and website builders vs custom websites.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Galleries with poor photos, or images that are not your own work.
  • No indication of price or notice periods.
  • Missing or unclear allergen information.
  • Online ordering with no limit on daily capacity.
  • Vague collection and delivery details.

FAQs

Should a cake maker show prices on their website?

Many find that "from" prices or a price guide by servings encourage enquiries, even when final prices depend on design.

What should a custom cake enquiry form ask?

The date, occasion, servings, flavours, dietary needs, design ideas and whether the cake will be collected or delivered.

Do bakeries need allergen information on their website?

If you sell online, allergen information generally needs to be available before purchase. Check current Food Standards Agency guidance.

Can a bakery take online orders for collection?

Yes. A date picker, cut-off times and daily capacity limits help manage collection orders.

Can I post cakes to customers?

Some products, such as brownies and cookies, travel better than others. Be realistic about what can be posted and package it carefully.
