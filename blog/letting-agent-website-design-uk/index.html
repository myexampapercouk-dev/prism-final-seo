Letting Agent Website Design: Winning Landlords and Serving Tenants

Website design for UK letting agents and property managers: landlord service pages, fee transparency, rental listings, tenant information and repairs.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • A letting agent's site usually serves two audiences with different needs, landlords choosing a manager and tenants looking for a home, so give each a clear path.
  • Fees, redress scheme membership and client money protection need to be displayed in line with the rules that apply in your part of the UK, so check current requirements.
  • Tools for existing tenants and landlords, such as maintenance reporting and portal logins, can reduce routine calls if they are easy to find.

Letting agents and property management companies rely on their websites in two quite different ways. Landlords use them to decide who to trust with a valuable asset and an ongoing responsibility. Tenants use them to find a home, apply and, once they have moved in, to report problems and find information. A website that only works well for one of these groups leaves value on the table. One that serves both, clearly, can win new instructions and reduce the volume of routine enquiries.

This guide covers website design for UK letting agents, property managers and block management companies. It focuses on the website itself. Our guide to estate agent website design covers sales-focused agency sites, and our guide to estate agent marketing covers wider promotion.

Two audiences, two journeys

The homepage and main navigation should make it obvious where each visitor should go. A common pattern is clear routes for:

  • Landlords: services, fees, valuations and how management works.
  • Tenants looking for a home: available properties and how to apply.
  • Current tenants: reporting repairs, contacts and useful information.
  • Current landlords: portal login and statements.

Labelling these clearly, rather than hiding them in a long menu, helps everyone get to what they need quickly. Our guide to website navigation covers menu design.

Winning landlords

For many letting agents, landlord instructions are the commercial priority. Landlords often want to know:

  • What service levels you offer, such as let only, rent collection or full management.
  • What each level includes and excludes.
  • What it costs.
  • How you find and check tenants.
  • How maintenance, inspections and compliance are handled.
  • How and when they get paid and receive statements.

A comparison table of service levels can make the differences easy to see. Describe what you genuinely do, and avoid implying that you guarantee things you cannot control, such as rent being paid or properties never being empty. If you offer rent guarantee products, describe them accurately and make clear who provides them and what terms apply. Our guide to pricing page design covers comparison layouts.

Fees and required information

Letting agents in the UK are subject to rules on displaying fees and certain information, and these differ between England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. In England, for example, agents are generally required to publish their fees, membership of a redress scheme and whether they are members of a client money protection scheme on their website. Restrictions on fees that can be charged to tenants also apply in several parts of the UK. Requirements change, so check current official guidance for each nation where you operate, and your trade body's advice if you belong to one.

Practical points for the website:

  • Display landlord fees clearly, including VAT where it applies.
  • Make sure any tenant charges shown are permitted where the property is located.
  • Show redress scheme and client money protection membership accurately, with certificates where required.
  • Keep fee pages consistent with your terms of business.

Material information also needs to be included in property listings in line with consumer protection rules. Trading Standards guidance on property listings is a useful starting point.

Property listings for renters

Renters often search on property portals first, but many will visit an agent's own website to see more properties or check details. Good listings pages include:

  • Search filters by area, price, bedrooms and availability date.
  • Clear rent figures, deposit information and any permitted charges.
  • Quality photos, floor plans and an accurate description.
  • Required material information, such as council tax band and energy performance information.
  • A simple way to request a viewing.

Listings usually come from your property management software via a feed or plugin, so check what your system supports before choosing how the site is built. Remove let properties promptly, or mark them clearly. Our guide to image optimisation covers keeping photo-heavy pages fast.

Explaining the application process

Renters, especially first-time renters, often find the process confusing. A page explaining, in general terms, how applications, referencing, Right to Rent checks where they apply, holding deposits and move-in work can reduce calls and frustration. Describe your actual process, link to official government guidance for tenants, and keep it updated as rules change. An FAQ page can work well here; see our guide to FAQ pages.

Current tenants: repairs and information

Once someone has moved in, the website becomes a service tool. Consider:

  • A clear "Report a repair" route, linked to your maintenance system if you use one.
  • Guidance on what counts as an emergency and who to contact out of hours.
  • Simple troubleshooting for common issues, such as a boiler that won't fire.
  • Information on ending a tenancy and the deposit return process.

Be careful with safety advice: for issues such as gas leaks, direct tenants to the appropriate emergency services first. Our guide to contact forms covers designing forms that capture the right details, such as photos of the problem.

Landlord portals and existing clients

If your software provides a landlord or tenant portal, link to it clearly from the header, separate from new business calls to action. Explain to new landlords what they will be able to see, but only describe features your system genuinely provides.

Block and leasehold management

If you manage blocks of flats or estates, your audience includes leaseholders, residents' management companies and freeholders. They want to understand how service charges are handled, how communication works and how to report communal issues. Leasehold rules are complex and changing, so keep explanatory content general, dated and linked to official sources.

Valuations and enquiries

A rental valuation form is a common way to start a landlord conversation. Keep it short: property address, type, bedrooms and contact details are often enough to begin. Explain what happens next. Online valuation tools that give instant estimates can attract enquiries, but make clear that any figure is an estimate and not a guarantee of achievable rent. See our guide to website trust signals for presenting credibility.

Local pages and search

Letting agents usually operate in defined areas, which makes local search important. Area pages describing the rental market in neighbourhoods you know, local transport and amenities can help both renters and landlords, as long as each page contains specific, useful information rather than duplicated text. Our guides to local SEO, Google Business Profile and multi-location SEO cover the essentials.

Mobile use and accessibility

Renters often search on phones and need to act quickly when properties come up. Listings, viewing requests and repair reports should all work smoothly on mobile. Clear text, good contrast and accessible forms help everyone. See our guides to mobile website design and website accessibility.

Choosing a platform

WordPress is common for letting agent sites because many property software providers offer listings plugins or feeds for it. Some property software includes its own website builder, which may be simpler but less flexible. Check how well your chosen approach handles listings, compliance information and portal links. See our guide to website builders vs custom websites.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A site that only addresses renters and gives landlords little reason to instruct you.
  • Fees or scheme memberships missing, hard to find or out of date.
  • Tenant charges shown that are no longer permitted.
  • Let properties left on the site as if available.
  • No clear route for reporting repairs or emergencies.
  • Rental estimates presented as guarantees.

FAQs

What must a letting agent show on its website?

Requirements vary across the UK. In England, for example, agents are generally required to publish fees and redress and client money protection details. Check current official guidance for your area.

Should a letting agent's site be aimed at landlords or tenants?

Both. Give each a clear route from the homepage, with separate paths for current tenants and landlords.

Can tenants report repairs through the website?

Yes. A clear repair reporting route, with emergency guidance, can reduce calls and capture useful details.

How do property listings get onto a letting agent's website?

Usually through a feed or plugin from your property management software. Check what your system supports before building.

Should a letting agent offer an online rental valuation tool?

It can generate enquiries, but make clear any figure is an estimate rather than a guarantee of achievable rent.
