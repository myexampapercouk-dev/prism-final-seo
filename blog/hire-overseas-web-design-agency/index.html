Hiring an Overseas Web Design Agency: A Practical Guide for US Businesses

Hiring an overseas web design agency from the USA? Learn how remote projects run, what to check on scope, accessibility, ownership and cost before you sign.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • An overseas web design agency can serve a US business well when discovery, scope, content and sign-off are all handled in writing.
  • Check that the site is built for US visitors: US spelling, US contact details and forms, and accessibility checks reviewed against current guidance.
  • Our published small website price is £1,000 to £2,000 with a 3 to 5 week timeline; other currencies are quoted on request.

Hiring an overseas web design agency from the USA makes sense when the agency runs a clear remote process: a written discovery brief, designs you can comment on, a staging site to test and a launch plan you sign off. If those pieces are in place, the distance matters far less than the quality of the thinking and the clarity of the contract.

We are a UK-based studio and we work remotely with US businesses on websites, ecommerce, SEO and paid media. This guide explains how an overseas website project should run, which questions to ask, and the details that are easy to miss when your designer is in another country.

Why US businesses hire web designers abroad

Most US businesses that look overseas are not only chasing a lower price. Common reasons include:

  • A specific style or skill: they like the work and approach of a particular studio and are happy to work remotely.
  • Combined services: design, development, SEO and ads in one team, so the site is built to be found and to convert.
  • Extended working hours: the time difference means changes can be made while the US team sleeps.
  • International plans: a UK or European team understands audiences there if expansion is on the cards.

Whatever the reason, choose the agency on the same basis you would choose a local one. Our guide on how to choose a web designer lists the questions that matter in any country.

Hiring an overseas web design agency: how the project runs

A remote project needs more writing and fewer meetings. Every decision should be captured so nobody relies on memory or a call recording. Here is a typical flow for a small business site.

Discovery is where remote projects succeed or fail

When you cannot sit in the same room, the brief does the heavy lifting. A good overseas agency will spend real time understanding your business before showing a single design. Expect questions about:

  • Who your customers are and what they need to know before they get in touch or buy.
  • Which services or products make the most money and deserve the most prominence.
  • Which competitors you admire, and which you want to look nothing like.
  • What the website must do: take bookings, generate quotes, sell products or build trust.
  • Who on your side approves designs and copy.

Our article on the website discovery workshop shows what this stage produces. If an agency skips it and goes straight to a template, the site will look fine but may not do the job.

Details to get right for US visitors

An overseas designer can build a site that feels completely local to American visitors, but only if the details are checked deliberately.

  • Spelling and phrasing: we write in UK English for our own site, but your pages should use US spelling and the terms your customers use. Put this in the brief.
  • Contact details and forms: US phone formats, state fields, ZIP codes and time zones on booking tools.
  • Dates, measurements and currency: month-first dates, imperial units where your audience expects them and prices in dollars.
  • Privacy and cookie notices: privacy rules differ by state, so check current official guidance or ask your lawyer to approve the final text.
  • Accessibility: many US businesses are concerned about accessibility complaints. Ask how the agency checks contrast, headings, keyboard use, alt text and form labels. Our website accessibility guide covers the practical checks, and your lawyer can advise on legal duties.
  • Hosting location and speed: make sure the site loads quickly for US visitors, ideally with hosting or a content delivery network that serves North America well.

Giving feedback from another time zone

Feedback is where remote projects most often slow down. A few simple habits keep the work moving:

  • Collect feedback in one place: comments on the design file or a shared document, not scattered across email, text messages and calls.
  • Consolidate before sending: if several people review, one person should merge the comments and resolve contradictions first.
  • Describe the problem, not only the fix: "visitors will not see our phone number on mobile" is more useful than "make the header bigger".
  • Use screenshots: mark up exactly what you mean, including the device and browser you used.
  • Set review deadlines: agree how many working days each review round takes, so the timeline stays realistic.

Handled well, the time difference means you send feedback at the end of your day and wake up to a revised version.

Contracts, payment and ownership

Cross-border work needs a clear contract. Before you sign, confirm the following in writing:

  1. Ownership: the domain, hosting account, website files and designs should be yours once the final invoice is paid. See our guide to website ownership and handover.
  2. Scope: the number of pages, templates, revisions and integrations included.
  3. Exclusions: copywriting, photography, logo design or ongoing SEO, if they are not included.
  4. Currency and payment stages: our published prices are in pounds, and other currencies are quoted on request.
  5. Change requests: how extra work is quoted once the scope is signed.
  6. Aftercare: a support window after launch, and what a monthly support plan would include.

Ask for that last point in writing too, including response times and what counts as an urgent fix, so there are no surprises once the site is live.

Agency or freelancer overseas?

You will find individual freelancers as well as studios abroad. A freelancer can be a good fit for a small, tightly defined site. An agency is usually the safer choice when you need design, development, SEO and support together, or when you cannot afford a single point of failure if someone is ill or unavailable. Our comparison of freelancer vs agency for a website goes into the trade-offs.

When comparing quotes from different countries, line them up by scope. A cheaper quote that leaves out discovery, testing or accessibility checks will cost more once those gaps are filled.

Red flags to watch for

  • No discovery stage, or a design shown before any questions are asked.
  • The domain or hosting registered in the agency's name.
  • No staging site, so you only see the website once it is live.
  • Vague answers about accessibility, privacy pages or who approves them.
  • No named contact and no agreed response times.
  • Promises of rankings or sales figures before any research has been done.

FAQs

Is hiring an overseas web design agency from the USA a good idea?

It can be, if the agency runs a clear remote process with a written brief, staging site and contract that leaves ownership with you. Judge the process, not the location.

How do time zones work with a UK web design agency?

The UK is usually five hours ahead of the US East Coast and eight ahead of the West Coast. Calls happen in the UK afternoon, which is the US morning, with written updates in between.

Will my website use US spelling?

It should. Agree in the brief that all customer-facing copy uses US spelling and phrasing, even if the agency uses UK English internally.

Can an overseas agency make my website accessible?

An agency can design and build with accessibility checks such as contrast, headings, keyboard use and alt text. For legal duties, check current official guidance or ask your lawyer.

Who owns the website when the project ends?

You should. The domain, hosting, files and designs should be in your name or transferred to you once the final invoice is paid.

Can I be invoiced in US dollars?

Our published prices are in pounds sterling. Other currencies, including US dollars, are quoted on request.
