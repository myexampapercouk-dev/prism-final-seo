Website Hero Section Design: Headlines, Images and Calls to Action That Earn Attention

How to design a website hero section that explains what you do in seconds, with guidance on headlines, imagery, calls to action, mobile layout and speed.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • The hero section is the first thing visitors see, so its main job is to make clear what you offer and who it is for.
  • A specific headline, a supporting line, one primary call to action and relevant imagery usually do more than sliders or vague slogans.
  • Design the hero for mobile and for page speed from the start, then test changes rather than guessing.

The hero section is the large block at the top of a web page, usually containing a headline, a short supporting line, an image or video and a button. On most sites it is the first thing a visitor sees before they scroll, and it shapes whether they keep reading or head back to the search results.

Because it is so prominent, the hero tends to attract a lot of opinions during a website project. Everyone has a view on the photo, the slogan or the colour of the button. This guide sets out what a hero section is for, the elements that usually matter, common mistakes and how to approach the hero differently on homepages, service pages and online stores.

What is a hero section for?

A hero section has one central job: to help a visitor understand, quickly, whether they are in the right place. Someone arriving from a search, an advert or a social post has a question in mind. The hero should answer the first part of it, which is usually some version of "What is this, and is it for me?"

Secondary jobs include setting the tone of the brand, pointing visitors towards the next step and reassuring them that the business is credible. These matter, but they should support the main job rather than compete with it. A beautiful hero that leaves people unsure what you sell has not done its work.

The core elements of a hero section

The headline

The headline carries more weight than anything else in the hero. A good one says what you offer in plain words, ideally with a hint of who it is for or what the visitor gets. Clever wordplay and abstract slogans can sound good in a meeting but often leave a first-time visitor guessing.

Compare a headline such as "Elevating possibilities" with "Bespoke oak furniture made to order in the UK". The second is less poetic, but a visitor knows instantly whether it is relevant. If you want personality, it can come through in the supporting line, the imagery and the rest of the page.

Our guide to website copywriting covers writing for clarity in more depth.

The supporting line

Beneath the headline, one or two short sentences can add detail: the main benefit, the type of customer you serve, the area you cover or what makes your approach different. Keep it brief. If the supporting text runs to a paragraph, it is probably trying to do the job of the rest of the page.

The primary call to action

Most heroes benefit from one clear primary action, such as "Shop the collection", "Book a consultation" or "Get a quote". The button text should describe what happens when someone clicks it. Generic labels such as "Learn more" or "Submit" give visitors less reason to act.

A secondary action can work, for example "See our work" alongside "Get in touch", but give it less visual weight so the two do not compete. Several equally prominent buttons tend to dilute attention.

Imagery or video

The visual should reinforce the message. For a product business, that might be the product in use. For a service business, it might be the work, the setting or the people the service is for. Generic stock images of handshakes and smiling office workers rarely add much, and visitors may recognise them from other sites.

Background video can set a mood, but it adds weight to the page and can distract from the headline. If you use it, keep it short, silent by default, compressed and paired with a still fallback image. Our guide to using video on your website covers the trade-offs.

Trust cues

Some heroes include a small trust element near the call to action, such as a review rating, an accreditation the business genuinely holds or a short line about delivery or guarantees. These can help, provided they are true and current. Our guide to website trust signals explains how to use them without overloading the page.

Common hero section mistakes

Rotating sliders

Carousels that rotate through several messages are a common way to keep different stakeholders happy, because everyone gets a slide. The problem is that visitors usually see only the first one, may find the movement distracting and can struggle to read text before it changes. Automatic movement can also cause accessibility issues for some users. One strong, focused message is usually a better starting point.

Vague headlines

Phrases such as "Welcome to our website", "Solutions for tomorrow" or the company name on its own tell visitors very little. If the headline could sit on a competitor's site without changing a word, it is probably too generic.

Text over busy images

White text over a detailed photograph can be hard to read, especially on small screens. Overlays, solid panels behind text or simpler images help. Check colour contrast against accessibility guidance, which our website accessibility guide covers.

Oversized heroes that hide everything else

A hero that fills the entire screen can look striking, but it may push all other content out of view. Some visitors will scroll; others may assume there is nothing more. Showing a hint of the next section can encourage people to keep going.

Heavy files that slow the page

The hero image is often the largest element on the page and is frequently the item that determines how quickly the main content appears. Uncompressed images, large videos and multiple web fonts can slow things down noticeably, particularly on mobile connections. Google's Core Web Vitals include a measure of how quickly the largest visible element loads, and the hero is often that element. See our guides to Core Web Vitals and image optimisation.

Designing the hero for mobile

On a phone, the hero has far less room. A layout that looks balanced on a wide desktop screen can turn into a tall stack where the headline sits beneath a large image and the button falls below the visible area.

A few principles help:

  • Prioritise the headline and button. Make sure both are visible without scrolling on common phone sizes where possible.
  • Crop images deliberately. A wide landscape photo may need a different crop, or a different image entirely, on mobile so the subject is still clear.
  • Keep text short. A supporting line that fits on two lines on desktop may wrap to five on mobile.
  • Make buttons easy to tap. Give them enough size and spacing for a thumb.

Hero sections on different types of page

Homepages

The homepage hero has the broadest audience. It needs to summarise the business as a whole and direct people to the most common next steps. For businesses offering several services, it can help to state the overall offer in the headline and let the sections below branch into specifics. Our homepage design guide looks at the full page structure.

Service pages

A service page hero can be much more specific, because the visitor has usually chosen a particular topic. The headline can name the service directly, and the call to action can relate to that service, such as booking a consultation for that particular need. See our guide to service page design.

Landing pages for adverts

When someone clicks an advert, the hero should continue the message they saw. If the advert mentions a particular product or offer, the headline should reflect it so the visitor feels they have landed in the right place. Our landing page design guide explores this message match in detail.

Online stores

Ecommerce homepages often use the hero to promote a collection, a seasonal range or a key product. That can work well, but it is worth keeping the overall proposition visible too, as a first-time visitor may not know what the store sells. Category pages usually need only a slim banner or none at all, so products appear quickly. Our category page design guide covers this.

How to approach hero design in a project

Rather than starting with the image, it can help to start with words. Write down who the page is for, what they are likely to be looking for and what you want them to do next. Then draft several headline options and test them against those answers. Once the message is clear, choose imagery and layout that support it.

Some practical steps:

  • Review the search terms, adverts and links that bring people to the page so the hero matches their intent.
  • Look at the hero on a real phone, not just a resized desktop browser.
  • Ask someone unfamiliar with the business to look at the hero for a few seconds and then explain what the company does.
  • Check page speed tools after adding images or video.
  • If you have enough traffic, test alternative headlines or calls to action rather than relying on opinion. Our conversion rate optimisation guide explains how testing works.

Measuring whether the hero works

No single metric tells you whether a hero section is effective, but several can point in the right direction. Analytics can show how many visitors scroll beyond the first screen, click the primary call to action or leave without interacting. Heatmap and session-recording tools, where used in line with your privacy notice and cookie consent, can show where attention goes. Changes in these numbers after a redesign are worth reviewing, bearing in mind that seasonality and traffic sources also affect results. Our GA4 setup guide explains how to track key actions.

FAQs

What is a hero section on a website?

It is the large area at the top of a page, usually containing a headline, supporting text, a call to action and an image or video. It is often the first thing visitors see.

Should I use a slider in my hero section?

Usually a single, focused message is a better starting point. Sliders can split attention, and many visitors only see the first slide.

How long should a hero headline be?

There is no fixed rule, but short and specific tends to work well. It should state what you offer clearly enough that a new visitor understands it at a glance.

Is background video a good idea?

It can be, if it supports the message and is kept light. Video adds file weight and can distract, so use a still fallback image and check page speed.

Does the hero section affect SEO?

Indirectly. The headline often forms the main heading of the page, and a heavy hero image can slow loading, which affects user experience and Core Web Vitals.
