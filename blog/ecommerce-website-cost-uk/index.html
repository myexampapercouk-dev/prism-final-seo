How Much Does an Ecommerce Website Cost in the UK?

Ecommerce website costs in the UK explained: build prices, platform fees, apps, payments, hosting and maintenance, plus how to get an accurate quote.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • An ecommerce website has two kinds of cost: the one-off build and the ongoing running costs. Budget for both.
  • Product volume, custom features, integrations and design complexity are what move a quote up or down.
  • A clear brief with your product count, must-have features and integrations is the fastest way to an accurate price.

An ecommerce website in the UK can cost anything from a few hundred pounds for a do-it-yourself template to tens of thousands for a large, custom-built store. For most small and medium-sized businesses using a professional agency on Shopify or WooCommerce, the build sits in the low thousands, plus ongoing monthly costs for the platform, apps, hosting and support.

As a reference, our own published starting price for an ecommerce site is £1,500–£3,500, with product volume and apps scoped separately. This guide explains what sits behind numbers like these, so you can budget properly and compare quotes fairly.

One-off build costs vs running costs

It helps to separate the two from the start.

  • Build costs are paid once: planning, design, development, product set-up, testing and launch.
  • Running costs continue every month or year: platform subscription or hosting, domain, paid apps or plugins, payment processing fees, maintenance and marketing.

Comparing quotes only on the build price can be misleading. A cheaper build that relies on several paid apps can cost more over two years than a slightly dearer build with fewer subscriptions.

What makes an ecommerce build cost more?

Number of products and variants

Ten products with no variants can be added by hand quickly. Thousands of products with sizes, colours and bundles need data imports, cleaning and careful filtering. This is why product volume is usually scoped separately.

Custom design

A well-chosen theme adapted to your brand is faster and cheaper than a fully custom design. Custom design makes sense when your brand or product presentation genuinely needs it.

Features and functionality

Subscriptions, product configurators, trade pricing, wholesale accounts, gift cards, bundles and multi-currency all add time, either in set-up or in custom development.

Integrations

Connecting your store to stock systems, accounting software, ERP, marketplaces or shipping platforms adds complexity. Each integration needs to be configured and tested.

Migration from an existing store

Moving products, customers and orders from another platform, and mapping redirects so you keep your rankings, is a project in its own right. See our guide to redesigning a website without losing rankings.

Shopify vs WooCommerce costs

Shopify has predictable monthly plan costs, with hosting, security and checkout included, and many features added through paid apps. WooCommerce is free software but needs hosting, often premium extensions, and regular maintenance. Neither is automatically cheaper; it depends on what your store needs. Our full comparison of Shopify vs WooCommerce for UK businesses covers this in detail.

UK-specific costs to remember

  • Payments. Card processing, PayPal and buy-now-pay-later providers such as Klarna and Clearpay each charge fees.
  • Delivery. Shipping apps or integrations for Royal Mail, Evri, DPD and others may carry their own subscriptions.
  • Legal pages and consent. A privacy notice, cookie consent tool and terms covering returns and cancellation rights are essential. Some consent tools have a monthly fee.
  • VAT. Prices for consumers should be shown including VAT where you are VAT registered. Get your accountant to confirm the set-up.

DIY, freelancer or agency?

A do-it-yourself build on Shopify can be the right first step for a very small catalogue and a tight budget. A freelancer can be cost-effective for a defined build. An agency usually costs more than a freelancer but brings design, development, SEO and marketing together, which matters if you want the store to be found and to convert, not just to exist.

Whoever you choose, ask what happens after launch. Many stores struggle not because the build was poor, but because nobody is responsible for speed, updates and marketing afterwards.

Three example scenarios

To make this more concrete, here is how scope tends to differ between three common types of UK online store. These are illustrations of what changes the work involved, not quotes.

A small independent shop

A gift shop or local brand with 30 to 80 products, simple variants and UK-only delivery. The store can usually be built on an adapted theme, with products added from a spreadsheet, standard card and wallet payments, flat-rate or free-over-a-threshold delivery, and a handful of essential apps or plugins. This is the kind of project that sits at the lower end of a typical ecommerce budget.

A growing product brand

A fashion, beauty or homeware brand with several hundred products, multiple variants, a buy-now-pay-later option, reviews, an email marketing integration and a blog for buying guides. The extra work is in product data, filtering, design for collection pages, integrations and making sure the store loads quickly with lots of images. Budgets rise accordingly, and ongoing app costs become more significant.

A trade or B2B seller

A supplier selling to both trade and retail customers, with account-based pricing, minimum order quantities, VAT-exempt or reverse-charge scenarios and integration with stock or accounting software. These stores need careful planning and testing, and often custom development. They are usually quoted after a scoping session rather than from a price list.

Seeing where your store sits on this spectrum is the quickest way to understand whether a quote is reasonable.

Where it is worth spending more

If budget is tight, prioritise the things that directly affect sales: a fast mobile experience, clear product pages with good photography, a simple checkout with familiar payment options, and obvious delivery and returns information. Decorative extras can wait. It is also worth paying for proper tracking from day one, so you know which marketing works once you start spending on it.

Hidden costs to ask about

  • Revisions beyond the agreed number.
  • Premium apps or plugins the design depends on.
  • Content writing and product photography.
  • Training for your team.
  • Post-launch support and bug fixes.
  • SEO set-up, including redirects, structured data and Search Console.

A good quote spells these out. If it does not, ask before you sign.

Running costs: a simple way to budget

When budgeting the first year, list every recurring item with its monthly or annual cost: platform plan or hosting, domain, theme renewal if applicable, each paid app or plugin, consent tool, email marketing tool and maintenance. Add an estimate for payment processing based on expected sales. Then add a marketing budget. Seeing the full picture early avoids the common surprise of a store that costs more to run than expected.

How long does an ecommerce build take?

A standard website typically takes 3–5 weeks, while ecommerce builds range from 5–12 weeks depending on catalogue size and custom functionality. Delays most often come from waiting for content, product data and decisions, so having these ready speeds things up.

How to get an accurate quote

Write a short brief that covers:

  1. What you sell, how many products and variants, and where your product data lives now.
  2. Your preferred platform, if you have one.
  3. Must-have features and integrations.
  4. Design expectations: adapted theme or custom design, with examples you like.
  5. Your timeline and budget range.
  6. Who will manage the store and marketing after launch.

With that, an agency can give you a quote you can trust and compare. You can see our project starting prices on our pricing page and what is included in our website and ecommerce development service.

Budgeting for growth after launch

Launching the store is the start. Plan a marketing budget for the first months, whether that is SEO to build organic visibility, Google Shopping ads for immediate sales, or both. FAQs

How much does a small ecommerce website cost in the UK?

Our published starting price for an ecommerce site is £1,500–£3,500, with product volume and apps scoped separately. Running costs for the platform, apps and support are extra.

What are the monthly costs of an online store?

Typically a platform plan or hosting, paid apps or plugins, payment processing fees and optional maintenance. The total depends on the features you use.

Is Shopify cheaper than WooCommerce?

Not necessarily. Shopify has predictable plan and app costs; WooCommerce needs hosting, extensions and maintenance. Compare the total over two to three years.

How long does it take to build an ecommerce website?

Ecommerce builds typically range from 5–12 weeks depending on catalogue size and custom functionality.

Do I need to pay a deposit?

Most agencies take a deposit before starting. We collect deposits for projects, with the remainder agreed in the proposal.
