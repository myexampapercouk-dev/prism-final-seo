WordPress Security UK: A Practical Guide for Business Websites

WordPress security guide for UK businesses: updates, hosting, logins, plugins, backups, forms and what to do if your WordPress website is ever compromised.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most WordPress security problems come from outdated software, weak logins and poorly chosen plugins rather than WordPress itself.
  • Good hosting, regular updates, tested backups and fewer plugins reduce risk more than any single security tool.
  • Have a written plan for what to do if the site is compromised, including who to call and where your backups live.

WordPress security is mostly about good habits. WordPress itself is a mature, widely used platform with an active security team, but the websites built on it are only as safe as the way they are hosted, updated and managed. For UK businesses, a hacked website can mean lost enquiries, damaged trust, search engine warnings and, if personal data is involved, data protection obligations. The good news is that most common risks can be reduced with sensible, repeatable steps.

This guide is written for business owners, marketing managers and anyone responsible for a WordPress or WooCommerce website. It explains where the real risks come from, the basics every site should have in place and what to do if something goes wrong. It is general guidance rather than a security audit of your own site.

Why WordPress sites get targeted

WordPress powers a very large share of the web, which makes it an attractive target. Most attacks are not personal. Automated bots scan huge numbers of sites looking for known weaknesses: an old plugin with a published vulnerability, a login page that accepts unlimited password guesses, or a server running outdated software. If your site has one of those weaknesses, it can be found and exploited without anyone ever looking at your business specifically.

That is why small business sites are not safe simply because they are small. A compromised site can be used to send spam, host phishing pages, redirect visitors to unwanted sites or inject hidden links. Often the owner only finds out when a customer complains, a browser shows a warning or search traffic drops.

Where the real risks come from

When WordPress sites are compromised, the cause usually falls into a handful of categories:

  • Outdated plugins and themes. Extensions are the most common weak point. Vulnerabilities are found and fixed regularly, but a fix only protects you once it is installed.
  • Weak or reused passwords. Accounts with simple passwords, shared logins or passwords reused from other services are easy targets.
  • Abandoned or untrusted software. Plugins that are no longer maintained, or premium plugins downloaded from unofficial sources, can carry hidden problems.
  • Poor hosting. Cheap, overcrowded or badly configured hosting can expose sites to risks that have nothing to do with WordPress.
  • Too many people with too much access. Former staff, old agencies and freelancers who still have administrator accounts all widen the risk.

Notice that WordPress core is rarely the problem on its own. The platform is regularly updated, and minor security releases are often applied automatically. The surrounding ecosystem is where most of the effort needs to go.

Keep everything up to date

Updates are the single most important habit. That means WordPress core, every plugin, your theme and the PHP version your server runs. Each update can include security fixes, and attackers often move quickly once a vulnerability is made public.

Updating should still be done carefully. On a busy site, particularly an online shop, an update can occasionally conflict with another plugin or with custom code. Sensible practice is to take a backup first, test larger updates on a staging copy where possible, and check key pages and forms afterwards. If a plugin has not been updated by its developer for a long time, consider replacing it.

Choose plugins and themes carefully

Every plugin adds code to your site, and every piece of code is something that needs to be maintained. A lean site is usually a safer site. Before installing anything, ask whether you really need it and whether the same job could be done another way.

When you do choose a plugin, look at when it was last updated, how widely it is used, whether it is compatible with your version of WordPress and whether the developer responds to support questions. Only download premium plugins and themes from the official developer. So-called nulled versions shared for free elsewhere are a well-known route for malware.

Remove plugins and themes you are not using. A deactivated plugin can still contain vulnerable files, so deleting it is better than switching it off.

Lock down logins and user accounts

The WordPress login page is a constant target for automated password guessing. A few simple measures make a big difference:

  • Use long, unique passwords for every account, ideally stored in a password manager.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication for administrators and anyone who can publish or manage orders.
  • Limit repeated failed login attempts so bots cannot guess endlessly.
  • Avoid obvious usernames such as admin.
  • Give each person their own account rather than sharing a single login.

Review user accounts regularly. WordPress has different roles, such as administrator, editor, author and shop manager. Give people the lowest level of access they need to do their job, and remove accounts for anyone who no longer works on the site, including past agencies and freelancers.

Hosting matters more than you think

Your hosting provider controls much of the environment your site runs in: server software, PHP versions, firewalls, isolation from other sites and backup options. Good managed WordPress hosting can take care of a lot of the underlying security work. Very cheap shared hosting may leave more of it to you.

When comparing hosts, look for current PHP versions, free SSL certificates, automatic backups stored separately from the server, malware scanning, a web application firewall and responsive support. Make sure your site runs entirely over HTTPS so data sent through forms and checkouts is encrypted.

Also check who controls the hosting account and the domain. Both should be registered in your business's name, with login details you hold, not tied to an individual who might leave.

Backups: your safety net

No security setup is perfect, so backups are essential. A good backup lets you restore a working version of your site if something goes wrong, whether that is a hack, a failed update or a simple mistake.

Backups should be automatic, include both the files and the database, and be stored somewhere other than the same server as the website. How often you back up depends on how often your site changes. A shop taking orders every day needs more frequent backups than a brochure site that changes once a month.

Most importantly, test that backups can actually be restored. A backup that has never been tested is a hope rather than a plan.

Forms, data and UK GDPR

Many WordPress sites collect personal data through contact forms, newsletter sign-ups, bookings or checkout. Under UK data protection law, businesses must keep personal data secure and may need to report certain breaches. Practical steps include only collecting the data you need, not storing form submissions on the website longer than necessary, using spam protection on forms and making sure plugins that handle data are well maintained.

If you take payments, use a reputable payment provider so card details are handled by them rather than stored on your site. This guide is not legal advice, so take professional advice on your own obligations.

Monitoring and early warning signs

The sooner a problem is spotted, the easier it is to fix. Signs that something may be wrong include unexpected redirects, strange new pages or users, spam links in your content, sudden slowdowns, warnings in browsers or in Google Search Console, and emails from your host about unusual activity.

Uptime monitoring, security scanning and keeping an eye on Google Search Console can all help you catch issues early, often before customers notice anything.

What to do if your WordPress site is hacked

If you think your site has been compromised, stay calm and work methodically:

  • Contact your hosting provider and whoever looks after your website.
  • Change passwords for WordPress, hosting, FTP or SFTP, the database and any connected email accounts.
  • Avoid making lots of changes before the issue is understood, as you may remove useful evidence.
  • Clean the site properly or restore a known clean backup, then update everything that was out of date.
  • Check for unfamiliar administrator accounts and remove them.
  • If Google has flagged the site, request a review in Search Console once it is clean.
  • Consider whether personal data may have been affected and whether you have reporting obligations.

Restoring a backup without fixing the original weakness often leads to the same problem returning, so finding the cause matters.

Security and SEO

Security problems can quickly become search problems. Injected spam pages, malicious redirects and browser warnings can damage rankings and click-through rates, and cleaning up can take time. A secure, well-maintained site protects your search visibility as well as your customers. If your site has been affected, our technical SEO audit checklist explains how to check for lasting damage.

A simple WordPress security checklist

  • WordPress core, plugins, themes and PHP kept up to date.
  • Unused plugins and themes deleted.
  • Strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication for key accounts.
  • User accounts reviewed and old ones removed.
  • Reputable hosting with HTTPS, firewall and malware scanning.
  • Automatic off-site backups that have been tested.
  • Hosting and domain owned by the business.
  • A written plan for what to do if the site is compromised.

FAQs

Is WordPress secure for a business website?

Yes, WordPress can be a secure choice when it is well hosted, kept up to date and managed sensibly. Most problems come from outdated plugins, weak passwords and poor hosting rather than the core software.

Do I need a security plugin?

A reputable security plugin can help with login protection, scanning and firewall rules, but it is not a substitute for updates, good hosting and backups. Some managed hosts provide similar protection at server level.

How often should I update WordPress plugins?

Check for updates regularly and apply security updates promptly. Take a backup first and test larger updates on a staging copy where possible, especially on ecommerce sites.

Can a hacked website affect my Google rankings?

It can. Spam pages, redirects and browser warnings can reduce traffic and trust. Cleaning the site and requesting a review in Search Console, where needed, is part of recovery.

Who should own my hosting and domain?

Your business should. Keep the accounts in the business's name and make sure you hold the login details, even if an agency or freelancer manages them day to day.

Is WooCommerce less secure than a normal WordPress site?

Not inherently, but an online shop handles orders and customer data, so the stakes are higher. Updates, backups and access control deserve extra care. Our WooCommerce agency guide covers shop support in more detail.
