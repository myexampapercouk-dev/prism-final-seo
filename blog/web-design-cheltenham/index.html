What Cheltenham businesses need from a website

Cheltenham is a Regency spa town with a strong independent retail scene, a busy calendar of festivals and racing events, well-known schools, a large professional services community and a growing cyber security and technology cluster. Customers here tend to expect quality, and a dated or slow website can quietly undermine a business that is excellent in person.

  • Design that matches your brand. Clean layouts, good photography and consistent typography signal care and quality.
  • Fast mobile pages. Even premium brands lose visitors when pages load slowly on a phone.
  • Clear service pages. One focused page per core service, written in plain English.
  • A simple next step. Consultation forms, booking links or tap-to-call buttons where people expect them.
  • Verifiable trust signals. Accurate contact details, real team photos and memberships you genuinely hold.

Planning around festival and race weeks

Cheltenham's events calendar brings large numbers of visitors to the town at predictable times. For hospitality, retail and transport businesses, that creates opportunity, but only if the website is ready before demand peaks.

  • Publish event pages, packages and offers well in advance, so search engines can index them.
  • Make booking and availability obvious on every relevant page.
  • Check that the site stays fast under heavier traffic.
  • Avoid using event or organiser names in ways that suggest an official partnership you do not have.

Compliance and accessibility, in brief

Keep this general and check official guidance or your adviser for specifics.

  • UK GDPR. Collecting personal data through forms or bookings means you need a clear privacy notice.
  • PECR. Non-essential cookies generally need consent before they load, and email marketing has separate consent rules.
  • Accessibility. Readable text, strong contrast, keyboard navigation and descriptive image text.
  • Sector rules. Legal, financial and health businesses have extra rules on claims, and approval rests with you and your compliance adviser.

How to prepare for your website project

The fastest projects are the ones where the groundwork is done early. Before the discovery call, it helps to gather a few things together. None of this needs to be perfect, but having it ready saves weeks later on.

  • A list of your services. Write down every service you offer, which ones earn the most and which ones you want more of.
  • Your best customers. Describe who they are, where they come from and the questions they ask before they buy.
  • Competitor websites. Note two or three local competitors and what you like or dislike about their sites.
  • Photos and logos. Real photos of your premises, team and work are far more convincing than stock images.
  • Access details. Make sure you can log in to your domain registrar, current hosting and Google Business Profile, or know who can.
  • Existing content. If you have brochures, price lists or old web pages, share them so useful material is not lost.

We will turn this into a written brief that sets out the pages, features and timeline, so everyone agrees on the scope before design starts.

Common website mistakes we see

When businesses come to us for a new site, the same problems appear again and again. Avoiding them is often worth more than any design trend.

  • A homepage that tries to say everything. Visitors should understand what you do within a few seconds. Detail belongs on service pages.
  • Hidden contact details. If people have to hunt for a phone number or form, many will simply leave and try a competitor.
  • Thin or duplicated pages. Pages that repeat the same text with a different town name rarely help rankings and can undermine trust.
  • Oversized images. Uncompressed photos are one of the most common reasons a site feels slow on mobile.
  • No tracking. Without analytics and conversion tracking, it is impossible to tell which pages or campaigns bring enquiries.
  • Out-of-date information. Old prices, staff who have left or last year's opening hours quietly damage credibility.

Measuring whether the website works

A website should be judged on what it does for the business, not only on how it looks. We set up analytics and conversion tracking at launch, with consent respected, so you can see how many people visit, where they come from and how many go on to call, enquire, book or buy. Useful things to review each month include the pages people land on most, the searches that bring them in, which forms are submitted and how mobile visitors behave compared with desktop visitors. If a key page attracts visits but few enquiries, that is usually the first place to improve copy, layout or the call to action.

How long does a new website take?

Most small business websites take three to five weeks from an agreed brief, and online shops usually take five to twelve weeks. Content and photos are the most common cause of delay.

Can you redesign an existing website without losing rankings?

We plan redirects from old pages to new ones, keep important content and check tracking before launch. That protects as much existing visibility as possible, although some movement after a redesign is normal.

When should we launch before a busy event week?

Aim to launch several weeks or more before the event, so new pages are indexed and you have time to test bookings and enquiries before traffic peaks.

Yes. We plan clear capability, sector and contact pages for business buyers. Technical claims and any regulated wording should be checked by your own team before publishing.
