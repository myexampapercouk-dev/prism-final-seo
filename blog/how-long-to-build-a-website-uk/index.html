How Long Does It Take to Build a Website? A Practical UK Guide

What affects how long a website takes to build in the UK: scope, content, platform, feedback rounds and testing, plus how to keep your project moving.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • There is no single answer: scope, content readiness, platform, integrations and feedback speed all shape how long a website build takes.
  • Content and decisions on the client side are often what sets the pace, so preparing them early is one of the best ways to avoid delays.
  • Ask any web designer for a written schedule with stages, dependencies and a launch plan rather than a single headline date.

"How long will it take?" is one of the first questions any business asks when it commissions a website. It is a fair question. A launch date might be tied to a product release, a rebrand, a new financial year or simply the point at which an old site becomes too embarrassing to keep. The honest answer is that it depends, but that answer is only useful if you know what it depends on. This guide breaks down the stages of a typical website project, the factors that make a build quicker or slower, and the practical steps you can take to keep things moving.

It is written for UK business owners and marketing teams planning a brochure site, a service business site or an online shop. It does not give you a fixed number of weeks, because any number quoted without knowing your scope would be guesswork. Instead, it shows you how to judge whether a proposed schedule is realistic.

Why there is no single answer

Two projects that both get described as "a new website" can be very different pieces of work. A five-page site for a sole trader using an existing theme, with copy already written, is a different undertaking from a custom-designed online shop with hundreds of products, stock syncing and several payment options. The word "website" covers both.

The time a project takes is driven by four broad things: how much needs to be designed and built, how ready the content is, how many systems need to connect, and how quickly decisions get made. A schedule that ignores any of these is likely to slip.

The typical stages of a website project

Most website projects move through a similar sequence, even if the names vary between designers. Understanding the stages helps you see where time actually goes.

1. Discovery and planning

This is where goals, audiences, pages, features and priorities are agreed. It may be a short call or a structured session; our guide to the website discovery workshop explains what a good one covers. Rushing this stage often costs time later, because unclear requirements lead to rework.

2. Sitemap and content planning

A sitemap lists the pages and how they relate. Alongside it, someone needs to plan what goes on each page. Our article on website navigation structure covers how to organise pages so visitors and search engines can find what matters.

3. Design

Design might start with wireframes (simple layouts showing structure) and move to visual designs for key templates such as the homepage, a service page and a contact page. The number of templates and the number of revision rounds both affect duration.

4. Build

The approved designs are turned into a working site on a platform such as WordPress, Shopify, WooCommerce, Squarespace or Wix. Custom functionality, integrations and complex templates take longer than configuring an existing theme.

5. Content entry

Text, images, products and downloads are loaded into the site. For an online shop with a large catalogue, this can be a substantial task in its own right, especially if product data needs cleaning first.

6. Testing

The site is checked on different devices and browsers, forms and checkouts are tested, and links, speed and accessibility are reviewed. Our guide to website staging and testing explains why this deserves proper time rather than a last-minute glance.

7. Launch and aftercare

Launch involves pointing the domain to the new site, setting up redirects from old URLs, checking analytics and submitting the sitemap to Google. The days after launch are often spent fixing small issues that only appear once real visitors arrive.

Content readiness

Content is frequently what holds a project back. Designers can lay out a page with placeholder text, but a site cannot launch with it. If the copy, photography, team details, case studies or product descriptions are not ready, the build can be finished and still be waiting. Our guide to website copywriting covers how to approach writing for the web, and our website content audit article helps if you are reusing material from an existing site.

Platform choice

Hosted platforms such as Shopify, Squarespace and Wix handle hosting and much of the infrastructure, which can remove some setup work. WordPress and WooCommerce offer more flexibility but involve hosting, plugins and configuration decisions. The right platform depends on your needs, not on speed alone. Our comparison of a website builder vs a custom website explains the trade-offs.

Ecommerce complexity

An online shop adds products, variants, delivery rules, tax settings, payment gateways, transactional emails and policy pages. Each needs configuring and testing. If you are planning a shop, our Shopify store setup guide lists the pieces involved.

Redesigns and migrations

Replacing an existing site brings extra steps: auditing current content, mapping old URLs to new ones and protecting search visibility. These steps are important and should not be squeezed. Our guide to a website redesign without losing rankings explains what is at stake.

Feedback rounds

Every review stage involves waiting for feedback. If feedback arrives late, or in fragments from different people, the schedule stretches. If it changes direction after approval, work may need redoing.

How to keep your website project on track

  • Agree the scope in writing. A clear list of pages, templates and features stops the project growing unnoticed.
  • Name one decision-maker. Gather internal opinions, then have one person give consolidated feedback.
  • Start content early. Begin writing and gathering images as soon as the sitemap is agreed, not when the build is finished.
  • Book review time in your diary. Treat feedback deadlines as seriously as you expect the designer to treat theirs.
  • Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves. A smaller first launch, followed by planned improvements, can be more realistic than trying to include everything at once.
  • Get access sorted quickly. Domain, hosting, analytics and existing site logins often take longer to locate than expected. Our guide to business domain and email setup covers the basics.
  • Allow time for testing. A launch date that leaves no room for checks is a risk, not a plan.

Questions to ask about any proposed schedule

When a web designer or agency gives you a timeline, it is worth asking a few questions so you understand what it assumes:

  • What stages does the schedule include, and when does each start and finish?
  • What do you need from us, and by when, for the schedule to hold?
  • How many rounds of design revisions are included?
  • Who is responsible for writing and loading content?
  • How much time is allowed for testing before launch?
  • What happens to the schedule if we add features or pages?
  • What support is available immediately after launch?

A realistic schedule shows dependencies clearly. If it only shows a launch date with nothing behind it, ask for more detail.

Common reasons websites launch late

Delays usually come from a handful of predictable sources rather than technical surprises. Scope creep, where new pages or features are added mid-project, is one. Waiting for content is another. Changing stakeholders, where someone senior sees the design late and asks for changes, can reset work. Third-party dependencies, such as waiting for a payment provider account to be approved or a supplier to provide product data, can also add time. Knowing these in advance lets you plan around them.

Should you set a fixed launch date?

A target date is useful because it focuses everyone. But if the date is genuinely fixed, for example a trade show or a product launch, say so at the start. That allows the scope to be shaped to fit. It may mean launching a focused version first and adding further sections afterwards. Forcing a large scope into a fixed window without adjusting anything else tends to compromise quality or testing.

FAQs

Can a website be built in a week?

A very simple site using an existing template, with content fully ready, can sometimes be put together quickly. A custom design, an online shop or a redesign with migration involves more stages and usually needs longer. Be cautious of any promise that skips planning or testing.

What slows a website project down the most?

It varies by project, but content that is not ready, slow feedback and scope changes are frequent causes. Many of these are within the client's control, which is good news because they can be planned for.

Does an online shop take longer than a brochure site?

Generally, yes, because there is more to configure and test: products, variants, delivery, tax, payments, emails and policies. The size and condition of your product data matter a lot.

Is a redesign quicker than a new site?

Not necessarily. You may be able to reuse some content, but a redesign adds auditing, URL mapping and redirects to protect search visibility.

How can I speed up my website project?

Agree scope early, prepare content in parallel with design, give consolidated feedback promptly and get all logins and accounts ready before the build starts.

Should I launch before everything is finished?

Launching a solid core site and adding planned sections later can be sensible, as long as what goes live is complete, tested and accurate. Avoid launching pages with placeholder content.
