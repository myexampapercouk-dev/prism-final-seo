Brand Identity Design UK: What It Includes, How It Works and What to Budget

Brand identity design creates the logo, colours, type and visual rules that make a business recognisable. See what it includes and how UK firms should plan it.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Brand identity design is the visual system behind your business: logo, colours, typography, imagery and the rules for using them consistently.
  • Good identity work starts with strategy, so the design reflects who you serve and how you want to be seen.
  • Plan how the identity will roll out across your website, social profiles, ads and print before you sign off the design.

Brand identity design is the creation of the visual system that makes your business recognisable: your logo, colour palette, typography, imagery style and the rules for using them. For UK businesses, a good brand identity is built on clear strategy, works across website, social, ads and print, and is documented so everyone applies it consistently.

A logo on its own is not a brand identity. Many small businesses commission a logo, then find their website, social posts and sales documents still look disjointed. This guide explains what brand identity design includes, how the process usually works, what to budget for and how to make sure the result actually gets used.

What is brand identity design?

Your brand is how people think and feel about your business. Your brand identity is the set of visual and verbal tools you use to shape that. Brand identity design focuses on the visual side, although the best work is closely tied to messaging and tone of voice.

A complete visual identity usually includes:

  • Logo: the main mark plus variations for different spaces, such as a stacked version and an icon.
  • Colour palette: primary and secondary colours with exact values for screen and print.
  • Typography: typefaces for headings and body text, chosen for readability on screen.
  • Imagery style: guidance on photography, illustration and icons.
  • Layout principles: spacing, grids and how elements sit together.
  • Brand guidelines: a document showing how to apply everything correctly.

Why strategy comes before design

Design choices should answer strategic questions. Who are your customers? What do they value? How are you different from alternatives? What should people feel when they see your business? Without answers, design becomes a matter of personal taste, which is hard to judge and easy to change your mind about.

That is why brand identity design usually follows positioning work. Our brand strategy service covers positioning, messaging and identity together, and our guide to choosing a brand strategy agency explains what that work involves.

How the brand identity design process usually works

Every studio has its own approach, but most brand identity projects follow a similar path.

  1. Discovery: understanding the business, customers, competitors and goals.
  2. Positioning: agreeing what the brand stands for and how it should come across.
  3. Creative direction: exploring visual directions before committing to details.
  4. Design development: refining the chosen direction into a logo, palette, type and imagery style.
  5. Testing in context: seeing the identity on a website, social posts, ads and print items.
  6. Guidelines: documenting how to use the identity.
  7. Rollout: applying it across your channels.

Testing in context matters. A logo can look strong on a presentation board and weak as a tiny social avatar or a browser tab icon.

Designing for digital first

For most UK businesses, customers meet the brand online first: in search results, on a website, in a social feed or in an ad. A modern identity should be designed with that in mind.

  • Accessibility: text and background colours need enough contrast to be readable, including for people with visual impairments.
  • Small sizes: the logo needs a simplified version that works as a favicon or app icon.
  • Web fonts: typefaces should load quickly and be licensed for web use.
  • Flexible layouts: the identity should work on mobile screens as well as desktop.
  • Ad formats: colours and type should stand out in paid social and display placements.

Heavy custom fonts and large image files can slow a website down, which affects both user experience and search. Our guide to website speed optimisation covers this.

Brand identity and your website

Your website is usually the most important place your identity lives. If you are refreshing your brand, it often makes sense to plan the website at the same time, so the new identity is built in rather than bolted on. What does brand identity design cost?

Costs vary widely depending on scope. A simple logo refresh is a very different job from a full identity with guidelines and rollout across many channels. When comparing quotes, check:

  • Whether strategy and positioning are included or assumed.
  • How many creative directions you will see.
  • Which logo variations and file formats you will receive.
  • Whether brand guidelines are included.
  • Whether rollout to your website, social and print is included.
  • Who owns the final artwork and font licences.

Common brand identity mistakes

  • Designing by committee. Too many opinions dilute a clear idea.
  • Following trends. A trendy look can date quickly.
  • Copying competitors. If you look like everyone else, nobody remembers you.
  • Skipping guidelines. Without rules, the brand drifts within months.
  • Ignoring legal checks. Before committing, check the name and logo are not too close to existing marks. A trade mark search, ideally with help from a trade mark adviser, is a sensible first step.
  • Forgetting rollout. A new identity only works if it replaces the old one everywhere.

Making the identity stick

The real test of brand identity design is consistency over time. Share the guidelines with everyone who creates materials, including staff, freelancers and suppliers. Build templates for common items such as social posts, proposals and email signatures. Review your channels every few months and fix anything that has drifted. When new needs appear, such as a new product line or a new social platform, extend the guidelines rather than improvising, so the identity stays coherent as the business grows.

Briefing a brand identity designer

A clear brief saves time and money, and makes feedback easier because everyone is judging the work against the same goals. Before you start, write down the following.

  • Background: what the business does, who it serves and why the identity is being created or changed now.
  • Audiences: the main types of customer and what they care about.
  • Positioning: how you want to be seen and how you differ from alternatives.
  • Personality: three or four words that describe the brand's character.
  • What to keep: any existing elements with real recognition, such as a colour customers associate with you.
  • Where it will be used: website, social, ads, signage, packaging, vehicles, print.
  • Constraints: budget, timing, sector rules and anything that must or must not appear.
  • Decision makers: who gives feedback and who signs off.

When reviewing designs, judge them against the brief rather than personal taste. Ask whether the direction will appeal to your customers, whether it sets you apart and whether it will work at every size and on every channel you listed. Feedback such as "make it pop" is hard to act on; feedback such as "this feels too formal for our younger customers" gives the designer something clear to work with.

Keep the number of people giving feedback small. One or two decision makers, with input gathered from others beforehand, usually leads to a stronger and more coherent result than a large group voting on options.

FAQs

What is brand identity design UK businesses should invest in?

A visual system covering logo variations, colours, typography, imagery style and guidelines, built on clear positioning and designed to work online first.

What is the difference between a logo and a brand identity?

A logo is one element. A brand identity is the full system of visual tools and rules that make every touchpoint look and feel consistent.

How long does brand identity design take?

It depends on scope, the number of creative rounds and how quickly feedback is given. Rollout across a website adds time; a new website typically takes 3 to 5 weeks.

How much does brand identity design cost?

It varies by scope. Compare what each quote includes, such as strategy, guidelines and rollout, rather than headline prices. Branding projects are quoted to scope.

Do I need brand guidelines?

Yes, if more than one person creates materials for your business. Guidelines keep the brand consistent as it is used by staff and suppliers.

Should I redesign my website when I rebrand?

Often it makes sense, as the website is where most customers meet your brand. Plan carefully to protect existing search rankings.
