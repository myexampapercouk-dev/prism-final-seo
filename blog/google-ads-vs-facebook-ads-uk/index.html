Google Ads vs Facebook Ads: How UK Businesses Can Choose

Google Ads reaches people already searching; Facebook Ads reaches people by interest before they search. Compare intent, cost and fit for your business.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Google Ads captures existing demand from people searching now; Facebook and Instagram ads create demand by showing your offer to likely buyers.
  • Urgent, need-based services usually suit Google first, while visual, impulse or new products often suit Meta first.
  • Judge both on cost per enquiry or sale with proper tracking, not on cost per click or reach alone.

Google Ads shows your ads to people who are actively searching for what you offer, while Facebook Ads (run through Meta, which also covers Instagram) shows your ads to people based on who they are and what they are interested in, often before they have started looking. That difference in intent is the main thing to understand when choosing between them.

Both platforms can work well for UK businesses, and both can waste money if they are used for the wrong job. This guide compares Google Ads vs Facebook Ads on intent, cost, creative, targeting and tracking, then gives a simple way to decide where to start. Advertising platforms change features and policies often, so check current official help pages before you launch.

The real difference: demand capture vs demand creation

Google Ads captures demand that already exists. Someone types "emergency plumber Leeds" or "oak dining table UK" and your ad appears at the moment they want to act. Because intent is high, clicks often convert well, but you can only reach as many people as are searching.

Facebook and Instagram ads create demand. You show a product, service or offer to people who match your ideal customer but may not have thought about it yet. Reach can be much larger, and good creative can make people want something they were not looking for. The trade-off is that many viewers are not ready to buy, so you often need several touches, such as a video view, then a retargeting ad, then an offer.

A useful question is: do people already search for what I sell? If yes, Google is usually the natural starting point. If your product is new, visual or bought on impulse, Meta often has more room to grow.

These are starting points, not rules. Your own results, measured properly, should decide where budget goes next.

How costs compare

Facebook ads often have a lower cost per click than Google Ads, but that comparison can mislead. A cheap click from someone casually scrolling may be worth less than an expensive click from someone searching for your exact service. What matters is the cost per enquiry, booking or sale, and the value of each customer over time.

Both platforms run on auctions, so prices change with competition, season and audience. For budgeting help, see our guides to Google Ads costs in the UK and Meta Ads costs. Whatever you spend on ads is paid to the platform, separately from any management fee.

Creative and effort needed

Google search ads are mainly text: headlines and descriptions written around keywords, supported by assets such as sitelinks and callouts. The skill lies in keyword choice, negative keywords, bidding and landing pages. Our guide to Google Ads for small businesses explains a sensible starting structure.

Meta ads rely heavily on creative. Strong images and short videos that stop the scroll are the biggest lever, and they tire faster, so you need a steady supply of fresh versions. If you cannot produce regular visual content, Meta will be harder to sustain. Our Facebook ads starter guide covers simple creative that works on a small budget.

Tracking and measurement

Both platforms need proper tracking to optimise. On Google, that means conversion tracking for calls, forms and purchases. On Meta, it usually means the Meta Pixel and, where suitable, a server-side connection. In the UK, consent rules under UK GDPR and PECR affect how tracking tags can run, so check current ICO guidance and use a proper cookie banner.

Expect the platforms to report results differently. Each tends to credit itself for a sale when a customer saw both, so compare them in GA4 with consistent goals and UTM-tagged links rather than adding the two platforms' own figures together.

Using Google and Facebook ads together

The two platforms often work best as a pair. Meta builds awareness and interest; some of those people later search on Google, where your search ads or organic listing catch them. In the other direction, people who visited your site from Google can be shown reminder ads on Facebook and Instagram, which we explain in our remarketing guide.

If budget is limited, start with one platform, get tracking and results stable, then add the second with a clear test plan rather than splitting a small budget thinly across both from day one.

Common mistakes when comparing the two

  • Judging Meta on last-click sales alone. Social ads often start a journey that ends with a search or a direct visit, so last-click reports can undervalue them.
  • Using the same landing page for both. A searcher wants a direct answer to their query, while someone arriving from a feed may need more context, images and reassurance first.
  • Broad targeting without enough data. On Google, loose keyword matching can spend on irrelevant searches; on Meta, a tiny budget spread across many audiences rarely gives the algorithm enough to learn from.
  • Stopping too early. Both platforms have learning periods. Changing everything every few days resets that learning and makes results harder to read.
  • Ignoring the offer. Neither platform can fix an unclear offer or a slow website. Strong ads still need a page that makes taking the next step easy.

A simple way to choose where to start

  1. Check search demand. If plenty of people already search for what you offer, Google is a strong first test.
  2. Look at your product. Visual, giftable or new products usually show well on Instagram and Facebook.
  3. Assess your creative capacity. If you cannot produce regular images and video, lean towards search first.
  4. Know your numbers. Work out what you can afford to pay for a lead or sale before spending.
  5. Run a focused test. Give one platform enough budget and time to learn, review cost per result, then decide whether to add the other.

FAQs

Google Ads vs Facebook Ads: which is better?

Google Ads is usually better for reaching people already searching for a product or service, while Facebook Ads is usually better for showing visual or new products to likely buyers who are not searching yet. The better choice depends on your offer, budget and creative capacity.

Are Facebook ads cheaper than Google Ads?

Facebook clicks are often cheaper, but the people clicking are usually earlier in their buying journey. Compare cost per enquiry or sale, not cost per click, to judge which is better value for you.

Should a small business use Google Ads or Facebook Ads first?

If customers search for what you sell, start with Google Ads. If your product is visual or something people discover rather than search for, start with Facebook and Instagram. Get one working before adding the other.

Do Facebook ads also show on Instagram?

Yes. Campaigns set up in Meta Ads Manager can run on Facebook, Instagram and other Meta placements. You can let Meta choose placements or select them yourself, depending on your creative and goals.

Can I run Google Ads and Facebook Ads at the same time?

Yes, and many businesses do. Meta often builds interest that later turns into searches, while Google catches those searches. Make sure tracking is in place so you can see how the two work together.

How long should I test each platform?

Give each platform enough time and budget to gather meaningful conversion data, usually several weeks at least. Avoid judging a campaign in the first few days while it is still learning.
