Funeral Director Website Design: Calm, Clear and Compassionate

Website design for UK funeral directors: clear first steps after a death, transparent pricing, service options, contact at any hour and a calm, respectful tone.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • People visiting a funeral director's website are often grieving, stressed and short of time, so clarity and calm matter more than anything else.
  • Explaining what to do first, how to reach you at any hour and what things cost can remove a great deal of uncertainty.
  • Funeral directors in the UK are subject to price transparency requirements, so pricing information needs careful planning and checking against current rules.

Few websites are visited at a harder moment than a funeral director's. Someone may have just lost a parent, partner or child. They may be searching late at night, on a phone, unsure what they are supposed to do next. Others are planning ahead, comparing options for themselves or a relative. In every case, the website should feel calm, honest and easy to use.

This guide covers website design for UK funeral directors, including independent family firms, multi-branch businesses and those offering specialist services such as direct cremation or natural burial. It focuses on the website's structure, content and tone. Because the subject is sensitive, it leans on clarity and restraint rather than marketing technique.

Start with the person arriving in distress

The most urgent visitor is someone who needs help now. The site should make two things immediately obvious:

  • How to contact you, including a phone number that is answered at any hour if you offer that, shown clearly at the top of every page and tappable on mobile.
  • What to do first, in simple steps, depending on whether the death happened at home, in hospital, in a care home or elsewhere.

A short "When someone dies" page, linked prominently from the homepage, can be one of the most helpful things on the whole site. Keep it practical and gentle. Explain what usually happens, what the person needs to do and what you can take care of. Where official processes are involved, such as registering a death, keep the explanation general and link to official government information, because details vary across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland and can change.

Tone of voice

The writing on a funeral director's site sets the emotional tone. Aim for:

  • Plain, warm language rather than euphemism or formal jargon.
  • Short sentences and short paragraphs that are easy to take in when tired or upset.
  • Reassurance without pressure. Avoid urgency-based sales language.
  • Respect for different beliefs, cultures and family circumstances.

Read every page imagining you have just lost someone close. If anything feels cold, salesy or confusing, rewrite it. Our guide to website copywriting covers writing clearly for your audience.

Visual design

Design choices should support calm. Generally that means:

  • A restrained colour palette and generous white space.
  • Readable fonts at a comfortable size, with strong contrast.
  • Photography that feels real and respectful, such as your premises, vehicles, chapel of rest or the people who will help, rather than generic stock images.
  • No pop-ups, autoplay video or flashing elements.

Many visitors will be older, and some may have visual impairments, so accessibility is especially important. Make sure the site works with screen readers, can be navigated by keyboard and remains readable when text is enlarged. See our guides to website accessibility and colour and typography.

Pricing transparency

Cost is a real worry for many families, and it can feel uncomfortable to ask. Clear pricing information helps people make decisions without embarrassment. It is also a regulatory matter: following a Competition and Markets Authority investigation, funeral directors in the UK are required to publish certain price information, including a standardised price list, prominently on their website and in their premises. Scotland also has its own regulatory framework for funeral directors. Check the current requirements that apply to you and make sure your pricing page meets them.

Beyond the minimum, it can help to:

  • Explain what each package or service includes and does not include.
  • Separate your own fees from third-party costs, such as crematorium or cemetery fees, which you may need to show as estimates.
  • Make clear how payment works, including deposits and timings.
  • Show the price list in a format that is easy to read on a phone, not only as a downloadable document.

Our guide to pricing page design covers layout principles, though the tone here should be especially gentle and plain.

Explaining services and choices

Families often don't know what options exist. Service pages can explain them simply:

  • Traditional funerals, burial and cremation.
  • Direct cremation, if you offer it, explained honestly.
  • Religious and cultural funerals you support.
  • Non-religious and personalised ceremonies.
  • Natural or woodland burial, if relevant.
  • Repatriation, if you handle it.

Describe what you actually provide. Avoid implying services or facilities you don't have, and avoid comparisons with other funeral directors. Where you work with third parties, such as celebrants, florists or stonemasons, you can describe the help you give in arranging them without naming or promoting specific businesses unless you choose to.

Pre-paid funeral plans

If you sell or arrange pre-paid funeral plans, be aware that funeral plan providers have been regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority since 2022. This affects how plans can be promoted and who can sell them. If plans appear on your website, check that the content and any links meet current FCA requirements, and take advice if you are unsure. Keep plan information separate from the urgent "someone has died" journey so neither confuses the other.

Online notices and tributes

Many funeral directors publish funeral notices on their website, showing the date, time and place of services, sometimes with donation details or a link to a livestream. If you do this:

  • Always get the family's agreement before publishing.
  • Be mindful of security. Publishing funeral times can indicate when a home is empty, so let families decide what to include.
  • Make notices easy to find and readable on mobile.
  • Have a clear process for removing or updating notices.

Online donation pages involve payments and charity arrangements, so make sure any system you use is reputable and handles payments securely.

Introducing your team and premises

People want to know who will be looking after their loved one. An about page introducing the real team, with photos and a little about each person, can be reassuring. Photos of your premises, including the arrangement room and chapel of rest, help families know what to expect when they visit. Include clear address, parking and accessibility information for each branch.

If you belong to a professional association or follow a code of practice, you can mention it accurately. Only list memberships you actually hold. Genuine reviews can help, but think carefully about how they are displayed given the sensitivity, and never edit them to change their meaning. Our guide to website trust signals has more.

Contact forms and privacy

Some families prefer to make first contact by form or email rather than phone, especially when planning ahead. Keep forms short and say how quickly you will respond. Personal information about a death is sensitive, so ensure forms are secure, collect only what is needed and are covered by a clear privacy notice in line with UK GDPR. See our guide to website contact forms.

Local visibility

Most families choose a funeral director nearby, so local search matters. A complete Google Business Profile for each branch, with accurate hours and phone numbers, and a website that clearly names the areas you serve, can help people find you. Avoid aggressive tactics; a calm, helpful site is itself the best case for your service. See our guide to Google Business Profile.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A phone number that is hard to find on mobile.
  • No guidance on what to do first.
  • Pricing that is missing, hard to find or unclear.
  • Pushy sales language or pop-ups.
  • Stock photography that feels impersonal.
  • Publishing funeral notices without the family's agreement.

FAQs

Do funeral directors have to show prices on their website?

Funeral directors in the UK are required to publish certain price information, including a standardised price list, on their website. Check the current CMA requirements and, in Scotland, the Scottish framework.

What should a funeral director's homepage include?

A clearly visible phone number, a link to guidance on what to do when someone dies, an overview of services and easy access to pricing.

Should funeral notices be published online?

They can be helpful, but only with the family's agreement, and families should decide what details to include for privacy and security reasons.

Can funeral directors promote funeral plans on their website?

Funeral plans are regulated by the FCA, which affects how they are promoted and sold. Check current rules and take advice before featuring them.

What tone should a funeral director's website use?

Calm, warm and plain. Avoid jargon, euphemism and sales pressure, and write for someone who may be grieving and tired.
