Website Analytics for Small Businesses: What to Measure and Why

Website analytics for small business: which measures matter, how to set goals, which Google tools to use and how to turn reports into decisions.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start with what the website is for, define the actions that matter, then measure those before anything else.
  • Google Analytics shows what people do on your site; Google Search Console shows how you appear in search. Together they cover the essentials.
  • Look at trends over time and act on them; a short monthly review is more useful than a dashboard nobody opens.

Analytics tools can report hundreds of numbers. For a small business, almost all of them can be ignored most of the time. What you need is a handful of measures that tell you whether the website is doing its job, and a habit of looking at them often enough to act.

This guide explains what to measure on a small business website or online shop, which free Google tools to use, and how to turn the numbers into decisions. For the technical side of setting up Google Analytics 4, see our GA4 setup guide.

Start with what the website is for

Before looking at any report, write down the main job of your website in one sentence. Examples:

  • "Get local homeowners to request a quote."
  • "Sell our products online to UK customers."
  • "Get people to book an appointment."
  • "Show credibility to prospects who already know us, so they call."

That sentence tells you what counts as success. Everything you measure should connect back to it. A site whose job is to generate quote requests should be judged on quote requests, not on page views.

Define your key actions

Next, list the actions a visitor can take that move them towards that goal. In Google Analytics these are tracked as events, and the most important can be marked as key events. Typical examples:

Type of siteKey actions to track
Service businessContact form submitted, quote form submitted, phone number clicked, email link clicked
Online shopPurchase, add to basket, begin checkout
Booking-based businessBooking completed, booking page viewed
Any siteNewsletter sign-up, brochure or price list downloaded

Check that each action is actually being recorded. A common problem is a form that sends a thank-you message without loading a new page, so nothing is tracked unless an event has been set up for it. Submit a test enquiry and confirm it appears in the reports.

The measures worth watching

1. Key events (conversions)

The count of key actions completed. This is the most important number on the list, because it connects directly to business results. Watch the total and how it changes over time.

2. Conversion rate

Key events divided by visits. If traffic rises but enquiries stay flat, the conversion rate falls, which suggests the new visitors are less relevant or something on the site is getting in the way. Compare your own rate over time rather than worrying about benchmarks from other businesses.

3. Where visitors come from

Google Analytics groups traffic into channels such as organic search, paid search, social, referral, email and direct. Knowing which channels bring visitors who go on to enquire or buy helps you decide where to spend time and money. A channel with lots of visits but no key events may be less valuable than it looks.

4. Landing pages

The pages people arrive on first. Check which pages bring in the most visitors and whether those visitors go on to take a key action. A popular landing page with almost no enquiries is a clear candidate for improvement.

5. Engagement

GA4 reports engagement rate and average engagement time. These are useful as rough signals: a page where most visitors leave without engaging may not be matching what they searched for. Treat them as prompts to investigate, not as goals in themselves.

6. Device split

Compare desktop and mobile. If mobile visitors convert much less than desktop visitors, test the mobile experience yourself: forms, buttons, menus and load speed. Our Core Web Vitals guide explains how page experience is measured.

7. Search performance

Google Search Console shows which searches your site appears for, how often, its average position and how many people click through. It is the best free view of your organic search visibility, and it shows things Analytics cannot, such as searches where you appeared but nobody clicked.

For online shops: a few more

  • Revenue and orders by channel.
  • Average order value.
  • Checkout drop-off: how many people start checkout but do not finish.
  • Top products by views and by sales, to spot items that attract interest but do not sell.

Platforms such as Shopify, WooCommerce, Wix and Squarespace also have built-in reports for sales and orders. Use those alongside Google Analytics, but expect small differences between them, because each counts things slightly differently.

Measures that can mislead

  • Total page views. Nice to see rising, but they say little about results on their own.
  • Time on site in isolation. Longer is not always better. A visitor who finds your phone number quickly and calls has had a good visit.
  • Short-term swings. Daily numbers jump around. Compare weeks or months, and compare with the same period last year where seasonality matters.
  • Single figures without context. A number only means something compared with something else: last month, last year or another channel.

The free Google tools to use

ToolWhat it tells you
Google Analytics 4Visitors, channels, pages, key events, ecommerce data
Google Search ConsoleSearch queries, impressions, clicks, indexing and page experience issues
Google Business ProfileFor local businesses: calls, direction requests and website clicks from your listing
Google Tag ManagerOptional: manages tracking tags such as form and click events without editing site code

If you serve local customers, our Google Business Profile guide explains how to use its performance data.

Privacy and cookie consent

Analytics tools typically use cookies or similar technologies. Under UK rules, you generally need to give visitors clear information and, for non-essential cookies such as many analytics cookies, get consent. The rules have been changing, so check the latest guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) or take advice. Bear in mind that visitors who decline cookies may not appear fully in your reports, so treat numbers as indications rather than exact counts.

A simple monthly review

A dashboard nobody opens is worthless. A short, regular review is far more useful. Once a month, spend half an hour answering these questions:

  1. How many key events did we get, and how does that compare with last month and the same month last year?
  2. Which channels brought those key events?
  3. Which landing pages performed well, and which attracted visitors but no action?
  4. In Search Console, which searches are growing, and are there pages with many impressions but few clicks?
  5. Is mobile performing noticeably worse than desktop?
  6. What is one thing we will change this month as a result?

The last question is the point of the whole exercise. Write the answer down, make the change, and look at the effect next month.

Turning numbers into actions

  • Page with many impressions but few clicks in Search Console: review its title and meta description.
  • Popular landing page with few enquiries: make the next step clearer, check the form works and add trust information.
  • Checkout drop-off: test checkout yourself on mobile and check delivery costs are visible early. Our Shopify conversion guide covers this.
  • Channel with traffic but no results: check whether the audience matches what you sell before spending more on it.

When numbers suddenly change

If a measure jumps or drops sharply, check for a tracking problem before assuming anything about customers. Common causes include a tracking tag removed during a site update, a form changed so its event no longer fires, a cookie banner change affecting consent rates, or a new filter in the reports. Compare Analytics with your own records, such as enquiry emails or platform orders. If those are steady while Analytics has fallen, the cause is probably tracking rather than demand. Keeping a simple log of site changes, campaigns and tracking edits, with dates, makes these investigations much quicker, because you can line up a change in the numbers with something that happened on the same day.

FAQs

What is the most important website metric for a small business?

The number of key actions that match your website's purpose, such as enquiries, bookings or sales. Other measures help explain why that number moves.

Is Google Analytics free?

The standard version of Google Analytics 4 is free, as are Google Search Console and Google Business Profile.

Do I need both Google Analytics and Search Console?

They answer different questions. Analytics shows what visitors do on your site; Search Console shows how your site appears in Google Search. Using both gives a fuller picture.

How often should I check my website analytics?

A monthly review suits most small businesses, with a quick check after any major change, such as a redesign or a new campaign.

Why do Shopify and Google Analytics show different numbers?

They collect and count data in different ways, and visitors who decline cookies may be missing from Analytics. Look at trends in each rather than expecting an exact match.
