SEO Audit Cost in the UK: What's Included, Types of Audit and What to Do Next

SEO audit cost in the UK depends on site size, depth and audit type. See what's included, how audits differ and what to do with the findings afterwards.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 30 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • SEO audit cost depends on site size, platform complexity and depth: an automated scan is not the same as a prioritised, expert-reviewed audit.
  • A useful audit covers technical health, content, authority, local or ecommerce specifics and AI search readiness, with every issue ranked by impact.
  • The audit is only valuable if it is implemented, so budget for fixes and ask who will make the changes before you commission one.

SEO audit cost in the UK depends mainly on the size of your site, how complex the platform is and how deep the review goes: a quick automated scan costs little, while a full, expert-reviewed audit of a large ecommerce site takes significantly more time. What matters most is not the price but whether the audit gives you a prioritised, practical action plan you can actually implement.

This guide explains what an SEO audit should include, the different types of audit, what affects the price and what to do once you have the findings. If you want to run through the technical checks yourself first, use our technical SEO audit checklist.

What affects SEO audit cost in the UK

Agencies and freelancers price audits differently, but the same factors drive the effort involved.

  • Site size: a ten-page brochure site and a ten-thousand-product store need very different levels of crawling and analysis.
  • Platform: Shopify, WooCommerce, WordPress and custom builds each have their own common issues.
  • Scope: technical-only audits cost less than full audits covering content, links, local SEO and AI visibility.
  • Depth of manual review: automated tools find symptoms; experienced reviewers find causes and decide priorities.
  • Data access: audits using Google Search Console, GA4 and server logs are more accurate but take longer.
  • Deliverables: a spreadsheet of issues differs from a prioritised roadmap with developer-ready tickets and a walkthrough call.

What a good SEO audit should include

Whatever the type, a useful audit should cover the areas that actually limit your visibility and present them in a way you can act on.

Technical health

Crawlability, indexation, robots.txt, XML sitemaps, canonical tags, redirects, HTTPS, mobile usability, page speed and Core Web Vitals, structured data and JavaScript rendering.

Content and search intent

Whether key pages match what searchers want, duplicate or thin content, keyword cannibalisation, title tags and meta descriptions, headings and internal linking.

Authority and reputation

The quality and relevance of your backlinks compared with competitors, unlinked brand mentions and review profiles.

AI search readiness

Whether your content gives clear, quotable answers, your business details are consistent across the web and AI crawlers can access your pages. Our explainer on generative engine optimisation covers this in more depth.

Prioritised recommendations

Every issue ranked by likely impact and effort, with clear owners. A list of 400 warnings with no priorities is not an audit; it is a tool export.

How audit findings should be presented

The format of an audit matters almost as much as its content. The most useful audits open with a short summary for decision makers: the three to five issues most likely to be holding the site back, what fixing them involves and roughly how much effort each takes. Detailed findings then follow for whoever will implement them.

Each issue should explain what the problem is, which URLs or templates are affected, why it matters for search and what the fix looks like. Where a developer is needed, the recommendation should be specific enough to turn into a ticket without another meeting. Examples help: a sample of affected URLs and screenshots from Search Console make it far easier to agree priorities.

Finally, a good audit separates genuine problems from noise. Tools often report hundreds of low-priority warnings that make little difference to rankings. An experienced reviewer will say plainly which ones can be ignored, so your team spends its time on changes that matter.

When to commission an audit

Common triggers include an unexplained drop in organic traffic, a planned redesign or platform move, a change of agency, a new market or product line, or simply not having had a proper review in over a year. An audit is also a sensible first step before signing up to any ongoing SEO programme, because it shows where the effort should go.

Is a cheap SEO audit worth it?

A low-cost automated report can be a useful starting point, but it has limits. Tools flag every missing alt attribute with the same urgency as a noindex tag on your best category page. They cannot tell whether a page is cannibalising another, whether your faceted navigation is wasting crawl budget, or whether a redirect chain is costing you rankings.

Watch for these red flags:

  • A "free audit" that is really a sales document with generic findings.
  • No access requested to Google Search Console or GA4.
  • No prioritisation or explanation of business impact.
  • Scare tactics about "toxic links" with a disavow package bundled in.
  • Guarantees of ranking improvements once fixes are made.

SEO audit checklist: questions to ask before you commission one

  1. Which audit type does my site actually need right now?
  2. Will the auditor use Search Console, GA4 and, where relevant, server logs?
  3. Is manual expert review included, or is it a tool output?
  4. Will issues be prioritised by impact and effort?
  5. Are recommendations specific enough for a developer to action?
  6. Is there a walkthrough call to explain the findings?
  7. Does it cover AI search readiness as well as traditional SEO?
  8. Who will implement the fixes, and has that been budgeted?
  9. Will the audit include a re-check after fixes are made?

What to do after an SEO audit

The audit is only the diagnosis. Its value comes from what happens next.

  1. Fix critical blockers first: anything stopping important pages being crawled or indexed.
  2. Tackle quick wins: title tags, internal links and redirects that are low effort and high impact.
  3. Plan larger changes: template fixes, site speed work and content consolidation, scheduled with your developer.
  4. Improve key content: strengthen the pages closest to converting, such as service and category pages.
  5. Build authority: once the site is sound, earn links and mentions through digital PR and content.
  6. Measure and re-audit: track progress in Search Console and re-check after major changes.

FAQs

What is the typical SEO audit cost in the UK?

It varies with site size, platform and depth. An automated scan costs little, while a full manual audit of a large ecommerce site takes considerably more time. Ask for a clear scope and deliverables before comparing quotes.

How long does an SEO audit take?

A small site audit can take a few days, while large or complex sites may take several weeks, especially when log files and detailed content analysis are included.

How often should I get an SEO audit?

A full audit once a year suits most businesses, with lighter checks after major site changes, migrations or unexplained traffic drops.

Can I do an SEO audit myself?

Yes, for the basics. Our technical SEO audit checklist and free tools such as Google Search Console will catch many common issues, though prioritising complex problems benefits from experience.

What is the difference between a technical audit and a full SEO audit?

A technical audit covers crawling, indexation, speed and site structure. A full audit adds content, backlinks, local or ecommerce factors, competitors and AI search readiness.

Do I need an audit before starting SEO?

It is strongly recommended. Without one, you risk spending on content or links while technical issues hold your site back.
