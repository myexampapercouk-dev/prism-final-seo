Sports Club Website Design UK: Membership, Fixtures and Volunteer-Friendly Sites

Sports club website design guide for UK clubs: membership pages, fixtures and results, junior sections, safeguarding, sponsors and easy volunteer updates.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • A sports club website must be easy for volunteers to update, or it will quickly fall out of date.
  • New members need a clear route in: what the club offers, who it is for, when sessions run and how to join.
  • Junior sections need clear safeguarding information and careful handling of children's images and data.

A sports club website has to welcome new members, keep existing members informed and be simple enough for volunteers to maintain. Good sports club website design gives newcomers a clear route to join, puts fixtures, training times and news where members can find them, and treats junior sections, safeguarding and sponsors with care.

This guide applies to UK community sports clubs such as football, rugby, cricket, hockey, tennis, golf, running, swimming and athletics clubs. Commercial gyms and fitness studios have different needs, covered in our gym website design guide.

The particular challenge of club websites

Most community sports clubs are run by volunteers. Committee members change, the person who built the site moves on and the password gets lost. Many club websites end up with fixtures from two seasons ago and a contact email nobody checks.

That shapes the design brief. The most important features of a club website are often not visual. They are:

  • Simple editing that a non-technical volunteer can learn quickly.
  • Shared access, so the site does not depend on one person.
  • Clear ownership of the domain, hosting and accounts, held by the club rather than an individual.
  • Low ongoing maintenance.

Our website maintenance guide covers what needs looking after once a site is live.

Who the website serves

  • Prospective members. Adults or parents looking for a club, often comparing a few options.
  • Current members and parents. Checking training times, fixtures, results, kit and news.
  • Volunteers and coaches. Finding documents, rotas and contacts.
  • Sponsors and local businesses. Seeing how the club presents itself and its supporters.
  • Visitors and opposing teams. Looking for directions, parking and facilities.

Make joining easy

For many clubs, recruiting and keeping members is the main reason to have a website. The join page should answer the questions newcomers actually ask:

  • Do I need experience, or are beginners welcome?
  • Can I try a session before committing?
  • When and where does training happen?
  • What do I need to bring?
  • How does membership work, and where can I see the fees?
  • Who do I contact?

A "Join us" or "Try a session" button on every page keeps that route visible. If your club uses an online membership or payment system, link to it clearly rather than rebuilding it inside the website. Our online booking systems guide covers how websites and booking tools fit together.

Fixtures and results without the workload

Fixtures and results are among the most visited parts of a club site, and the most likely to go stale. Some approaches reduce the work:

  • Link or embed from the league system. Many leagues and governing bodies publish fixtures and results online. Linking to or embedding these, where permitted, avoids double entry.
  • Keep match reports optional. Short reports add life to the site, but the site should not look abandoned if one is missed.
  • Archive old seasons. Move past fixtures into an archive so the current season is easy to find.

Social media often carries the day-to-day news. A feed or link on the website can keep it looking current, but the key information, such as training times and how to join, should live on the site itself, where people can always find it.

Junior sections and safeguarding

Parents of young players want to know their children are in safe hands. A clear junior section helps, but it needs care.

  • Safeguarding information. Publish your safeguarding policy or a summary, and name your welfare or safeguarding officer and how to contact them, if your club has that role. Your national governing body may have specific expectations, so check its guidance.
  • Coach information. Describe coaching qualifications and checks accurately. Do not imply every coach holds a qualification or check unless that is the case.
  • Images of children. Many governing bodies publish guidance on photographing young people. Get clear consent from parents, avoid naming children alongside photos and think carefully about whether identifiable images are needed at all.
  • Personal data. Registration forms for juniors collect sensitive information. Your privacy notice should explain how it is handled. The Information Commissioner's Office has guidance for small organisations, and it is worth taking advice if you are unsure what applies.

Requirements can vary by sport and by governing body, so treat this as a starting point rather than a complete checklist.

Sponsors and income

Sponsorship is an important source of income for many clubs. The website can support it by giving sponsors visible, tidy recognition and by making it easy for local businesses to ask about opportunities.

  • A dedicated sponsors page with logos and links.
  • Sponsor recognition on relevant team pages, if agreed.
  • A short "Sponsor the club" page explaining how to get in touch, without promising specific exposure you cannot deliver.

Make sure sponsor logos are compressed, so a row of large images does not slow every page down. Our image optimisation guide explains how.

Facilities, venues and accessibility

Visiting teams, new members and spectators all need practical information: address, a map, parking, changing facilities, refreshments and accessibility details. Clubs that hire out facilities may also need a page for room or pitch hire with a clear enquiry route.

The website itself should be accessible too. Clear text, sufficient contrast, descriptive links and properly labelled forms help everyone, including older members and people using assistive technology. Our website accessibility guide covers the essentials.

Charitable and community status

Some clubs are registered charities or community amateur sports clubs. If yours is, you may have obligations about how you describe your status and present information. Check the relevant guidance from the Charity Commission or HMRC, as applicable, rather than assuming. If donations or fundraising are part of your plans, our charity website design guide covers related considerations.

Volunteer and member resources

A members' area or resources page can save committee members a lot of time. It might hold kit information, codes of conduct, coaching rotas, forms and the annual general meeting papers. Keep anything sensitive behind a login or out of the website altogether, and avoid publishing personal phone numbers or home addresses of volunteers. A shared club email address for each role, rather than personal addresses, also makes handovers easier when committee members change.

Platform choice for clubs

The best platform is usually the one your volunteers can manage. Squarespace and Wix can be easy to learn and require little technical upkeep. WordPress offers more flexibility for news, multiple teams, documents and integrations, but needs updates and security maintenance. Our website builder vs custom website guide and WordPress security guide can help you decide.

Whatever you choose, register the domain and hosting in the club's name, keep login details in a shared, secure place and give at least two committee members admin access.

What to ask a web designer

  • Can our volunteers update news, fixtures and training times without help?
  • Will you provide simple guidance or a handover session for new volunteers?
  • How will fixtures and results be handled with our league systems?
  • How will the site handle junior registrations and images of children?
  • Who will own the domain, hosting and accounts?
  • What ongoing maintenance will the site need, and who will do it?

FAQs

What is the most important feature of a sports club website?

Ease of updating. A simple site that volunteers keep current is more useful than an impressive one that goes stale after a season.

Should we put membership fees on the website?

Usually yes. Clear fees and what they include help newcomers decide and reduce questions to committee members.

Can we use photos of junior players?

Only with parental consent and in line with your club's policy and any governing body guidance. Avoid naming children alongside images.

Do we still need a website if we use social media?

Social media is good for news, but a website gives a stable home for key information, such as how to join, training times, policies and contacts, that people can always find through search.

Who should own the club website?

The club. Register the domain and hosting in the club's name and make sure more than one committee member has access.
