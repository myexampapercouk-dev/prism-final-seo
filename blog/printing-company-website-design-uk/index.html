Printing Company Website Design: Products, Quotes and Artwork

Website design for UK printers: product pages, online ordering or quotes, artwork upload and file guidance, proofs, turnaround times and delivery information.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 1 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Print buyers usually want to know quickly whether you produce what they need, how much it costs and how soon it can arrive.
  • Clear artwork guidance and an easy upload route can reduce delays and back-and-forth on every order.
  • Whether to sell standard products online or quote for each job depends on your work mix, and many printers combine both.

Printing companies range from local high street print shops to commercial litho and digital printers, large format specialists and businesses producing printed merchandise. Whatever the scale, customers increasingly expect to see products, prices and turnaround times online, and many want to upload artwork and order without a phone call. A well-planned website can handle routine orders smoothly while still bringing in larger, more complex jobs.

This guide covers website design for UK printers. It focuses on the website itself rather than on wider marketing. If you sell your own art prints or posters directly to consumers rather than offering a print service, our guide to Shopify for art print sellers is more relevant. For general online shop design, see our guide to ecommerce website design.

Decide how customers will buy

The biggest structural decision is how people will order. Printers typically use one or a mix of three models:

  • Online ordering for standard products such as business cards, flyers or banners, with set sizes, stocks and quantities.
  • Quote requests for bespoke work, such as brochures with unusual finishes, packaging or large runs.
  • Trade or account ordering for agencies, resellers or regular business customers.

Many printers combine these: an online shop for standard items and a quote route for everything else. Be realistic about which model fits your work. Building complex online pricing for products you mostly quote for individually can create more problems than it solves.

Organise products the way customers think

Customers often search for the thing they need, such as "leaflet printing" or "roller banners", rather than for a process like litho or digital. Structuring the site by product type tends to be easier to navigate. Categories might include:

  • Business stationery, such as cards, letterheads and compliments slips.
  • Marketing print, such as flyers, leaflets, brochures and booklets.
  • Large format, such as posters, banners and signs.
  • Labels, stickers and packaging.
  • Event and wedding stationery.
  • Printed clothing or promotional items, if you produce them.

Each product page can then explain available sizes, paper stocks, finishes and turnaround, with good photos of real printed samples. You can still include a page explaining your equipment and processes for buyers who care. See our guide to website navigation and our guide to category page design.

Product options and pricing

Print products often have many combinations: size, stock weight, finish, sides printed, quantity. Presenting these clearly is a real design challenge. Some principles help:

  • Let customers choose options step by step, with the price updating as they go.
  • Explain technical terms, such as paper weight in gsm or finishes like matt lamination, in plain language.
  • Show whether prices include VAT and delivery.
  • Make quantity price breaks visible so customers can see the value of ordering more.

Complex option matrices may need a specialist print ordering plugin or custom development rather than a standard product page. Test the ordering flow thoroughly on mobile. Our guide to product variants and bundles covers related decisions.

Artwork upload and file guidance

Artwork problems are a common cause of delays in print. Good website design can reduce them by making requirements clear before the customer uploads anything. Consider:

  • A clear artwork guidelines page covering bleed, resolution, colour mode and accepted file formats.
  • Downloadable templates for common products, if you provide them.
  • A straightforward upload step during or after ordering, with file size limits stated.
  • Explaining what happens if artwork has problems, and whether you offer checks or design help.

Write the guidance for non-designers as well as professionals. Many small business customers will not know what bleed means until you explain it. Make sure uploaded files are stored and handled securely, and explain this in your privacy notice.

Proofs and approvals

Explain your proofing process clearly. Customers want to know whether they will see a digital proof, whether physical proofs are available, how long they have to approve, and what happens to turnaround if approval is delayed. Describe your actual process and make the approval step easy to find. Clarity here reduces disputes later, particularly about colour or errors in supplied artwork.

Turnaround and delivery

Speed is a deciding factor for many print buyers, often because they have an event or launch date. Show realistic turnaround times on each product page, explain when the clock starts, for example after artwork approval, and state any cut-off times for same-day or next-day production. Delivery options, collection from your premises and costs should be easy to find before checkout. Our guide to delivery and returns pages covers how to present this information. Because printed items are usually personalised, explain your policy on errors and reprints clearly; consumer rights for personalised goods differ from standard goods, so check current guidance or take advice.

Quote forms for bespoke work

For jobs that do not fit standard products, a quote form should collect the information you need to price accurately:

  • The type of item and its purpose.
  • Size, page count and quantity.
  • Paper or material preferences, and finishes.
  • Deadline.
  • Whether artwork is ready or design help is needed.
  • An optional file upload for reference material.

Say how quickly you will reply. Our guide to website contact forms covers form design.

Trade customers and repeat orders

Many printers rely on repeat business from agencies, resellers and local companies. Features that can help include customer accounts for reordering previous jobs, trade pricing for approved customers, and the ability to store artwork for repeat runs. Only build what your customers will actually use. Our guide to customer accounts covers the options.

Showing your work

Print is tactile, which makes it hard to sell online. Good photography of real finished products, close-ups of finishes and stocks, and short videos of items being handled can help customers understand quality. A gallery of genuine jobs, shown with client permission, can demonstrate range. If you offer a sample pack, make it easy to request. Our guide to product photography covers how to photograph items well.

Local visibility

Many printers still serve a local area for collection, urgent jobs and face-to-face customers. Make your address, opening hours and phone number clear, and keep your Google Business Profile up to date. Our guide to local SEO covers more.

Design services and help for beginners

Not every customer arrives with print-ready artwork. Small businesses, clubs and individuals may have only a rough idea, a logo or some text. If you offer design help, give it a clear page explaining what is available, how the process works in practice and how it is priced. Our guide to pricing page design covers presenting options like this clearly. Make it easy to add design help during ordering, rather than forcing customers to start again with a separate enquiry.

A short FAQ can also help beginners with common questions, such as the difference between paper stocks, which finish suits a particular use, or how many leaflets a typical door-to-door drop might need. Answering these on the site can reduce phone calls and help customers order with confidence. Keep answers general and practical, and avoid promising outcomes that depend on the customer's artwork or use. Our guides to FAQ pages and website copywriting cover how to write this kind of content.

Choosing a platform

Standard print products can be sold through Shopify or WooCommerce, often with apps or plugins for product options and file uploads. More complex print pricing may need specialist web-to-print software or custom development. Consider how orders will connect to your production workflow. See our guides to Shopify vs WooCommerce and website builders vs custom websites.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Technical jargon with no explanation for non-designers.
  • Hidden or vague turnaround times.
  • No artwork guidance, leading to repeated file problems.
  • Prices shown without making VAT and delivery clear.
  • Stock photos that do not show your real print quality.

FAQs

Should a printer sell online or ask for quotes?

Many printers do both: online ordering for standard products and quote forms for bespoke work.

How can a printing website reduce artwork problems?

Clear artwork guidelines, templates and a simple upload step, written for non-designers, can reduce errors.

Can Shopify handle print products?

It can handle standard products, often with apps for options and file uploads. Very complex pricing may need specialist tools.

Should turnaround times be shown on product pages?

Yes. Show realistic times and explain when production starts, such as after proof approval.

Do printers need customer accounts on their website?

They can help trade and repeat customers reorder, but only add them if your customers will use them.
