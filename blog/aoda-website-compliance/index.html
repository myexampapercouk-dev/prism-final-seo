AODA Website Compliance: What Ontario Businesses Need to Know About Accessible Websites

AODA website compliance explained for Ontario businesses: who it may apply to, how WCAG fits in, what to fix first and how to plan an accessible website.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 9 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • AODA website requirements apply to certain Ontario organisations depending on their size and type, and are based on the international WCAG accessibility guidelines.
  • Most accessibility problems come from a handful of repeat issues: missing alt text, poor colour contrast, unlabelled forms, keyboard traps and inaccessible documents.
  • Accessibility works best when built into design and content from the start, then checked regularly, rather than patched once with an overlay tool.

AODA website compliance means making your website accessible to people with disabilities in line with the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act, which for websites is based on the international Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG). Whether the rules legally apply to you depends on your organisation's size and type, so this guide explains the practical side of building and maintaining an accessible website, and you should check current official guidance from the Government of Ontario for your own obligations.

Prism Works Studio is a UK-based studio that designs and builds websites for Canadian businesses remotely, with no office in Canada. We are not lawyers and this is general information, not legal advice. What we can do is explain what accessible websites involve and build sites to recognised accessibility guidelines.

What is AODA website compliance?

The AODA is Ontario's accessibility law. It includes standards covering several areas of daily life, and one of these covers information and communications, which includes websites and web content. For organisations it applies to, the website requirements point to WCAG, a set of internationally recognised guidelines published by the World Wide Web Consortium.

At the time of writing, official Ontario guidance indicates that the website requirements apply to the Ontario government, designated public sector organisations and private or non-profit organisations above a certain number of employees in Ontario, with the standard set at WCAG 2.0 Level AA, subject to some exceptions. Requirements, thresholds and deadlines can change, and there are also reporting duties for some organisations, so please check the current guidance on the Government of Ontario website and take your own advice.

Federally regulated organisations may also have obligations under the Accessible Canada Act, and other provinces have introduced or are developing their own accessibility laws. If you operate across Canada, check what applies in each place you do business.

Why accessibility matters even if the rules do not apply to you

Even small businesses outside the legal thresholds have good reasons to build accessible websites:

  • More customers. People with visual, hearing, motor or cognitive disabilities, and older users, can actually use your site.
  • Better usability for everyone. Clear headings, readable text and simple forms help every visitor, including people on phones in bright sunlight.
  • Search benefits. Descriptive headings, alt text and clean structure also help search engines understand your pages.
  • Lower future cost. Building accessibly from the start is far cheaper than fixing a finished site later.
  • Selling to larger organisations. Public sector and enterprise buyers increasingly expect suppliers to take accessibility seriously.

The WCAG principles in plain language

WCAG is organised around four principles, often shortened to POUR. Every specific requirement sits under one of these.

PrincipleWhat it meansEveryday examples
PerceivablePeople can see or hear the content in some formAlt text for images, captions for video, enough colour contrast
OperablePeople can use the site with different input methodsFull keyboard navigation, visible focus, no content that flashes
UnderstandableContent and interactions are clear and predictablePlain language, consistent navigation, helpful form error messages
RobustContent works with assistive technologies such as screen readersValid code, proper labels, correct use of headings and landmarks

Level A covers the most basic requirements, Level AA adds a broader set that most laws and policies refer to, and Level AAA is the most demanding and rarely required for whole sites.

The most common accessibility problems on business websites

Most sites we review fail on the same small group of issues. Fixing these usually makes the biggest difference first.

  1. Missing or poor alt text. Images that carry meaning need a short, accurate description; decorative images should be hidden from screen readers. Our image alt text guide explains how to write it.
  2. Low colour contrast. Light grey text on white, or text over busy images, is hard to read. Our guide to website colour and typography covers contrast and readable type.
  3. Unlabelled forms and vague errors. Every field needs a visible label, and error messages should say what went wrong and how to fix it. See our guide to website form error messages.
  4. Keyboard problems. Menus, pop-ups and sliders that cannot be reached or closed with a keyboard block many users.
  5. Interactive components built incorrectly. Accordions, tabs and carousels need the right structure so screen readers announce them properly. Our guide to website accordions and tabs covers accessible patterns.
  6. Inaccessible documents. Scanned PDFs and untagged documents are often unreadable by screen readers.
  7. Video without captions. Pre-recorded video with speech generally needs accurate captions.
  8. Data tables that break on mobile. Tables need proper headers and a layout that works on small screens, as covered in our guide to website tables on mobile.

How to approach AODA website compliance step by step

Accessibility is easier to manage as an ongoing process than a one-off project. A practical approach looks like this:

  1. Confirm your obligations. Check current official guidance on whether and how the AODA applies to your organisation, including any reporting duties.
  2. Audit the current site. Combine automated testing tools with manual checks using a keyboard and a screen reader. Automated tools alone typically miss many issues.
  3. Prioritise. Fix problems on the most important pages and journeys first, such as the homepage, contact forms, booking and checkout.
  4. Fix templates, not just pages. Correcting a shared header, footer or product template fixes the problem everywhere it appears.
  5. Train content editors. Many issues return when new content is added without alt text, headings or captions.
  6. Re-test regularly. Check again after redesigns, new features and major content changes.

Accessible content: what your own team can do

A well-built site can still become inaccessible as new pages, posts and products are added. Simple habits from whoever edits the website make a large difference:

  • Use real headings in order rather than bold text styled to look like a heading.
  • Write link text that makes sense on its own, such as "view our delivery options" rather than "click here".
  • Add alt text to every meaningful image as it is uploaded, not afterwards.
  • Keep sentences and paragraphs short, and explain jargon or abbreviations.
  • Publish important information as a web page rather than only as a PDF.
  • Caption videos before they go live, and check automatic captions for errors.

A short written checklist for editors, reviewed every few months, usually prevents most new problems from creeping back in.

Do accessibility overlays make a website compliant?

Accessibility overlays are scripts that add a toolbar or claim to fix problems automatically. They can change text size or contrast, but they generally cannot fix underlying issues such as poor structure, missing labels or keyboard traps, and some disabled users report that overlays interfere with the assistive technology they already use. We recommend fixing the website itself rather than relying on an overlay.

FAQs

What is AODA website compliance?

It means meeting the website accessibility requirements of Ontario's accessibility law, which refer to the WCAG guidelines. Whether they apply depends on your organisation, so check current Government of Ontario guidance.

Does AODA apply to small businesses?

The website requirements apply to certain organisations based on size and type, and small businesses may fall outside them. Check the current thresholds in official guidance. Building accessibly is still good practice for any business.

Which WCAG level does AODA require?

Official guidance at the time of writing refers to WCAG 2.0 Level AA for organisations the website rules cover, with some exceptions. Confirm the current requirement before planning your work.

Can an automated tool check AODA compliance?

Automated tools are a useful start but miss many issues, such as poor alt text, confusing focus order and screen reader problems. Manual testing is needed alongside them.

Will an accessibility overlay make my site compliant?

Overlays generally cannot fix underlying problems in the code and content. Fixing the website itself is the more reliable route to an accessible site.

Can a UK studio build an accessible website for an Ontario business?

Yes. Prism Works Studio builds websites to WCAG guidelines for Canadian businesses remotely, with no office in Canada. Legal judgement on your AODA obligations rests with you and your advisers.
