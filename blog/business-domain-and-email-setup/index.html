Business Domain and Email Setup: Getting the Foundations Right

How UK businesses can choose a domain name, keep control of it, set up professional email and configure SPF, DKIM and DMARC so emails get delivered.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Register your domain in the business's own account and keep access details safe; losing control of a domain is painful to fix.
  • Use email addresses on your own domain rather than free webmail addresses for a more professional, portable setup.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC records so your emails are less likely to be marked as spam or spoofed.

Your domain name and business email are the quiet foundations under your website. When they are set up properly nobody notices. When they are not, websites go offline, emails land in spam, and businesses discover that a former supplier or employee holds the keys. A little care at the start avoids most of these problems.

Choosing a domain name

  • Keep it short and easy to spell. Imagine saying it over the phone.
  • Avoid hyphens and numbers unless they are part of your brand.
  • Pick the right ending. UK-focused businesses often choose .co.uk or .uk; .com can suit businesses with international ambitions. Some register both and redirect one to the other.
  • Check for conflicts. Make sure the name does not clash with an existing trade mark or a well-known business. This is general guidance, not legal advice; take advice if you are unsure.

Consider registering common misspellings or alternative endings if they are inexpensive, and redirecting them to your main domain.

Keep control of your domain

This is the most important point in this guide. Your domain should be registered in an account the business controls, with the business named as the registrant, using a shared business email address rather than one person's personal inbox.

  • Enable two-factor authentication on the registrar account.
  • Turn on auto-renewal and keep payment details current. Expired domains can be lost.
  • Record where the domain is registered and who has access, somewhere safe.
  • If a web designer or agency registers it for you, ask that it is in your name and that you are given access.

Domains, DNS and hosting: what is what

PieceWhat it does
RegistrarWhere you rent the domain name and renew it
DNSThe settings that point the domain to your website and email
Web hostingWhere your website files and software live
Email hostingWhere your mailboxes live and messages are stored

These can all be with one provider or with different ones. Keeping them separate can make it easier to change one without disturbing the others, but either approach can work. Our WordPress hosting guide covers choosing web hosting.

Professional email on your own domain

An address such as hello@yourbusiness.co.uk looks more established than a free webmail address and stays with the business if staff change. Most businesses use a dedicated email hosting service rather than the basic email bundled with web hosting, because it tends to offer better reliability, spam filtering and shared calendars.

Think about structure as well. Role-based addresses such as sales@ or accounts@ can be shared or forwarded, which helps when people are away or leave. Avoid having critical services, such as your domain registrar or payment accounts, tied to a single employee's mailbox.

Email authentication: SPF, DKIM and DMARC

These three DNS records help receiving mail servers decide whether an email genuinely came from you. Without them, your messages are more likely to be flagged as spam, and it is easier for someone to impersonate your domain.

  • SPF lists the services allowed to send email for your domain, such as your email provider and any newsletter tool.
  • DKIM adds a digital signature to outgoing emails so recipients can check they have not been altered.
  • DMARC tells receivers what to do with messages that fail those checks and can send you reports.

Large mailbox providers have tightened their requirements for senders, particularly those sending marketing email in volume, so these records matter more than they used to. A common approach is to start DMARC in a monitoring mode, review the reports, and tighten the policy once you are confident all legitimate senders are covered.

Don't forget website-generated email

Contact forms, order confirmations and password resets are sent by your website or shop platform. Make sure they are sent through an authenticated service included in your SPF and DKIM setup, or they may quietly go to spam. Test them after any change. Our contact forms guide has more on this.

Changing providers safely

Moving a domain, website or email between providers is routine but needs planning. Note your existing DNS records before changing anything, lower record lifetimes in advance if possible, and switch at a quiet time. For email moves, plan how existing messages will be migrated. Check website, email sending and email receiving straight afterwards.

A simple setup checklist

For a new business or a tidy-up of an existing one, the following steps cover most of the essentials:

  • Register the domain in an account the business owns, with two-factor authentication and auto-renewal switched on.
  • Decide whether the website should load with or without "www" and redirect the other version to it.
  • Make sure the site loads over HTTPS with a valid certificate, and that the plain HTTP version redirects.
  • Set up email hosting and create the mailboxes and shared addresses you need.
  • Add the MX records your email provider gives you so incoming mail arrives in the right place.
  • Add SPF, DKIM and DMARC records covering every service that sends email for you.
  • Send test emails to a few different mailbox providers and check they arrive in the inbox.
  • Write down where everything is held and who has access, and store it securely.

Security basics

Domains and email accounts are attractive targets because they unlock so much else. If someone gains access to your registrar account, they can redirect your website and email. If they gain access to a mailbox, they can reset passwords on other services or send convincing invoices to your customers. Sensible precautions include:

  • Two-factor authentication on registrar, DNS and email accounts.
  • Registrar lock, sometimes called transfer lock, to make unauthorised transfers harder.
  • Removing access promptly when staff or suppliers leave.
  • Training staff to treat unexpected payment-change requests with suspicion and verify them by phone.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre publishes free guidance for small businesses that is worth reading alongside this.

Subdomains and extra sending services

As a business grows, it often adds tools that send email on its behalf: a newsletter platform, a help desk, an invoicing tool or an online shop. Each needs to be included in your authentication setup, and SPF in particular has limits on how many services it can reference. Some businesses send marketing email from a subdomain, such as news.yourbusiness.co.uk, to keep its reputation separate from everyday business email. That can be helpful, but it adds complexity, so it is worth planning rather than adding piecemeal.

When a supplier holds your domain

It is surprisingly common for a business to find its domain registered in a former web designer's account. If this applies to you, ask the supplier politely to transfer the domain into an account you own; most registrars have a standard process using an authorisation code. Keep the request in writing. If the domain is held in your name, you will usually be in a stronger position. If it is not, or the supplier is unresponsive, you may need to take advice.

FAQs

Should I choose .co.uk or .com?

If you mainly serve UK customers, .co.uk is familiar and signals a UK business. A .com may suit international plans. Many businesses register both and redirect one to the other.

Who should own my domain name?

Your business. It should be registered in the business's name and in an account you control, even if someone else manages it for you.

Can I use my web hosting for email?

You can, but dedicated email hosting often offers better deliverability, security and features. It also means a website problem is less likely to affect your email.

Why are my emails going to spam?

Missing or incorrect SPF, DKIM or DMARC records are a common cause. Content, sending volume and your domain's reputation also play a part.

What happens if my domain expires?

Your website and email will usually stop working, and after a grace period the domain may become available for others to register. Auto-renewal and current payment details help prevent this.

Do I need a separate email address for each employee?

Usually each person benefits from their own mailbox for accountability and security, with shared role-based addresses such as sales@ or support@ for functions several people handle. Shared passwords on a single mailbox make it hard to control access when people leave.

How long does it take for DNS changes to work?

Often minutes, but sometimes several hours, depending on the record lifetimes previously set. Lowering those values a day or so before a planned change can make the switchover quicker and easier to reverse.
