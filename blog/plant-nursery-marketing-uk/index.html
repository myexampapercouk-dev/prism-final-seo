Plant Nursery Marketing in the UK: Selling Plants Online with SEO, Google Shopping and a Better Website

Plant nursery marketing in the UK: online shops, plant-by-plant SEO, Google Shopping and seasonal campaigns that help specialist growers sell plants direct.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • Plant nursery marketing depends on detailed plant pages, accurate stock and clear delivery rules, because buyers search for specific varieties and want to know a plant will arrive healthy.
  • Google Shopping and plant-by-plant SEO bring in most online demand, while seasonal email and social content keep collectors and gardeners coming back.
  • E-commerce sites cost £1,500 to £3,500 and retainers start at £350 a month, with ad spend separate and plant health compliance resting with the nursery.

Plant nursery marketing in the UK means helping specialist growers and retail nurseries sell more plants, both online and at the gate, by making it easy for gardeners to find specific varieties, trust the quality and buy with confidence. For most nurseries the biggest gains come from a well-built online shop, plant-by-plant search visibility, Google Shopping campaigns and seasonal communication with existing customers.

Prism Works Studio is a UK-based web design and digital marketing studio that works remotely with retailers and growers. This guide focuses on specialist and independent nurseries, such as growers of perennials, shrubs, fruit trees, roses, alpines, ferns, carnivorous plants, houseplants and hedging. If you run a larger garden centre with a café, gifts and furniture, our guide to garden centre website design covers that wider model.

How plant nurseries differ from other retailers

Plants are living products, and that shapes almost every marketing decision.

  • Stock changes constantly. Varieties sell out, come into flower, go dormant or are only available at certain times of year.
  • Buyers search very specifically. A gardener looking for a particular salvia, clematis or apple rootstock types the variety name, not "plants".
  • Delivery is difficult. Plants need careful packing, fast delivery and clear guidance on pot sizes, bare-root seasons and planting.
  • Trust is everything. Customers want reassurance that plants will arrive healthy and true to name.

Good marketing for a nursery starts with these realities rather than copying a generic online shop template. It also means being honest about what can and cannot be sent by post. Some nurseries choose to sell larger specimens for collection only, offer local delivery by their own van within a set radius, or run click and collect for open days. Each of these options needs its own clear explanation on the website, so customers understand exactly how they will receive their plants before they pay, and so your team spends less time answering the same questions by phone and email.

Building an online plant shop

A plant shop needs more information per product than most online stores. Each plant page should include the botanical and common name, pot size or root type, height and spread, flowering time, position and soil preferences, hardiness, care notes and honest photos. Ideally it also shows a photo of the plant as it will be delivered, not only in full flower.

Shopify and WooCommerce can both handle this well when the store is set up thoughtfully. We use product fields and filters so shoppers can browse by sun or shade, height, flowering season, colour, wildlife value or pot size. Our e-commerce sector page explains how we build online shops, and our guide to e-commerce product photography covers how to photograph products consistently.

Delivery needs particular care. Many nurseries limit deliveries to certain days so plants do not sit in a depot over a weekend, charge by size or weight, and pause certain items in very hot or cold weather. Our guides to online shop shipping rates and Shopify shipping profiles explain how to set these rules up so customers see accurate costs before they reach the checkout.

SEO for varieties, genera and growing guides

Nurseries have a natural SEO advantage: they sell many specific products that people search for by name. The key is to make each plant page useful and unique rather than copying supplier descriptions.

We usually work on three levels:

  1. Variety pages. Each plant has its own page with original descriptions and care information written from your growing knowledge.
  2. Genus and group pages. Category pages for groups such as hardy geraniums, hostas or fruit trees, with an introduction and buying guidance.
  3. Guides. Content that answers common questions, such as when to plant bare-root hedging, how to choose a rootstock or which plants suit clay soil.

Seasonal products, such as bare-root plants, should keep their pages live all year with a clear availability message and a back-in-stock option, so the page keeps its search visibility between seasons.

Google Shopping for plant nurseries

Google Shopping can be very effective for nurseries because shoppers searching for a specific variety are close to buying. The quality of the product feed decides how well it works. Titles should include the variety name, plant type and pot size, images should be clear, and prices and availability must match the website exactly.

Our Google Shopping ads guide explains how campaigns are structured, and our Google Merchant Center setup guide covers the feed and account setup. For nurseries we often separate high-value or seasonal lines into their own campaigns and pause out-of-stock items automatically. Some product categories, such as live animals for ponds or certain chemicals, may be restricted by Google's policies, so we check the current rules before adding them. Collectors and keen gardeners often buy repeatedly. A simple email programme that announces new varieties, restocks and seasonal guides, sent with consent in line with PECR, can bring in a steady share of sales at low cost.

Gate sales, open days and local customers

Many nurseries also sell from a sales area, attend plant fairs or hold open days. Local SEO and a complete Google Business Profile help nearby gardeners find you, and a calendar of open days and plant fairs on your website gives local customers a reason to visit. Nurseries that supply landscapers and garden designers may also want a trade page; our guide to landscaper website design shows how those businesses think about suppliers.

Plant health, labelling and data protection

Selling plants is subject to UK plant health rules, which in some cases include plant passport requirements and restrictions on moving certain plants, particularly between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and other countries. Product descriptions should be accurate about names, sizes and hardiness, and consumer protection rules apply to online sales, including information about delivery and cancellations.

We are not legal or plant health advisers. Check the official UK government guidance on plant health and online selling, and ask your own adviser where needed. Compliance decisions rest with the nursery. UK GDPR and PECR also apply to customer data, cookies and marketing emails.

FAQs

What does plant nursery marketing in the UK involve?

It usually combines an online plant shop, variety-level SEO, Google Shopping, seasonal email and social campaigns, and local SEO for nurseries with a sales area.

Is Google Shopping worth it for a small nursery?

Often yes, because shoppers searching for a named variety are close to buying. It depends on an accurate feed, competitive delivery costs and reliable stock levels.

Should we use Shopify or WooCommerce for a plant shop?

Both can work well. Shopify is simpler to run, while WooCommerce offers more flexibility. We recommend the platform that fits your catalogue, delivery rules and team.

How do we keep plant pages ranking when stock sells out?

Keep the pages live with a clear availability message, expected return date and back-in-stock option rather than deleting them.

Can you write our plant descriptions?

We can structure and edit plant pages, but the growing knowledge should come from your team so descriptions are accurate and unique.

Who is responsible for plant health rules?

The nursery is. We can display the information you need on your website, but you should follow official UK plant health guidance and your own adviser.
