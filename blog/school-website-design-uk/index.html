School Website Design: Clear for Parents, Easy for Staff

School website design for UK schools: navigation for parents, statutory information pages, accessibility, safeguarding-aware content and easy staff updates.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • A school website mostly serves current parents day to day, so term dates, letters, menus and contact details should be quick to find on a phone.
  • Many schools are required to publish certain information online; check the current Department for Education guidance for your type of school rather than relying on a generic checklist.
  • Choose a structure and platform that office staff can update confidently, and think about safeguarding before publishing any pupil images or names.

A school website has a surprising number of jobs. It is the first impression for families choosing a school, the daily reference point for current parents, a place where certain required information is published, a recruitment tool for staff and, often, something inspectors and governors look at too. It is usually kept up to date by a busy school office rather than a dedicated web team. Good design takes all of that into account.

This guide focuses on the website itself: structure, content, compliance-related pages, accessibility and maintenance. For admissions campaigns, open events, social media and wider promotion, see our guide to school marketing in the UK.

Who uses a school website

Before deciding on pages, it helps to be honest about who actually visits and why:

  • Current parents and carers, looking for term dates, letters, the lunch menu, uniform details, club information or who to contact about an absence.
  • Prospective families, trying to get a feel for the school, its ethos, results where published, and how admissions work.
  • Pupils, particularly in secondary schools, looking for homework platforms, revision resources or timetables.
  • Job applicants, checking vacancies and what the school is like to work for.
  • Governors, local authority staff and inspectors, checking policies and required information.

Current parents are often the largest and most frequent group, yet many school sites are designed mainly as a brochure for prospective families. A good design does both: an attractive, welcoming home page, plus fast routes to the practical things parents need every week.

A structure that suits many schools

  • About us: welcome message, vision and values, staff and governors, history, contact details.
  • Admissions: how to apply, key dates, open events, admissions policy and links to the local authority where relevant.
  • Parents: term dates, letters and newsletters, uniform, meals, attendance, clubs, and how to report an absence.
  • Curriculum: what is taught, how, and subject-by-subject information if appropriate.
  • Key information: policies, performance information, pupil premium and sports premium reports, SEND information and other published documents.
  • News and events, if someone will keep it current.
  • Vacancies.
  • Contact, with address, phone, email and a map.

Keep the top-level navigation short and use labels parents would use themselves. "Parents" and "Term dates" tend to be clearer than internal terms. A small set of quick links on the home page, such as term dates, letters, lunch menu and report an absence, can save a lot of clicks and phone calls.

Statutory and required information

In England, maintained schools and academies are generally required to publish specific information on their websites, and the Department for Education publishes guidance listing what is expected. For academies, further requirements can come from the funding agreement and the academy trust handbook. Independent schools have their own requirements, and schools in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland follow different rules set by their own governments and inspectorates.

Requirements change from time to time, so the safest approach is to check the current official guidance for your type of school, and take advice from your local authority, trust or governing body where needed, rather than relying on a checklist copied from another school's site. From a design point of view, the useful things are:

  • A clearly labelled area for required information, so it can be found by anyone checking.
  • Documents named descriptively and dated, for example "Behaviour policy, reviewed autumn 2026" rather than "Policy v3 final".
  • A simple way for staff to replace an outdated document without leaving the old version live elsewhere on the site.
  • A review schedule so someone checks the section each term.

Avoid stating on the site that it is "fully compliant" unless that has been properly checked. It is better to keep the information accurate and current.

Documents, PDFs and letters

School websites tend to accumulate PDFs: letters home, policies, menus, newsletters. PDFs are often harder to read on a phone and can be inaccessible to screen reader users if not created carefully. Where practical, publish short, frequently used information such as term dates, uniform details and the lunch menu as normal web pages, and keep PDFs for documents that genuinely need to be downloaded or printed.

When PDFs are needed, create them from a properly structured source document with headings, give them meaningful titles, and archive old letters rather than letting them pile up in one long list. A letters page organised by term or year group is much easier to use than a single page of hundreds of links.

Accessibility

Many schools in the UK fall within the scope of the public sector accessibility regulations, which generally expect websites to meet recognised accessibility standards and to publish an accessibility statement. Whether and how these apply depends on the type of school, so check the current government guidance. Either way, accessibility is good practice: parents and carers include people with disabilities, people using older phones and people for whom English is an additional language.

Practical steps include readable text sizes and good contrast, meaningful headings, descriptive link text, alt text for images, captions on videos where possible, forms that work with a keyboard and screen reader, and plain language. Our guide to website accessibility covers the principles in more detail.

Safeguarding and pupil images

Photos of pupils bring a school website to life, but they need care. Follow your school's safeguarding and data protection policies, collect and record the appropriate consent before publishing images, and think about whether names should ever appear alongside photos. Many schools choose not to name pupils in images at all. Avoid publishing details that could identify where an individual child can be found at particular times.

Staff information also deserves thought. Listing roles and a contact route is useful, but staff should agree to how their names and photos are used. Data protection obligations under UK GDPR apply to the site's forms, newsletters sign-ups and any personal information it displays. Your data protection officer is the right person to sign off on these decisions.

Making the home page work

The home page has to welcome prospective families and serve current parents at once. A layout that often works is:

  • A short, genuine welcome with a real photograph of the school, used with consent.
  • Quick links to the most-used parent pages.
  • Upcoming dates and the latest news, pulled automatically so they do not go stale.
  • A clear route into admissions for prospective families.
  • Contact details and the school's address in the footer.

Keep large rotating banners to a minimum. They slow pages down and push useful content further down the screen on a phone. One strong image and a clear heading usually do the job better.

Admissions pages that answer real questions

Prospective families want to understand what the school is like and how to apply. Explain the application route clearly, including whether applications go through the local authority, link to the admissions policy and oversubscription criteria if relevant, list open event dates and how to book, and give an honest picture of daily life. Short pages on the school day, wraparound care, clubs and SEND support often answer questions that would otherwise become phone calls.

If you publish results or performance information, present it accurately and in line with official guidance, with dates and context. Do not cherry-pick figures in a way that could mislead.

Choosing a platform staff can maintain

The best school website is one the office can keep current. When choosing a platform, think about who will update it, how easy it is to add a letter, change a date or upload a newsletter, whether several staff can have accounts with different permissions, and how backups and security updates are handled. WordPress is widely used and flexible, while hosted builders such as Squarespace and Wix can be simpler for small schools. Whatever you choose, provide short written instructions for common tasks so the site does not depend on one person.

Security matters too: use strong, unique passwords, give each user their own account, remove accounts when staff leave, and keep software up to date.

Speed and mobile use

Parents often check the school website on a phone at the school gate or on the bus. Pages should load quickly and be easy to use with one thumb. Compress images before uploading, avoid heavy sliders and auto-playing video, and test the most-used pages on a real phone. Our guide to website image optimisation explains how to keep photos sharp without slowing the site down.

Common mistakes on school websites

  • Term dates buried several clicks deep or out of date.
  • Policies with no review date, or several versions of the same policy online.
  • Huge lists of undated PDF letters.
  • News pages last updated many months ago.
  • Pupil photos published without clear consent records.
  • Navigation labels that make sense to staff but not to parents.

FAQs

What must a school publish on its website?

It depends on the type of school and where it is in the UK. In England, the Department for Education publishes guidance for maintained schools and academies. Check the current version and take advice from your trust or local authority.

Do school websites need an accessibility statement?

Many schools fall within the public sector accessibility regulations, which require one. Check current government guidance for your school type.

Can we show pupils' photos on the website?

Usually, with appropriate consent and in line with your safeguarding and data protection policies. Many schools avoid naming pupils in photos.

Should letters be PDFs or web pages?

Short, frequently used information works better as web pages. Keep PDFs for documents that need downloading or printing, and make them accessible.

Which platform is best for a school website?

One your staff can update confidently and that is kept secure. WordPress, Squarespace and Wix are all used by schools.
