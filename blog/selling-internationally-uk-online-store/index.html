Selling Internationally From a UK Online Store: A Practical Guide

How to start selling abroad from a UK online store: choosing markets, currencies, shipping, duties and VAT, returns, translation and international SEO.

By the Prism Works Studio team · 31 October 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start with one or two markets where you already see interest, rather than switching on every country at once.
  • Be upfront about total costs, including delivery, duties and taxes, so overseas customers are not surprised at the door.
  • Tax and customs rules are complex and change, so take professional advice before you sell cross-border.

A UK online store can, in theory, sell to customers anywhere. In practice, selling internationally involves more than ticking a box to allow overseas addresses. Customers abroad want to see prices in their own currency, know how long delivery will take and understand exactly what they will pay, including any duties or taxes. Behind the scenes you need to deal with shipping, customs, returns and tax registration.

This guide walks through the main decisions involved in selling internationally from a UK store, so you can expand in a measured way rather than discovering problems one parcel at a time.

Is your business ready to sell abroad?

Before thinking about markets, check the basics are working at home. A store with unclear product pages, slow fulfilment or a high return rate will find those problems magnified across borders. Ask yourself:

  • Is the store converting well for UK customers?
  • Can your fulfilment handle extra volume and more complex shipping?
  • Do your margins survive higher delivery costs and possible duties?
  • Are your products allowed to be sold and shipped to the countries you have in mind?

Some products, such as certain foods, cosmetics, batteries or items with restricted ingredients, face extra rules overseas. Check this early, because it can rule out a market entirely.

Choosing your first markets

It is usually better to start with a small number of markets and do them well. Good signals that a market is worth trying include:

  • Existing demand: visits, enquiries or orders from that country already showing in your analytics.
  • Language: English-speaking markets avoid translation at first.
  • Shipping practicality: reasonable delivery times and costs from the UK.
  • Regulatory simplicity: fewer barriers for your product type.

Google Analytics and Google Search Console can show where visitors and search impressions are coming from, which is a sensible starting point for deciding where to focus.

Currency and pricing

Shoppers are more comfortable buying in their own currency. Most major platforms, including Shopify and WooCommerce with suitable extensions, can display and charge in multiple currencies. Decide whether you will:

  • Convert automatically using exchange rates, which is simple but produces awkward prices that move over time, or
  • Set prices per market, which takes more effort but gives you clean, rounded prices and lets you account for local costs.

Also think about whether displayed prices include local taxes. Customers in many countries expect tax-inclusive prices, and showing one figure on the product page then adding more at checkout is a common reason for abandoned baskets.

Shipping and delivery

Delivery is often where international selling becomes complicated. Key points to plan:

  • Carriers and services: compare tracked and untracked options, delivery times and costs for each market.
  • Delivery pricing: flat rates, weight-based rates or free delivery over a threshold, set per region.
  • Packaging: lighter, smaller packaging can significantly reduce cross-border costs.
  • Delivery promises: give realistic estimates and allow for customs delays.

Update your delivery and returns pages with clear information for each region you sell to, so customers can check before they buy.

Customs, duties and taxes

This is the area where expert advice matters most. Since the UK left the EU, goods sent from the UK to EU customers are exports, and parcels to many other countries have long been subject to customs processes. Depending on the destination and the value of the order, there may be import duties, local VAT or sales tax and customs paperwork.

A crucial decision is who pays these charges and when:

  • Delivered duty unpaid (DDU or DAP): the customer pays any charges on delivery. Simpler for you, but customers can be surprised by unexpected fees, leading to refused parcels and poor reviews.
  • Delivered duty paid (DDP): you collect charges at checkout and pay them, so the customer pays nothing more at the door. A better experience, but more administration for you.

Some destinations have schemes that let overseas sellers register and collect tax at the point of sale, and the rules vary by country and order value. Requirements also change, so speak to an accountant or customs specialist with cross-border experience before you launch. This guide is general information rather than tax advice.

Whatever approach you take, be transparent. Tell customers clearly on product pages, delivery information and at checkout whether duties and taxes are included.

Returns from abroad

International returns are expensive and slow. Decide in advance:

  • Whether you will offer free returns, paid returns or a local returns address.
  • How long customers have to return items, taking into account any consumer rights in their country.
  • Whether low-value items are worth having returned at all, or whether a refund without return makes more sense.

Consumer rights differ between countries, so check the requirements for each market you sell to.

Language and localisation

If you sell to non-English-speaking markets, translation can make a real difference to trust and conversion. Machine translation is improving, but product descriptions, checkout wording and policies benefit from review by a fluent speaker. Poor translations can make a store look untrustworthy.

Localisation goes beyond language. Consider:

  • sizing conventions and units of measurement,
  • date and address formats,
  • local payment methods customers expect, and
  • seasonal differences, especially for clothing and gifts.

International SEO

If you want customers abroad to find you through search, your site structure matters. Common approaches include subfolders for each market or language, such as a section for Ireland or for French-speaking customers, or separate domains for each country. Subfolders are often simpler to manage because they share the authority of your main domain.

Whichever structure you choose, use hreflang annotations so Google understands which version of a page is intended for which audience, and avoid automatically redirecting visitors based on location in a way that stops them, or search engines, reaching other versions. Payments and fraud

Offering familiar payment methods matters overseas. Card payments and major digital wallets are widely accepted, but some countries have strong preferences for local methods. Check which options your payment provider supports per market.

International orders can also carry a higher risk of fraud. Use your platform's fraud tools, watch for mismatched billing and shipping details and set clear rules for reviewing high-value orders.

Marketing to new markets

Once the store is ready, you need to reach customers. Options include targeting new countries with Google Shopping ads, localised email campaigns and content written for each market. Start with modest budgets, measure carefully and expand where results are good.

A sensible rollout plan

  1. Review analytics to identify likely first markets.
  2. Check product restrictions and take tax and customs advice.
  3. Decide on currency, pricing and whether to collect duties at checkout.
  4. Set up shipping rates, carriers and returns.
  5. Update product pages, policies and delivery information.
  6. Configure international SEO if you want organic traffic.
  7. Launch in one or two markets, measure, learn and then expand.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Switching on worldwide shipping without checking costs or restrictions.
  • Surprising customers with duties and taxes on delivery.
  • Ignoring tax registration obligations in destination countries.
  • Relying on unreviewed machine translation for key pages.
  • Forcing location-based redirects that frustrate visitors.

FAQs

Can a Shopify store sell in multiple currencies?

Yes. Shopify supports selling in multiple currencies and setting up different markets, though available features depend on your plan and payment setup.

Do I need to charge VAT to overseas customers?

It depends on the destination, the type of goods and the order value. Rules differ by country and change over time, so take advice from an accountant with cross-border experience.

Should I collect duties at checkout?

Collecting duties upfront usually gives a better customer experience because there are no surprise charges on delivery, but it involves more administration. Weigh the trade-off for each market.

Do I need a separate website for each country?

Not necessarily. Many stores use subfolders or market settings within one site. Separate domains are an option but take more work to maintain.

Which country should I sell to first?

Start where you already see interest in your analytics and where shipping and regulations are manageable for your products.
